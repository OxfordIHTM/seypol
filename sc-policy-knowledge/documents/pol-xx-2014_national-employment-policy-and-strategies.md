# Foreword

## Creating Decent Work for all

It is an honour for me to present the new National Employment Policy for the Republic of Seychelles, which reflects the Government’s strategies for achieving its employment and human resource development objectives. The previous National Employment Policy was adopted in 1999 and its revision reaffirms the importance that the Government attaches to work with a view to continuously improving the quality of life of the people of Seychelles.

I am delighted to note that this is the first policy document adopted under the Seychelles Decent Work Country Programme (2011–2015), which is being implemented with the strong collaboration of the International Labour Organization (ILO). In this context, I wish to express my sincere appreciation to the ILO for its technical assistance throughout the review process.

The new employment policy reflects a time when our country is calling upon us to strive for greater accomplishments, whilst appreciating the sound moral values which are crucial for individual, organisational and national productivity. It is our attitude towards work that will direct the Seychelles economy to a sustainable future.

Furthermore, this important document is timely in addressing the socio-economic impact of external shocks on the labour market. Since the past economic reforms, coupled with the world financial crisis of 2008, we have witnessed a shift of labour whereby the private sector is today leading the economy and generating the majority of employment opportunities and small businesses. At the same time, other challenges have emerged, notably our limited human resources and a skills mismatch in areas of demand. The increase in demand for labour has also led to greater mobility in the labour market. Amidst these challenges, the Government has continuously been engaged in ensuring that its economic and social policies keep abreast with the changing dynamics of the labour market.

Accordingly this new policy renews our efforts to ensure that employment is at the heart of our national development strategies, so that decent jobs are continually being created with our labour force becoming more productive and competitive. My Ministry will unceasingly promote human resource development, appropriate working conditions, stable industrial relations and employment research as these will further contribute to the growth of our economy.

We have ensured that all key stakeholders have been consulted in the formulation of this new policy. I therefore urge the employers’ and workers’ representatives and the private sector to collaborate closely with the Government in the implementation of its new strategies. The new National Employment Policy reflects the hard work of all those who have contributed significantly to its development; its successful implementation, however, will depend on the commitment of all of us. This is our National Employment Policy: let us take ownership of it and let it be our guiding tool to guarantee that we attain our vision of creating decent work for all.

Finally, I wish to reiterate my gratitude to all our national and international partners for their support towards this new employment policy. Rest assured that my Ministry is fully committed to seeing its successful realisation.


**Idith Alexander**
Minister for Labour and Human Resource Development
# Executive Summary

The new **National Employment Policy (NEP)** for Seychelles is in keeping with the changing socio-economic environment and future development of the country. Since the previous employment policy was adopted in 1999, Seychellois society has undergone socio-economic changes. Prior to the late 1990s, most economic activities were carried out by government departments and parastatals. As a result, the economy was largely dominated by the public sector, employing over 65 per cent of the total labour force. Presently the public sector employs around 28 per cent of the labour force.

Although economic development is generally regarded as the principal driving force for sustainable development, it is nonetheless insufficient to achieve overall socio-economic objectives. The Seychelles Government has long recognised and further acknowledged the fact that human development and well-being cannot stand on their own either. Sustainable and durable development and progress (including human resources development) need a solid base in the form of good infrastructure and facilities, as well as policies and an institutional framework that can create an environment conducive to both domestic and foreign private investment. Such investments should aim at longer-term growth that would generate local employment. Without considerable investment in human and capital resources, economic and social progress would take longer to achieve. The Government's commitment to human development, as shown by the huge investment in health, education and training, is further proof that it is creating an enabling environment for sustainable growth and decent and productive employment for all. In spite of this, we acknowledge that the country still has some key employment issues and challenges to address such as:

* The emigration of skilled labour at all levels
* A growing number of migrant workers
* Low productivity levels and attitudes to work
* Skills shortages and mismatch of supply and demand for labour
* A growing informal sector

“**Decent work for all**” as advocated by the International Labour Organization (ILO) has been placed at the heart of the NEP. Presently, Seychelles has ratified 37 international labour Conventions out of which 29 Conventions are in force and 8 have been denounced. Furthermore, Seychelles is also benefiting from the Decent Work Country Programme (DWCP) of the ILO. Seychelles' DWCP priorities are:

1. Review of labour legislation and strengthening the dispute resolution framework
2. Promotion of quality employment and reduction of unemployment, particularly for women and youth
3. Promotion of effective tripartism and social dialogue

All these priorities are addressed by the NEP.

The NEP places great emphasis on skills development, acquisition, and retention. Integrating employment objectives as cross-cutting issues within the fiscal, monetary, trade and other sectoral policies is one key objective of the NEP. The spirit of consultation has been the cornerstone in drafting the NEP and already the sense of ownership is strong among the stakeholders. The NEP was conceived with a view to consolidating the linkages with other sectors, and thus the role of each stakeholder in ensuring the successful implementation of the NEP is crucial. The NEP will provide an all-embracing support framework to several existing national and sectoral policies related to employment and human resources development and it will further consolidate government commitment to “Decent work for all”.

# I. VISION

The long‐term vision of the National Employment Policy is to provide productive, non‐discriminatory, exploitation‐free, decent, safe and healthy work opportunities for citizens of working age. This will be achieved through building an investment‐friendly environment, in addition to managing a labour market which contributes to the national economy so that it can compete at the global level. The employment policy also ensures sustainable economic development by promoting full, productive and freely chosen employment for the country’s workforce; developing high–quality, multi‐skilled human resources that are in line with the changing needs of the industry and international labour standards.

# II. SETTING POLICY PRIORITIES

Employment challenges at the national and sectoral levels have been exacerbated in particular by issues relating to the quality of training, availability of appropriate skills, productivity, emigration of skilled professionals (“brain drain”), and synergy between the roles of social (employment and training) institutions. More importantly, the academic orientation of education and training is a major challenge to employment creation as it results in a lack of functional skills. In view of the Government’s new approach to socio‐economic development by assuming the role of facilitator while paving the way for private‐sector‐led growth and investment, it is imperative that all stakeholders involved make their specific contribution.

# III. THE POLICY FRAMEWORK

## III.A. Policy objectives

In contributing to the country's changing socio‐economic environment and future development, the employment policy endeavours to achieve the following objectives:

1. Promote full, productive and freely chosen employment without discrimination;
2. Meet national human resource requirements in line with labour market demands;
3. Maintain the right competitive edge to perform better and to stimulate economic growth and development;
4. Promote an information‐technology‐literate and competent labour force;
5. Equip Seychellois with the knowledge, competencies and entrepreneurial mindset required to compete successfully in a global, knowledge‐based economy;
6. Promote a healthy and productive labour force through continuous investment in health, education and training;
7. Promote fair and good working conditions to ensure sound industrial relations and protect the health and safety of workers.
### III.B. Guiding principles

The proposed employment policy framework is set out within the context of prudent economic management, sound macroeconomic policies and a stable political environment, where employment and non‐discrimination in the workplace are carefully integrated into the overall national development strategies of Seychelles. The successful implementation of the policy framework rests on the full commitment of the Government and the social partners (Employers’ Associations and Trade Unions). The thrust of the policy framework is directed towards enhancing both the quantity and quality of employment, leading to decent and productive employment for all, in conditions that foster equity, security, human dignity, growth and productivity. More specifically, the following will be promoted:

1. **Fostering a shared, partnership approach** that will create smart partnerships at all levels (national and organisational), where the Government provides policy leadership. This partnership should encompass individuals, groups, communities, civil society, the private sector, and the Government, as part of an overall participatory approach;
2. **Improving the quality of employment**, and not only the quantity, by promoting decent, productive and freely chosen employment;
3. **Ensuring that employment remains at the heart** of socio‐economic policies;
4. **Nurturing a stronger job‐creation** and entrepreneurial culture;
5. **Establishing well‐targeted policies and programmes** based on an efficient and effective Labour Market Information System (LMIS) that is appropriately decentralised;
6. **Encouraging an employment culture** that enhances productivity and fair and equitable performance‐based reward.

### III.C. Policy

1. The National Employment Policy has been designed taking into account the need to ensure that the country has an educated, multi‐skilled, disciplined, motivated, healthy and highly productive workforce operating in a working environment which is conducive to the achievement of this aim, with sound industrial relations practices in place. The workforce should be capable of responding to new challenges in sustaining the socio‐economic development of the country, spearheaded by private‐sector‐led investment. The Government is committed to the provision of 13 to 17 years of free education to its youth, which includes 10 years of compulsory education. It is also the duty of employers, including those in the private sector, especially in industry, to train their employees so as to promote better performance and foster career advancement.

**Government shall:**

2. Remain committed to creating the necessary enabling environment for private‐sector‐led participation in socio‐economic development and growth, with emphasis on job creation and a safe working environment in that sector, especially in the small and medium‐sized enterprises (SMEs), and on self‐employment.
3. Mainstream labour productivity in the national development strategies.
4. Make greater use of timely, accurate and reliable data from the Labour Market Information System (LMIS) for planning purposes, sound decision‐making and policy implementation and review;
LMIS could also be used to provide informed career guidance to young people in planning their careers, and to other jobseekers who are facing the need to change their current occupation.

5. Continually monitor the dynamics of the labour market for the formulation of national development policies.

6. Promote a merit‐based performance appraisal system throughout government and parastatals, while encouraging private sector participation, to ensure non‐discrimination on the basis of age, sex, disability, HIV/AIDS status, maternity/family responsibilities, race, colour, nationality, social origin, language, religion, sexual orientation, political opinion, trade union activities or other associations and any other grounds.

7. Ensure proper management of migrant workers accompanied by progressive localisation of highly skilled occupations with qualified and competent Seychellois.

8. Maintain tripartite social dialogue and bipartite collective bargaining at all levels in order to ensure the functioning of a sound industrial relations system of consultation, provision of safe, healthy and fair working conditions, and dispute resolution at work.

9. Safeguard the right and duty of every citizen, able‐bodied or with disabilities, to work and to enjoy just and favourable conditions of work without discrimination.

10. Ensure that the minimum wage regulation is being enforced and fully respected by all organisations without exception.

11. Ensure that its social and employment policy objectives support and protect the social welfare and well‐being of its citizens, and the most vulnerable persons and sectors of society.

12. Strengthen integration of Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) in schools and vocational training programmes in workplaces, so as to match industry needs, new work methods and development trends.

13. Ensure that effective career guidance and career counselling services are provided at school, national, institutional and organisational levels, to produce an informed population and a motivated and productive workforce.

**Government and social partners shall:**

14. Promote the importance of good balance between work and social life, for the well‐being of employees.

15. Promote a performance reward management system based on performance and productivity to assist in aligning people’s responsibilities with the organisation’s strategies.

16. Promote equal remuneration for Seychellois and non‐Seychellois for work of equal value.

# IV. POLICY IMPLEMENTATION AND INSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORKS

## IV.A. Policy implementation

While pursuing its objective of improving the country’s socio‐economic development, spearheaded by private‐sector‐led investment, it is also important for the Government to place productive employment and decent work priorities at the heart of the national development strategies. The overall development objective of Government will be attained through the continuous promotion of an appropriate and safe working environment, with sound industrial relations practices. This element needs to be effectively incorporated into existing national macroeconomic policies. Mainstreaming employment in the national development strategies of the country will guarantee that employment objectives are integrated as cross‐cutting priorities within the framework of fiscal, monetary, trade, and other sectoral policies. Capacity‐building, in terms of human resource development, working conditions and skills acquisition, are at the heart of the National Employment Policy. Seychelles will continue striving to create a policy and institutional environment that attracts diversified domestic and foreign direct investment which generates local employment in Seychelles.

## IV.B. Institutional frameworks

### 1. Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development (MLHRD)

The Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development (MLHRD) is the authority responsible for labour and employment issues in Seychelles, and it has the mandate to ensure that the Government’s policy on employment is implemented accordingly. It is the arm of the Government of Seychelles critically charged with the responsibility to monitor the labour market with a view to rendering it conducive to economic and social enhancement. Through its programmes, the Ministry is responsible for developing, promoting and monitoring employment laws, regulations and policies governing a wide range of issues including:

- (a) Improved economic efficiency and productivity;
- (b) Skills development and the facilitation of employment growth;
- (c) Tripartite social dialogue and sound industrial relations;
- (d) Enhanced occupational health and safety awareness and compliance in the workplace;
- (e) Promotion and protection of workers’ fundamental rights, in particular equality of treatment and non‐discrimination in the workplace;
- (f) Sensitisation of both workers and employers on their rights and responsibilities.

In order to achieve the set objectives and targets laid down in the National Employment Policy, it is imperative for the MLHRD to work in close collaboration with its social partners, other Government institutions, non‐governmental organisations (NGOs) and international partners. The MLHRD shall ensure the effective implementation of the NEP, notably through developing yearly action plans in consultation with its partners and stakeholders whilst ensuring the integration of the policy strategies into the national development strategy and other sectoral plans. The MLHRD shall evaluate the performance of the NEP and its national impact, and shall produce biannual or annual reports, as appropriate, to be presented to the National Consultative Committee on Employment (NCCE) for monitoring purposes.
## 2. Employment Tribunal

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following shall be considered:

* (a) That the Employment Tribunal shall function as an independent body/authority under the hierarchy of the court;
* (b) That the Employment Tribunal shall manage its own budget and its administration.

## 3. National Consultative Committee on Employment (NCCE)

In line with the commitment made by the Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development and in conformity with the ratified ILO Convention on Tripartite Consultation (International Labour Standards) Convention, 1976 (No. 144), a National Consultative Committee on Employment (NCCE) was set up consisting of representatives from employers and workers’ organisations, as well as key stakeholders, to provide their expertise and guidance on pertinent employment issues. The NCCE has an important role to play in monitoring the performance of the NEP and to make necessary recommendations for its successful implementation. Members of the committee are from:

* The Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development
* Association of Seychelles Employers
* Seychelles Federation of Workers’ Union
* Seychelles Chamber of Commerce and Industry (SCCI)
* The Attorney General’s Chambers

## 4. Ministry of Education

The Ministry of Education provides education from primary to post‐secondary level and it is the main provider of graduates for the labour market. However, learning outcome in general has to be improved to address the issues of skills gaps. The key challenges are the performance in primary schools, an academically‐driven secondary curriculum and students exiting post‐secondary training institutions (professional centres) with inadequate competencies to meet the demands of industry. The curricula of professional centres have to adapt quickly to the changing conditions of the labour market to produce graduates of the appropriate calibre who satisfy the requirements of the labour market. There is also a need to emphasise more focused training for job growth, especially in the areas of hospitality, construction, fisheries, financial services, information, communications and technology.

# V. RESOURCE MOBILISATION

The National Employment Policy covers a wide spectrum of activities overlapping those of other ministries and institutions not necessarily affiliated with the MLHRD. As such, it requires a high level of collaboration and, most importantly, financial resources to implement the policy. While the responsibility to finance the implementation generally rests with the Government through the national budget, some of the interventions could also be actualised with the support of development partners. Already there are development partners working either with the Government or directly with the MLHRD in implementing strategies for the country (e.g. the DWCP of ILO, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank). These partners should be approached to participate in the process. The social partners, private sector and NGOs have an equally important role to play in this process, as highlighted in the technical review of employment issues undertaken prior to the preparation of the employment policy. Involving a large audience of collaborators is a means of fostering a sense of ownership among the various partners and consolidating and extending synergies between existing programmes, especially in education and training, and in public‐private partnerships (PPP), particularly in the tourism sector.

# VI. MONITORING AND EVALUATION

## VI.A. Monitoring and evaluation

The labour market, being a dynamic place with major interventions, requires continual evaluation, review and monitoring. The monitoring and evaluation aspect of the policy falls under the authority of the Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development in collaboration with stakeholders and other social partners. In order to ensure effective implementation of the programmes under the policy, continual monitoring and evaluation is indispensable. Evaluations should take place at regular intervals (biannual), especially during the first two years, and thereafter once a year. Furthermore, the Labour Market Information System is an important tool that could facilitate the monitoring and evaluation process by providing timely, accurate and reliable labour market information. Routine reports will be produced annually.

## VI.B. Review of Policy

The policy will be reviewed every five years to reflect changes in trends in development and to adapt to challenges in the global socio‐economic environment.

# Appendix I: Strategies of the National Employment Policy

## A. Problem Identification (Issues and Challenges)

The main issues and challenges identified in the context of Seychelles have been disaggregated into the following:

1. **Prudent macroeconomic management and sound macroeconomic policies**
    - (a) Risk of exchange rate instability and fluctuating inflation and their potential adverse effects on real wages;
    - (b) High cost of production and reduction in employment opportunities;
    - (c) Limited economic diversity due to the small size of the economy.

2. **Human resources and skills development and employability**
    - (a) Skills shortages and mismatch of supply with demand for labour;
    - (b) Need for improved responsiveness of post‐secondary training institutions (professional centres) to the current and emerging demands of the labour market;
    - (c) Need for improved performance of graduates from training institutions;
    - (d) Need for improved performance of students from the school system;
    - (e) Skills acquisition and Technical and Vocational Training needs to be strengthened;
    - (f) Weak performance management system in the workplace in some sectors.

3. **Active labour market policies, employment services and labour market information system**
    - (a) Lack of standards and quality of labour market data;
    - (b) Lack of clear structure for employment services;
    - (c) Inadequate coordination among the relevant institutions.

4. **Sectoral development, small and medium-sized enterprise development and formalisation of informal employment**
    - (a) Updated legislative and policy environment for starting SMEs required;
    - (b) Lack of entrepreneurial skills to cope with rapidly changing scientific, technological and innovation development;
    - (c) A growing informal sector.

5. **Migration, foreign labour and localisation**
    - (a) Small population size characterised by a slow growth rate and an ageing population, creating a limited human resource base;
    - (b) “Brain drain” – migration of skilled labour at all levels;
    - (c) Growing number of incoming migrant workers;
(d) Better management and regulation of migrant workers and enhanced employability of local workers needed.

## 6. Youth employment
(a) Youth unemployment and number of dropouts from the school system, especially among young males;
(b) Higher substance abuse among the youth.

## 7. Vulnerable groups/Labour force challenges
(a) Productivity enhancement and improved attitude to work are required;
(b) The blight of substance abuse (alcohol and drugs) and its adverse effects on productivity and attitude to work;
(c) Significant proportion of the local labour force suffering from chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension and an alarming number of cases of Hepatitis C infection.

## 8. Non-discrimination in the workplace
(a) Need to ensure non-discrimination and equality of treatment in employment for women, the elderly, people with disabilities, people living with HIV/AIDS and migrant workers;
(b) Increase in labour mobility across occupations in certain sectors mainly due to a decrease in real wages and competing demand for labour;
(c) Lack of various types of flexible working conditions.

## 9. Social protection
(a) Efficacy and sustainability of the current social protection schemes need to be assessed.

## 10. Labour market institutions and social dialogue
(a) Weak worker education;
(b) Strengthening of Public-Private Partnership (PPP);
(c) Social dialogue institutions and environment.

As a consequence, the priority challenges to be addressed by the national employment policy framework include:

1. Integration of employment priorities into the macroeconomic policies;
2. Integration of employment and skills development priorities into sectoral development strategies and plans;
3. Better management of skills retention of Seychellois workers;
4. Better management of employment of non-Seychellois and the localisation strategy;
5. Development and sustainment of Seychellois human resources to reduce the skills mismatch and shortages;
6. Development of a fully functional Labour Market Information System;
7. Development of a legislative and policy environment conducive to the evolution of SMEs;
8. Addressing youth employment challenges;
9. Gender dimensions of the employment/occupation patterns and key employment issues to be tackled to ensure equal employment and training opportunities and fair treatment for all regardless of gender, disability or other grounds;
10. Addressing HIV/AIDS‐related issues and how the overall trends may be affecting the demographic trends and employment patterns, as well as any related discrimination issues;
11. Ensuring the inclusion of those who are most vulnerable in employment and employment programmes;
12. The long‐term sustainability, effectiveness and coverage of social protection, and in particular the extent to which social welfare schemes and programmes effectively cover the most vulnerable and the overall long‐term financial viability of such schemes;
13. The effectiveness and scope of the current tripartite social dialogue institutions and their functions, and how they can be improved to create an environment more conducive to social dialogue;
14. Current key employment and skills development challenges faced by persons living with disabilities and older workers aged 55 years and over, so that they may become employable, economically independent and safe from poverty;
15. Fostering fair working conditions and benefits, and a working environment conducive to productivity and harmonious industrial relations;
16. The need for constant review of employment legislation in line with the current economic environment and international labour standards.

As identified in the technical report,³ the origins of the priority employment issues are related primarily to skills shortages and a mismatch of supply and demand for labour, caused by the small size of the population, emigration and inability of some training institutions to produce sufficient graduates that can be easily absorbed by the labour market. Productivity, the growing number of foreign labour and the lack of social dialogue are also important factors.

# B. Strategies

The National Employment Policy intends to attain its objectives through the following strategies:

## 1. Prudent economic management and sound macroeconomic policies

- **(a)** Contribute to the attainment of macroeconomic stability through the promotion of employment in support of growth;
- **(b)** Ensure better policy coordination and synergies and placing employment and human resource development at the heart of all socio‐economic policies, i.e. fiscal, monetary, trade and other sectoral policies;
- **(c)** Create sufficient and appropriate fiscal space for human resource development, which meets the current and future labour market demand through adequate fiscal incentives for the private sector.

## 2. Human resources and skills development and employability

### Education

#### (i) Primary education
Universal access to primary education is a constitutional right in Seychelles; however, students’ performance needs to be improved.

#### (ii) Secondary education
- **(a)** On average, around 75 per cent of students successfully complete secondary education (up to S5); the target rate should be a minimum of 90 per cent to have an impact on the labour market and youth unemployment;
- **(b)** The high dropout rate (around 25 per cent) and those who are not registered for the International General Certificate of Secondary Education (IGCSE) (around 22 per cent) has led to a skills gap in some sectors, particularly in construction, education, health and tourism, a situation that requires urgent attention. Enrolment numbers at “A” level are not sufficient to make any inroads in the preparation of the required number of future professionals, and the school system needs to produce better‐quality students.

#### (iii) Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) in secondary schools
One of the main critics of TVET is that the policy formulation and development process has been undertaken in a variety of forms and by different sectors or sub‐sectors. There needs to be an integrated policy framework on technical and vocational education and training. Government should allocate an appropriate budget and sufficient resources to secondary schools to implement TVET.

#### (iv) Tertiary education
**Post‐Secondary Training Institutions (Professional Centres):**
- **(a)** The PSTIs must adopt a model where industry is directly involved with the school curriculum, in accordance with the Tertiary Education Act, 2011; Government must promote the Public‐Private Partnership in training, so as to obtain more direct input from the private
sector in designing training programmes that meet labour market requirements; PSTIs must review and adapt their curricula in line with the requirements of the labour market as far as possible and in accordance with the regulations of the Seychelles Qualification Authority (SQA);

* (b) Each PSTI should establish a Governing Board comprising of representatives of Government, the private sector and civil society, with clear mandates and set targets (see the Tertiary Education Act, 2011);
* (c) The governing boards of PSTIs should have greater input in course development;
* (d) Linkages between PSTIs, the university and the private sector, particularly in the development of programme delivery and in their research agenda, should offer more prospects and be further strengthened;
* (e) An in‐depth feasibility study to determine whether certain PSTIs would perform better if they were relocated under their appropriately affiliated ministry, whilst continuing to receive curriculum and pedagogical support from the Ministry of Education, should be undertaken;
* (f) Entry requirements for PSTIs should be respected to ensure that standards are maintained, thus ensuring a better quality of students and graduates that will meet the requirements of the labour market;
* (g) Incorporation of apprenticeship schemes in the PSTI curriculum as a form of training for those who do not meet the entry requirements should be considered;
* (h) Government should promote greater awareness of the different pathways from "A" levels (advanced secondary) to tertiary education (professional centres and university);
* (i) The Government should provide sufficient funds for PSTIs to cater for more training materials and equipment and for the recruitment of better‐qualified staff;
* (j) The number and types of courses offered need to respond to labour market needs;
* (k) Work attachment programmes should be structured and formalised to ensure effective school‐to‐work transition;
* (l) Government should promote recognition of prior learning as an alternative pathway towards obtaining a qualification;

### The University of Seychelles:

* (m) The number and types of courses the university offers need to respond to labour market needs;
* (n) The university should continue to play an important role in professional development with the aim of developing new sectors;
* (o) The university should place emphasis on degree‐level courses and research;
* (p) Tertiary education institutions (including PSTIs) should continue to explore twinning programmes, where necessary;
* (q) Work attachment programmes should be structured and formalised to ensure effective school‐to‐work transition.
(v) Sustaining skills development and producing a marketable and multi-skilled labour force to respond to labour market needs by:

    (a) Equipping training institutions with the necessary infrastructure, human resources capacity, and access to timely and robust labour market information in order to ensure successful absorption of their graduates in the labour market;
    (b) Allocating a sufficient share of the budgets of relevant ministries to education and training;
    (c) Pursuing a skills retention and intensive training programme within the framework of fiscal, monetary, trade, and other sectoral policies by integrating employment objectives as cross-cutting issues;
    (d) Promoting a multi-skilled labour force through continuous investment in education and training;
    (e) Transforming the education and training system to become more responsive to the dynamics and demands of the labour market;
    (f) Putting in place mechanisms, such as retention schemes, a reward system and career planning, within organisations, to counter emigration of skilled workers in sectors most affected (for example, education, health, and finance and business);
    (g) Identifying ways to attract emigrated professionals to participate in the development of the country;
    (h) Establishing and implementing a good human resource development strategy that is responsive to the dynamics of the labour market in Seychelles;
    (i) Creating an efficient and effective Labour Market Information System to enhance labour market analysis and human resource development and utilisation;
    (j) Promoting the Public-Private Partnership approach to education and training and consolidating social dialogue;
    (k) Encouraging businesses to contribute to training as part of their corporate social responsibility;
    (l) Improving the quality of students produced by the school system;
    (m) Providing opportunities for employees to update their knowledge to keep abreast of new developments in the job market;
    (n) Providing affordable training that is available to in-service workers and pre-service students to ensure that people can access further training that is in line with human resource needs;

### Apprenticeship schemes:

    (o) There needs to be renewed impetus and drive;
    (p) The schemes should be enhanced in the PSTI curriculum;
    (q) There should be more interaction with employers for the development and effective implementation of apprenticeship programmes;
    (r) PSTIs should review and adapt their curricula in line with the requisites of the labour market as far as possible and in accordance with the regulations of the Seychelles Qualification Authority (SQA).
# 3. Active labour market policies, employment services and labour market information system

## 3.1 Empower jobseekers to be more marketable

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

* (a) Incorporating interview skills in school curricula, post‐secondary training institutions and skills acquisition programmes;
* (b) Revamping the career guidance and counselling unit of the Ministry of Education in consultation with the Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development;
* (c) The MLHRD should work in close collaboration with the private employment agencies to refer jobseekers to opportunities for basic soft skills development, career guidance and counselling should be intensified at the national level, including in schools and organisations;
* (d) Promoting a mechanism to encourage jobseekers as well as in‐service employees to undertake training, especially at diploma and certificate level, at an affordable rate;
* (e) Emphasising that staff at all levels should benefit from a marketable allowance or an increase in salary after receipt of a certificate and/or diploma qualification;
* (f) Considering strategies for the retention of skilled workers, in areas of demand, who have passed the retirement age and who would like to remain in employment, in order to address the problems of the ageing population, skills shortages and growing employment of migrant workers. For example, consider introducing part‐time schemes and mentoring.

## 3.2 Employment services

In line with the government strategy to reform the public sector, part of the employment service was privatised. Effective September 2009, jobseeker registration and job placement, two activities previously carried out by the ministry responsible for labour, were outsourced to private employment agencies. Presently, services to jobseekers, i.e. vacancy information, registration and placement in employment, are provided by private employment agencies and coordinated by the MLHRD.

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

* (a) Encouraging the registration by the private employment agencies of vacancies, jobseekers and placements by employers, for the purpose of accounting when analysing national labour market trends;
* (b) By law, ensuring effective application and enforcement of the use of job cards;
* (c) Establishing close working links with other government bodies involved in the process of data capture, e.g. the Seychelles Revenue Commission, the Seychelles Pension Fund and the National Bureau of Statistics, with a view to improving the quality and scope of data collection;
* (d) By law, making it mandatory for private employment agencies to comply with statistical procedures as set by the MLHRD.

***

**Note:** It is against the Employment Act for employers to employ anyone above retirement age unless authorization has been sought and received from the ministry concerned.
### 3.3 Retraining

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

* (a) The Government should continue to make budgetary provisions available to MLHRD to cater for retraining of jobseekers;
* (b) The Labour Department should continually identify groups of jobseekers and coordinate the provision of appropriate training in response to labour market needs.

### 3.4 Job promotion fair

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

* (a) A formal structure should be put in place (by the MLHRD in collaboration with the private sector) to ensure effective planning and implementation;
* (b) Fairs should be organised annually (two on Mahé and one on Praslin) by the MLHRD in collaboration with the private sector;
* (c) The private sector should also hold recruitment/job promotion fairs based on the demands of their respective organisations.

### 3.5 Recruitment of local workers for other islands[^1]

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

* (a) Maintaining regular joint inspections of workers’ accommodation by officials of the MoH and the MLHRD;
* (b) Strengthening standards on the quality of worker accommodation to ensure that workers feel safe and secure, and can live comfortably;
* (c) Providing incentives to organisations to develop social facilities for their employees, such as a cinema, tutoring for children, day‐care, and similar attractions;
* (d) Taking into account the family status of local workers to encourage work on the outlying islands.

### 3.6 Labour Market Information Systems (LMIS)

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

**National Bureau of Statistics:**

* (a) Having a standardised format for data across all ministries and organisations;
* (b) Future labour force surveys should be carried out in collaboration with the MLHRD;
* (c) Increasing the frequency of labour force surveys to every 2 years;
* (d) MLHRD should work in collaboration with the National Bureau of Statistics and other partners to strengthen institutional capacity to harmonise data collection procedures and reporting.

[^1]: Excluding the main island of Mahé.
(e) MLHRD should aim at improving the quality of statistics, especially labour market indicators, with a view to better guide decisions and strategies;

### Employment data:
(f) There should be a fully functional LMIS by the end of 2014;
(g) Data collection procedures should be standardised;
(h) The LMIS should be extended to include the Employment Tribunal, education, training, the Seychelles Revenue Commission, immigration and others;
(i) The private employment agencies should (indirectly) be part of the LMIS;
(j) By law, the private employment agencies should provide the MLHRD with timely and accurate information following an agreed format and schedule;
(k) The contents of the Labour Statistics Bulletin should be revamped, assuming that all relevant stakeholders improve and adhere to data collection standards;
(l) Short qualitative surveys and regular, annual, employment surveys should be conducted, provided that the necessary resources are made available.

## 4. Sectoral development, small and medium-sized enterprise development and formalisation of informal employment

### 4.1 Sectoral development strategy
(a) Integrate employment priorities into the sectoral development strategies (tourism, construction, fisheries, agriculture, transport, information technology and communications, off-shore banking and other sectors);
(b) Implement strategies to formalise the informal sector by making available the necessary resources and infrastructure that promote the development of small and medium-sized enterprises and cottage industry;
(c) Target sectors and training programmes that enhance the labour market absorptive capacity of the economy by, for instance:
	(i) Putting in place an appropriate legal framework to address anti-competitive conduct by firms in naturally competitive markets and ensuring that the legislation is enforced;
	(ii) Considering the creation of a one-stop shop to facilitate business activities, hence eliminating some of the administrative procedures that hinder business development and employment opportunities.

### 4.2 Boosting productivity

To address productivity issues in Seychelles, there must be a national drive, policies and a clear national productivity management framework. Due consideration should be given to align performance management systems between the workers’ performance targets and the organisation’s objectives and goals, to ensure ownership. It is therefore essential that such a framework be developed, along with a clear strategic plan that is ultimately communicated to the community.
It will be necessary to strengthen the multi‐sectoral productivity secretariat of the Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development so as to help integrate and mainstream labour productivity in the national development strategies of the country. This would be achieved through:

### 4.2.1 Leadership
- (a) Developing engagement strategies to allow organisations to determine the needs and expectations of different stakeholders;
- (b) Establishing a framework to allow more effective delegation of autonomy in decision‐making and implementation of strategies;
- (c) Employers being committed to the workplace, thus empowering their staff;
- (d) Nurturing the notion of productivity in all schoolchildren in the country.

### 4.2.2 Strategy and Planning
- (a) Aligning plans and targets of organisations with those of the country’s strategies – there is a need for the National Employment Policy and National Development Plan to be effectively communicated to the public, through the media, with briefing in English, French and Creole;
- (b) Communicating the organisational vision and mission, translated into measurable goals and objectives;
- (c) Instituting good monitoring mechanisms within organisations, in order to know how they are performing against set plans and targets;
- (d) Strengthening existing systems that allow for more effective data collection – this should apply to all relevant ministries, organisations and stakeholders, through regular monitoring and collection of data.

### 4.2.3 Human Resources Management
As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:
- (a) Reviewing the national human resource development policies in line with a coherent management philosophy, and having an organisational culture that is communicated throughout organisations. Such culture should be mainstreamed in all management systems and decisions that affect the nature of the relationship between an organisation and its employees;
- (b) Developing and implementing strategies within organisations, which build the ideal organisational culture that favours productivity. The beliefs, ideologies, principles and values of an organisation form its culture and the culture of the workplace has a direct impact on productivity;
- (c) Promoting awareness of the well‐being of employees, and the importance of creating a good balance between work and social life;
- (d) Developing and implementing a performance reward system to assist in aligning people with their responsibilities and how best they can be achieved;
- (e) Empowering staff to understand the challenges and be part of change;
- (f) Continual updating and harmonisation of employment laws, through tripartite social dialogue, to ensure good human resources management practices, thereby promoting sound industrial relations;
(g) Promoting the sharing of best practices among organisations and stakeholders;
(h) Employers developing and implementing induction programmes, to familiarise new employees with the strategic plans of the organisation and allow for better integration within the organisation;
(i) Aligning training objectives with organisational goals, bearing in mind that each individual's skills and abilities play a vital part in workplace productivity. Training needs assessments should be conducted to allow organisations to discover exactly where the skills gaps are in a workforce, and provide continual development through a range of targeted training.

### 4.3 Promotion of SMEs (Enterprise development)

(a) Promote active labour market policy interventions and other measures to curb the growing informal sector, especially through the promotion and formalisation of SMEs;
(b) Review the current legislative frameworks related to the establishment of SMEs and remove the barriers for doing business, thereby creating an environment conducive to the development of small and medium‐sized enterprises – particular attention should be paid to youth and women;
(c) Promote financial support for SMEs, especially by the banking sector, and adopt a systemic approach to enterprise development;
(d) Inculcate a culture of entrepreneurship, hence changing the perception of self‐employment;
(e) Consider establishing entrepreneurship development programmes, targeting professionals and jobseekers;
(f) Promote business development services at the intermediate level, as well as appropriate micro‐level interventions;
(g) Promote SMEs and provide technical support and management development services to entrepreneurs to build their capacity;
(h) Encourage the private sector and NGOs to engage in entrepreneurship development;
(i) Develop a national policy and strategy to promote the growth of SMEs in different economic sectors and foster employment creation.

## 5. Migration, foreign labour and localisation

### 5.1 Foreign and local labour

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following may be considered:

(a) The Government should set up a taskforce in collaboration with the MLHRD, the Ministry of Finance, and other partners, to undertake future reviews of the minimum wage regulations so as to ensure meaningful consultation and effective application of the mechanism stipulated in the relevant ratified international labour Conventions;
(b) Any organisation breaching the minimum wage regulations to be prosecuted in accordance with the Employment Act;
(c) To consider introducing sectoral wage schemes in areas of demand through collective bargaining;
(d) Attestation of contract for migrant workers to be well scrutinised and contracts of employment of migrant workers must be translated by the employer into the national languages of the migrant workers;
(e) Terms and conditions of employment for migrant workers must be complied with and penalties are to be imposed on employers failing to abide by such practices;
(f) To ensure that all workers receive a payslip, regardless of nationality;
(g) The MLHRD must ensure that important information about Seychelles labour laws is translated by the employer into the different languages necessary according to the different nationalities of migrant workers in Seychelles;
(h) Terms and conditions of employment of migrant workers must be clearly stipulated;
(i) Promoting equal pay for work of equal value regardless of nationality at all levels.

## 5.2 Localisation strategies

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

### 5.2.1 Short-term Strategies

(a) **Restrict non‐Seychellois employment in cases where qualified Seychellois are not available:**
- (i) Approval of employment of a non‐Seychellois should not be granted if a qualified Seychellois is available to fill the post;
- (ii) PSTIs should develop courses at post‐secondary level, based on current and future demands.

(b) **Monitoring of organisational localisation strategy and plans:**
- (i) Organisations employing non‐Seychellois must have an approved localisation plan;
- (ii) The MLHRD will monitor the implementation of the localisation plan through regular visits;
- (iii) Renewal of gainful occupation permits (GOP) for consecutive employment of non‐Seychellois should be subject to the execution of localisation plans.

(c) **Quarterly submission of establishment lists by organisations to the MLHRD:**
- (i) Strengthen collaboration between the MLHRD and the Seychelles Revenue Commission, Seychelles Pension Fund and other relevant agencies to gain access to relevant data of working organisations;
- (ii) Such lists will provide information on the employment status of individuals, their positions and pay levels.

(d) **Committee on Employment of Non‐Seychellois to discuss recruitment and management of migrant workers:**
- (i) Have a checklist of issues discussed and actions to be taken for future reference;
- (ii) Recommend strategies to effectively manage the employment of non‐Seychellois in specific occupations and specific sectors, based on the labour market needs, notably through review of the quota system and continual update of the list of occupations reserved for Seychellois.
 (e) **Promotion of Seychellois to higher positions, allowing recruitment of other Seychellois in junior positions:**
    (i) Organisations should be encouraged to put in place in‐house training plans with the objective of promoting existing employees into higher positions, thus increasing the number of posts localised;
    (ii) Organisations should, as part of their corporate social responsibility, invest in effective in‐house training programmes through the fiscal incentives provided by the Government.

 (f) **Improvement of workers’ well‐being (e.g., accommodation, leisure time) in establishments:**
    (i) Regular joint inspections should be made by officials from the MoH and MLHRD;
    (ii) MLHRD to collaborate with MoH to ensure standards are met on the quality of workers’ (including migrants’) accommodation, to ensure that workers feel safe, secure and can live comfortably.

 (g) **Guidelines for vacancy advertisements in newspapers:**
    (i) Legislation needs to be strongly considered and implemented;
    (ii) A standard format, comprising basic information, for publication of vacancies must be implemented;

 (h) **Selected occupations to be reserved for Seychellois only, depending on labour market signals:**
    (i) Certain activities must be reserved for Seychellois jobseekers;
    (ii) The MLHRD must devise a list clearly defining the jobs reserved for Seychellois. The list is to be regularly updated to ensure that it always accurately reflects labour market needs.

### 5.2.2 Long­term strategy and intervention:
See Section 2 – Human resources and skills development and employability.

(d) Establish benchmarks, and monitor and evaluate the performance of policies on youth employment;

* (g) Strengthen career guidance programmes within the Ministry of Education and include their integration in the Seychelles National Youth Council programme;
* (h) Increase information and career and vocational guidance to those classified as “Not in Employment, Education or Training” (NEET), including through the social media;
* (i) Update school curricula to respond to labour market demands;

### Advocacy and partnerships:

* (v) Encourage partnerships between training institutions and enterprises to include work placement of young graduates as part of the curricula;
* (w) Advocate safe working conditions for youth in accordance with the ratified international labour Convention No. 138;
* (x) Support youth participation in decision–making;
* (y) Promote youth rights and responsibilities in the world of work with in collaboration with the ILO constituents.
## 7. Vulnerable groups/Labour force challenges

Enhance participation of vulnerable groups in the labour market such as young mothers, young single parents (14–17 years of age), workers above the age of 55 years, welfare‐dependent people, ex‐convicts, substance‐abuse social welfare cases, non‐academic youths, and people living with disabilities by:

1. Offering subsidised training or retraining;
2. Considering providing specific employment incentives to people living with physical or mental disabilities, such as allowing retention of a portion of their invalidity benefit even after taking up employment;
3. Providing flexible working conditions, targeting young mothers and other women who would like to contribute to the labour market but due to other responsibilities in the home cannot assume full‐time employment;
4. Providing fiscal incentives to organisations committed to participating in schemes that target employment of vulnerable groups;
5. Monitoring programmes for vulnerable groups, including those with criminal records to ensure effectiveness and productivity;
6. Raising awareness within tripartite consultations on the importance of supporting vulnerable groups, especially with regard to employment opportunities;
7. Promoting incentive schemes for employers who employ vulnerable groups such as ex‐convicts, youths with criminal record, people with disabilities and pregnant women;
8. Ensuring non‐discrimination against people with disabilities in terms of wages;
9. Collaborating with the Ministry of Social Affairs, Community Development and Sports and stakeholders in implementing the “Employee Assistance Programme”.

## 8. Non-discrimination in the workplace

It is important for the MLHRD and key partners to conduct a study on discrimination in the workplace to identify any possible forms of discrimination. The study will also aim to ensure the effective application of the ratified ILO Conventions on Equal Remuneration, 1951 (No. 100) and; Discrimination (Employment and Occupation), 1958 (No. 111) and to promote good human resources practices.

### 8.1 Promotion of gender equality
As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

* (a) Putting in place mechanisms to ensure equal and fair treatment for both women and men;
* (b) Making provision for special measures for parents with young children, including flexible working conditions and working from home, if applicable;
* (c) Considering programmes to support functional families, such as granting paternity leave. In so doing, recognising and supporting efforts of good fathers, promoting positive healthy masculinity, supporting mothers and encouraging closer relationships between children and their fathers, and devising suitable methods that meet the primary goal of supporting functional families;
* (d) Promoting the use of gender‐neutral language in labour laws and regulations;
- (e) Improving the availability of quality and timely gender‐responsive labour statistics (especially in the private sector) and economic development (e.g. access to credit, property, tenders, subsidies, etc.);
- (f) Promoting the value, professionalism and gender equality of female‐dominated sectors such as teaching, nursing and social work, particularly through training according to the requirements of the labour market;
- (g) Promoting education and skills development of women and girls in economically strategic sectors which traditionally have been male dominated, including but not limited to information, communications and technology, engineering (solar/wind power, mechanical, avionic, etc.), construction, heavy machinery operation, plumbing, carpentry, transport, fishing, farming, and other science, technology, engineering and maths (STEM) sectors;
- (h) Encouraging women and men to move from the informal sector to the formal sector, which offers more job security and better working conditions;
- (i) Further promoting and actively supporting women’s participation in economic leadership;
- (j) Promoting gender parity in leadership and membership of national boards and implementing temporary special measures to ensure achievement of a target of at least 40 per cent of members and chairpersons of national boards being women;
- (k) Monitoring discrimination against pregnant and lactating female workers in the private sector, and ensuring compliance with the law. Setting up focal points in MLHRD and conducting awareness‐raising campaigns among tripartite social partners;
- (l) Fostering favourable working conditions to support the breast‐feeding policy;
- (m) Raising awareness with employers regarding employment rights of lactating or pregnant women;
- (n) Encouraging and supporting the establishment of workplace child care facilities, where feasible;
- (o) Improving working conditions and the enforcement of safety regulations, especially in male‐dominated sectors involving manual labour;
- (p) Establishing safeguards in relation to occupational health and safety, social services and equality of opportunity and treatment of women working night shifts. This should include provisions for employers to provide collective means of transport for night workers, and provision of food‐heating facilities and child care;
- (q) Strengthening protection of workers from the risk of violence, especially those in high‐risk jobs, e.g. medical staff, police officers, prison officers and defence forces;
- (r) Taking effective legal measures, including penal sanctions, civil remedies and compensatory provisions, to protect women and men against sexual harassment in the workplace, including domestic workers, people living with HIV/AIDS and migrant workers. Laws and policies need to explicitly define sexual harassment and provide appropriate deterrent sanctions that reflect the unacceptability and serious nature of this form of discrimination. The laws should also ensure that employers and workers have accessible complaint mechanisms in place and foster a safe working environment.

## 8.2 Discrimination against people living with disabilities

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

- (a) Building gender‐sensitive information and statistics on people living with disabilities who are engaged in economic activity, through strengthened capacity in data–gathering, statistical analysis and sharing of information;
- (b) Ensuring that people living with disabilities are recognised as particularly vulnerable to harassment, including that of a sexual nature, and ensuring that all laws, policies and programmes are responsive to their special needs and vulnerabilities;
- (c) Raising awareness of the right to work of people living with disabilities, targeting families and potential employers;
- (d) Building the capacity of the Vocational Unit of the National Council for Disabled Persons to provide technical vocational training and guidance to the increasing numbers of people living with disabilities;
- (e) Introducing work experience and job placement programmes in the open labour market for people living with disabilities;
- (f) Ensuring that hiring processes do not discriminate against people living with disabilities;
- (g) Ensuring that people living with disabilities receive remuneration equal to that received by any other worker for work of equal value;
- (h) Monitoring and evaluating discrimination against people with disabilities in the labour force, including in the areas of hiring, firing, training, advancement, remuneration, working conditions, protection from harassment and redress;
- (i) Introducing temporary special measures, incentives and other measures to encourage people living with disabilities to enter employment and entrepreneurship;
- (j) Promoting flexible working hours to facilitate employment for persons with disabilities;
- (k) Ensuring that reasonable accommodation is provided to persons with disabilities in the workplace where applicable;
- (l) Encouraging the practice of making workplace buildings in Seychelles accessible to people with disabilities;
- (m) Ensuring that people who have had accidents or have any other disability have equal opportunities in terms of training and upgrading their skills;
- (n) Ensuring that the Employment Tribunal and job agency buildings are accessible to people living with disabilities;
- (o) Strengthening inspection of and compliance with health and safety regulations, especially in the private and parastatals sector to reduce the risk of temporary disability among men and women.

### 8.3 Discrimination against the elderly
As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

- (a) Ensuring that older workers are protected against discrimination, particularly as regards to termination of employment;
- (b) Strengthening the gathering and provision of statistics on elderly people living in Seychelles, which is essential for future planning of services;
- (c) Providing clear and gender‐responsive policy guidelines on retirement preparedness and active ageing to better prepare people for the economic, social and psychological realities of retirement. A policy should be introduced for voluntary, gradual and flexible transition from active working life to retirement in accordance with international labour Older Workers Recommendation, 1980 (No. 162);
- (d) Encouraging retired men and women to fill skilled human resource gaps and ensure elderly people continue to have access to decent work and receive equal pay for work of equal value
when in employment of their choice in both the public and private sector, ensuring that they receive equal treatment and terms and conditions, in accordance with the law;

(e) Ensuring that job agencies and other placement services provide equal treatment to older persons seeking work and do not advertise jobs with age limits.

## 8.4 Discrimination against people living with HIV/AIDS

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

(a) Reinforcing and coordinating workplace initiatives on HIV and AIDS by:
- Identifying and promoting best practices
- Promoting the HIV/AIDS workplace policy
- Ensuring harmonisation of national legislation with HIV/AIDS policies of the MLHRD and MoH;
(b) Ensuring widespread availability of the Policy on HIV/AIDS in the Workplace among all employers;
(c) Raising awareness of tripartite members on the Policy of HIV/AIDS in the Workplace and ensuring the establishment of HIV and AIDS focal points;
(d) Setting up benchmarks and a framework to monitor and evaluate the implementation of the policy through the focal points;
(e) Spelling out that the scope of the policy applies also to affected persons who are apprentices or volunteers, or are in the informal economy, armed forces and uniformed services, in accordance with the international labour HIV and AIDS Recommendation, 2010 (No. 200);
(f) Strengthening policy provisions on the prevention and prohibition of violence and harassment in the workplace, non‐discrimination against persons living with HIV/AIDS irrespective of their sexual orientation, active participation of men and women in preventive measures, the development of harm‐reduction guidelines, ensuring that testing is genuinely voluntary and free of any coercion, and focusing education on migrant workers and youth;
(g) To ensure compliance with international regulations concerning protection of workers with HIV/AIDS in the workplace, the Government shall enact and enforce laws and regulations and introduce penalties against employers or employers’ representatives forcing a worker, including migrant workers, to undertake HIV testing or disclose their HIV status for ill intentions which may lead to discrimination and/or place the worker in a negative socio‐economic situation, or refuse employment or terminate or discriminate against a worker on the basis of a real or perceived HIV status;
(h) Promote the development and implementation of sectoral strategies, with particular attention to sectors most at risk to infection with HIV, e.g. the MoH, private health practices, police, defence forces, security forces, prisons, beauty salons and waste management;
(i) Provide legal protection against breaches of privacy and confidentiality with regard to HIV status;
(j) Strengthen procedures and training on “shared confidentiality” of the HIV status of employees, in consultation with the MoH, following best practices of the Seychelles National HIV Testing and Counselling Guidelines.
## 9. Social protection

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

(a) Changing the ways in which disability is assessed from a purely medical to a more holistic approach;
(b) Changing the working environment so it better accommodates people living with disabilities;
(c) Reinforcing the concept of co‐responsibility through increased linkages between the MLHRD and the Agency for Social Protection, to ensure efficient allocation of social welfare assistance through a mechanism to encourage the unemployed to take up productive employment;
(d) The Agency for Social Protection should work closely with the National Council for Disabled Persons and the ministry concerned, in reviewing the entitlement criteria of a person with a disability who enters the job market.

## 10. Labour market institutions and social dialogue

As an immediate and/or medium‐term strategy, the following should be considered:

(a) Strengthening social dialogue and ensuring that it is structured effectively;
(b) Promoting a working environment which is conducive to sound industrial relations practices;
(c) Conducting periodic reviews of the Employment Act and the Industrial Relations Act with the aim of ensuring conformity with international labour standards and more flexible and responsive to the reality of today’s socio‐economic environment;
(d) Creating a labour clinic, targeting organisations with a high number of employment and labour‐related issues;
(e) Encouraging organisations and enterprises to establish internal dispute resolution systems;
(f) Ensuring that all human resources managers obtain basic training in Labour Law;
(g) MLHRD and Social Partners (Employers’ Associations and Trade Unions) to educate all workers and employers on their basic rights and responsibilities.
# Appendix II: Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development’s core functions

The core functions of the Ministry of Labour and Human Resource Development are summarised as follows:

1. **Formulate and implement employment and labour policies**: National Employment Policy, labour market policies, health and safety policy, Policy on HIV/AIDS in the Workplace, policy on employment of non‐Seychellois, and other relevant policies;
2. **Enforce and where applicable review employment legislation**: namely the Employment Act and subsidiary legislation, the Industrial Relations Act, the Occupational Safety and Health Decree;
3. **Adhere to the 29 ratified ILO Conventions**, particularly the 8 core Conventions and the decent work agenda;
4. **Provide employment services with a view to promoting full employment**: Employment and skills development schemes, productivity awareness campaign, employment registration and placement through private employment agencies and management of employment of non‐Seychellois;
5. **Promote social dialogue and collective bargaining** to ensure harmonious industrial relations through mediation, enforcement of the Industrial Relations Act, the Employment Act and the Employment Tribunal;
6. **Promote and ensure a safe and healthy working environment**: through the enforcement of the Occupational Safety and Health Decree, radiation safety, and protection through labour inspections);
7. **Promote the principles of Decent Work** and contribute to the enhancement of productivity in collaboration with social partners;
8. **Act as government focal point** for international labour relations;
9. **Collate and disseminate employment statistics** through the Labour Market Information System;
10. **Coordinate national human resources planning and utilisation**: as well as development of human resources in the public and private sectors;
11. **Promote a culture of training and development** and lifelong learning in individuals and organisations;
12. **Initiate research in the field of human resources development**, and design and implement strategies for the effective development of human resources;
13. **Manage and administer the Government of Seychelles Scholarships Scheme** and tertiary training fund;
14. **Promote initiatives for the provision of training** to meet the human resources needs of the country.

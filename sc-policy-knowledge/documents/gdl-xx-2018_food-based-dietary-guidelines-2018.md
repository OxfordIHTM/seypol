## Preface

Nutrient-based dietary guidelines has been found to be ineffective in promoting and establishing healthy eating habits of the target population (Gibney & Vorster, 2001). Although food is made up of nutrients an individual consumes more than just nutrients in food. As such it is important to advocate for the consumption of whole foods to attain maximum nutritional and health benefits as well as promote the enjoyment of eating. Food-based dietary guidelines has now taken precedence as a means of ensuring the individual attains a high nutritional status (Gibney & Vorster, 2001).
# Food-Based Dietary Guidelines for Seychelles

The new food-based dietary guidelines for Seychelles consists of a series of statements derived from the translation of the most up-to-date scientific evidence into simple layman terms to enable comprehension by the target population. It specifies the recommended dietary intake across the life course and promotes healthy food choices as part of a healthier lifestyle and as a means of alleviating the risk of diet-related diseases.

The guidelines themselves aim to guide the population to attain a nutritionally adequate diet specific for them. These guidelines have been developed in conjunction with the existing realities of the country from an economic, social, environmental and agricultural perspective. They are also in line with relevant and existing strategies, plans and policies that are nutrition specific and nutrition sensitive.

The Seychelles Dietary Guidelines of 2007 was the first formal guidelines depicting how the Seychellois should be eating for good health. These guidelines however are in need of revision to address the evolving health landscape and current public health burden. The main burden in Seychelles over the last few decades is over-nutrition and its related co-morbidities, mainly non communicable diseases (NCDs) and resulting from the social and economic transition.

Seychelles is ranked as a high income country, with a GDP per capita of USD 15,410 in 2016 (World Bank, 2017). The increased wealth has unfortunately had a remarkably negative impact on the dietary patterns and lifestyle choices of the population. Seychelles has the highest rate of overweight and obesity in Africa.

57% of men and 72% of women were either overweight or obese in 2013 (Bovet, Viswanathan, Louange, & Gedeon, 2015). Annual school survey show continued increase in the rates of overweight and obesity among school-aged children. In 2016, 20% of boys and 24% of girls were overweight whilst nearly 8.8% of boys and 8.7% of girls were obese (Mangroo, Viswanathan, Marie, & Bovet, 2016).

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as diabetes, heart disease, cancer and renal failure have been identified as the main causes of death, illness and disability among adults (Bovet, Viswanathan, Louange, & Gedeon, 2015). In children, overweight and obesity have also been associated with high blood pressure and other detrimental cardio-metabolic conditions (Mangroo, Viswanathan, Marie, & Bovet, 2016). Modifiable risk behaviours such as insufficient physical activity and unhealthy dietary choices are largely responsible for these growing public health issues (Bovet, Viswanathan, Louange, & Gedeon, 2015).

In crafting the new guidelines for Seychelles consideration have also been given to the nutritional transition. The shift from predominantly unprocessed traditional foods including but not limited to fish, local fruit and vegetables, tubers, roots and breadfruit, to a more westernised dietary intake consisting mainly of refined and processed foods has contributed largely to the increase in obesity and its complications such as non-communicable diseases (NCDs) (Ministry of Health, 2016).

Increased urbanisation and wealth has led to a boom in private car ownership, improvement in public transport facilities adding to the already sedentary lifestyle to which many people are accustomed to. In children and adolescents increased screen time and reduced access and opportunities for play and exercise further exacerbate the low level of physical activity amongst these population groups (Mangroo, Viswanathan, Marie, & Bovet, 2016).

Seychelles is also highly reliant on food imports with 80% of food coming from external markets. Local production is insufficient to supply the whole population due to the high cost of production, limited land for agricultural activities due to competition with other sectors, inappropriate allocation of land, limited resources, climate susceptibilities, amongst other factors (Government of Republic of Seychelles, 2013). To meet the Global Nutrition target of zero per cent increase in obesity rate by 2025 measures must be taken to ensure the population are guided on how to prevent excessive weight gain, or to lose weight and avoid chronic diseases (International Food Research Institute, 2016).

Reducing the risk or better management of obesity and NCDs can be achieved by simple lifestyle changes. This includes eating more healthily, through the inclusion of more local foods and reduction of processed foods and reducing time spent sitting and increasing physical activity in all age groups (Ministry of Health, 2016).

Tackling the issue of over-nutrition remains one of the top priorities of the Ministry of Health. The new edition of the Dietary Guidelines not only aims to provide ‘hands on’, practical guidance on how to address some of the root causes of obesity and its comorbidities but also how to prevent it in the first place. A series of consultations with professionals and the public has contributed to the creation of the current guidelines. With its ‘health in all policy’ at the forefront, the Ministry of Health adds these guidelines as a tool to reinforce the need for multi-sectorial collaborations in striving to attain better health for all.

## Introduction

The current food based dietary guidelines are a series of evidence-based statements aimed at promoting the consumption and enjoyment of healthy, local foods by the Seychellois population. The expected end result is improved health for all groups in the population today and for generations to come. The new guidelines now replace the ones published in 2007 which was designed for all those above the age of two years. The new guidelines are separated into three groups: infants and young children (below five years); pregnant and lactating women and all those above five years.

While this document is written for professionals and educators, the target group for these guidelines is the general population, hence the messages have been tailor-made to capture everyone. The use of simpler communication materials, including booklets and posters, and also translation into Creole and French, will ensure accessibility and use by all. These materials will be made available in printed copies and distributed in schools, health centres and the hospitals. They will also be available online via the Ministry of Health Website and the Ministry of Health Facebook page.

The summary guidelines will be easily accessible in all Ministry of Health facilities on Mahe and the inner islands as printed copies. They will also be made available to educators in other settings such as in schools, work places, sports facilities and wherever health is promoted. It is anticipated that all households will become familiar with the guidelines via the communication tools developed and start to put into practice the recommendations for all family members.

These guidelines do not specifically address the particular needs of persons with specific disease conditions. The advice and assistance of relevant health professionals should be sought and followed as part of the management of the disease condition.

**The overarching principles of these guidelines:**

1. Encourage local and minimally processed foods
2. Promotion of a predominantly plant-based diet
3. Establish healthy eating habits by understanding the social, economic, psychological and environmental factors that help to shape it.
4. Overcome barriers that prevents healthy dietary patterns (cooking skills, social events, advertising, cost)
5. Creation of meals from food ingredients portraying local culinary practices

The guidelines presented are as follows:

1. Infants and young children (0 – 5 years)
2. Population > 5 years
3. Pregnant and lactating women

# Section 1: Guidelines for the general population above 5 years

### Enjoy a variety of foods from different food groups every day

No single food contains all the essential nutrients that the body needs to be healthy and function efficiently. The nutritional status of a person depends on the overall balance of foods that is eaten over a period of time, as well as on the needs of the individual. It is after all only through the consumption of a variety of different foods that the nutrient and energy needs of the body will be met. For example, milk contains sufficient amount of calcium and protein but are comparatively deficient in iron and vitamin C, whereas oranges are relatively rich in vitamin C but deficient in calcium.

That is why a balanced diet should be one that includes a variety of foods, so that adequate intake of all nutrients are achieved and dietary diversity attained. On the contrary a diet with limited variety and which does not include all food groups is more likely to be deficient in one or multiple nutrients placing the individual at risk of malnutrition and possibly food insecurity.

A varied and balanced diet that is encouraged for the Seychellois population comprises of foods from all the food groups as illustrated in the Seychelles Food Guide (SFG) (refer to section 4). The SFG depicts six food groups in the proportion that they should be eaten as part of a balanced diet. Over half of the plate is plant-based foods which should form the basis of what we eat every day. This includes fruits, vegetables, grains, tubers, breadfruit ad plantains. Milk and milk products as well as fish, meat, eggs and plant-based alternatives should be consumed in moderation. Finally, foods that are rich in unsaturated fats should also be included in smaller amounts as they are essential for various functions in the body. Although foods that are high in sugar can be consumed in smaller amounts, this has not been included as part of a food group as there is unequivocal evidence about the negative impacts of excessive sugar consumption in relation to obesity and its complications.

One of the main barriers to eating a varied and nutrient-dense diet is the perceived cost associated with it. Although there have been no nutritional surveys conducted in Seychelles, it is apparent from the high rates of overweight and obesity in the population that the diet of most people is predominantly energy-dense. This guideline therefore highlights the importance of incorporating a variety of foods in meal preparation in line with personal preferences, inclusion of local dishes, family meals and also factor in affordability to ensure that nutrient needs are being met and not compromised.

### Eat plenty of fruits, vegetables and pulses (beans, lentils and peas) of different colours every day

Eating plenty of fruits, vegetables and pulses has been scientifically proven to reduce the risks of many non-communicable diseases (NCDs) including type 2 diabetes, hypertension and cancer. Such benefits arise from the fact that fruits, vegetables and pulses are packed with numerous phytonutrients, vitamins (e.g. A, C and E) and minerals (e.g. selenium) which act as antioxidants removing dangerous free radicals in the body. Fruits, vegetables and pulses are very rich in dietary fibre. Different types of fibre including insoluble and soluble fibre as well as resistant starch are thought to be abundant in pulses, and found in varying amounts in different fruits and vegetables. Fibre is essential for a healthy diet and prevention of diseases including cancer, heart diseases and intestinal problems like constipation.
Pulses include lentils, kidney beans, soya beans, black-eyed beans, peas, chick peas, mung beans, broad beans, butter beans, and cowpeas. All pulses except for soya beans are very similar in nutritional content and are rich in carbohydrate, protein and fibre and low in fat. Pulses are also a good source of iron and can therefore help prevent iron deficiency anaemia especially when combined with foods or drinks containing vitamin C which helps improve iron absorption. The nutritional quality of soya beans is superior to that of other pulses as it contains more protein and it is an extremely good source of iron and calcium.

Most fruits and vegetables are naturally low in fat and contain no cholesterol. Due to their low calorie content and their filling effect, fruits and vegetables are very beneficial for people who want to lose and maintain weight. The World Health Organisation (WHO) and Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) recommends that everyone should consume a minimum amount of 400 grams of fruits and non-starchy vegetables a day (excluding potatoes and other starchy tubers). This translates into five portions a day (80 grams per portion).

**What is a portion?**

A portion of fruits, vegetables or pulses is equivalent to 80g and can include the following examples:

* 1 medium fruit e.g. banana, orange, star fruit
* 1 slice of a large fruit e.g. pawpaw, pineapple, melon
* 2-3 small fruits e.g. grapes, guavas, plums
* 2-3 tablespoons of fresh food salad
* 2 tablespoons of raw, cooked, frozen or canned vegetables
* Half a cup or 2-3 tablespoons of cooked beans, peas and lentils
* 1 cup of green leafy vegetables e.g. moringa soup (‘bouyon bred”)

*("A child’s portion of fruit and vegetables is smaller than that of an adult, and is equivalent to the amount the child can hold in one hand).

It is established that local fruits’ and vegetables’ supply does not contain excessive pesticide residues and that the benefits of eating fresh produce in terms of superior nutritional content far exceeds any health risks that any residues may pose. However, as a precaution, everyone should ensure that all fruits and vegetables are washed well with water to remove any residues that may have remained on the outer surfaces of such foods.

### Choose more whole grains, local tubers and other high-fibre starchy foods instead of rice

Carbohydrates are the main source of energy for our body. There are three main classes of carbohydrates: sugars, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides. Polysaccharides includes starches and non-starch polysaccharide (a component of fibre). A major part of the energy that we need for our bodily functions should come principally from starches.

**Whole grains – the best source of starchy foods**

The best carbohydrate source consists of unrefined or minimally processed foods commonly known as ‘whole grains’. Grains are the seeds of cereal plants (wheat, rice, corn etc.) that consist of an outer fibre rich layer (bran), a nutrient-packed inner area (germ) and a central starchy part (endosperm). During the processing or milling of cereal grains, the brans and the germ are often removed to give a more refined grain. Whole grains on the other hand contains all three layers of the grain and therefore have over 75% more nutrients than their refined counterparts. The outer bran and germ layers of the seed, are rich in fibre, B-vitamins, zinc, phosphorous, selenium, iron and vitamin E.

There are a lot of health benefits for consuming whole grains and other starchy foods rich in fibre, vitamins and minerals. The risk of numerous NCDs including heart diseases and cancer are thought to be lowered in people who regularly eat whole grains as part of a healthy diet. The benefit seems to be higher in those people who also consume plenty of fruits and vegetables. Since whole grains are high in fibre, they can be beneficial in keeping the digestive tract healthy and help reduce intestinal problems. Therefore, when choosing foods from the grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains group, it is advisable to replace refined cereal-based foods with whole grain varieties. For example, instead of having white bread choose wholegrain bread or cassava cake or use whole wheat flour instead of white flour. In Seychelles white rice is the main staple and it is often consumed in large quantities despite its low nutritive value. White rice should be replaced with starchy roots and tubers such as cassava, sweet potato, yam, and taro which contains more fibre, vitamins and minerals and are therefore nutritionally superior. Excessive consumption of starchy foods will lead to an excessive calorie intake and can therefore cause weight gain over time.

**Portion size variation:**

Energy intake is normally dependent on activity levels and therefore a person with a higher activity level such as an athlete or a construction worker would need to consume larger portions of starchy foods to obtain the energy required. Someone who leads a sedentary lifestyle must consume a much smaller portion as excess energy is stored in the body as fat. Around 6-11 portions from the grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains group is recommended daily. A portion of food from this food group is equivalent to about 20g of carbohydrates with one portion being equivalent to:

* 1 thick slice (40g) of bread
* 1 small bowl (40g) of wholegrain breakfast cereal (*may vary for different cereals)*
* 3 tablespoons (4g) raw, rolled oats
* 2 tablespoons (60g) of cooked rice
* 4 tablespoons/ ½ cup (75g) of cooked pasta
* 1 medium sized (70g) boiled plantain (‘bannann senzak’)
* 1 medium sized (100g) boiled potato/ sweet potato/ taro/ yam
* 1 piece (70g) of boiled cassava
* 1 large piece (140g) of breadfruit
* 1 cassava cake (‘galet’)
* 1 medium local rusk (‘gro biskwi’/ ‘biskwi bourbon’)

### Include more fish, including oily fish like fresh tuna, bonito and mackerel instead of fatty and processed meats

Recently the consumption of meat over fish has become more popular in the Seychellois diet. This has come about with the relatively lower prices of meat and its ease of access. However, such a trend is already negatively impacting on our traditional diet and potentially on our populations’ health. Meat contains a higher proportion of saturated fats as well as much more cholesterol than fish. High intake of saturated fat and cholesterol increases the risk of non-communicable diseases such as cancer and cardiovascular disease (Daniel et al., 2011).
Recently, the World Health Organization based on findings of studies carried out declared that eating processed meat is linked to certain forms of cancer, putting it in the same category as smoking. The cancer risk is increased especially with the consumption of red meat which includes beef, pork, lamb, mutton and goat (WHO, 2015). Therefore, adopting a diet containing the least possible amount of meat and processed meat products is ideal. Fish and pulses such as beans, peas, lentils and others are excellent substitutes for meat and processed meat.

In comparison to meat, fish are rich in high quality proteins, have a high proportion of healthy fats (unsaturated fats) namely omega-3 and contains an abundance of vitamins and minerals. Omega-3 is mostly found in oily fish such as fresh tuna, bonito, trevally, swordfish and mackerel and is linked with a lower risk of heart disease and stroke (Bowen, Harris & Kris-Etherton, 2016). It is recommended to eat fish on at least five days a week with oily fish being included at least twice weekly. Fish is best eaten grilled, baked, steamed or made into chutney rather than fried or other methods of cooking that requires adding additional oil.

The Seychelles Heart study carried out in 2013 amongst those aged 25 to 64 years revealed that approximately 26% of the study group comprising of 1240 participants ate fish every day and about 50% ate fish 5 to 6 days in a week (Bovet, Viswanathan, Louange, & Gedeon, 2015). Comparatively, about 75% of the population are eating meat and about 50% are eating processed meat once or twice weekly. There is however no information on how the fish was prepared which is important as frying is a common culinary practice in Seychelles and there is also no data for the younger age group. It is recommended that a population-based survey is carried out which includes other age groups and nutrition-specific variables.

### Include milk & milk products every day

Milk and milk products form part of an essential food group that should be included in the diet every day. These products include different types of milk, yoghurt and cheese. Research indicates that these products provide nine essential nutrients including protein, calcium, phosphorous, potassium, vitamins A, D, B1, B2 and B12.

Milk and milk products provide a readily absorbable and convenient source of calcium required for the normal development and maintenance of the skeleton. Calcium is stored in the teeth and bones, where it provides structure and strength. Low intakes of calcium have been associated with osteoporosis, which often results in bone fracture. The table below indicates the recommendations for calcium by age group.

Calcium is found in many foods, although its contents and bioavailability is much higher in milk and milk products. Other foods with moderate amounts of calcium include fish (with soft, edible bones), green leafy vegetables, pulses, almond with skin on, fortified products such as plant-based drinks like fortified soya milk or almond milk, fortified breakfast cereals.

#### Milk intolerance

There are some people who find it hard to digest milk and milk products because they are intolerant to lactose (milk sugar). For these people, milk and milk products do not need to be totally excluded in their diets. Research has identified simple strategies to make milk and milk products easier to digest-such as drinking lactose –reduced or lactose-free milk, eating yoghurt, or consuming hard cheeses such as Cheddar and Swiss that are naturally low in lactose. Starting with small portions and gradually working up on the portion size is also a useful way to include milk products in the diet.

Calcium supplements although not substitutes for milk and milk products can still help to meet your body’s calcium requirement. However, a calcium tablet should not take the place of calcium in food as you will be missing out on other nutrients that milk provides, such as vitamin D which helps the body absorb and deposit calcium in the bones. Furthermore, a supplement can never make up for an inadequate diet and therefore a food-based approach to achieving your dietary requirements is the best option in consuming a well-balanced diet.

### Choose water as your preferred drink to have every day instead of sugar-sweetened beverages like fizzy drinks, juices and cordials

Although water is not considered a nutrient, it is a fundamental part of our lives and is ranked second only to oxygen as essential for life. We can exist without food for two months or more, but we can only survive for a few days without water. Two thirds of our body weight is water with the average adult body being 55 to 75 % water. Some of the functions of water includes digestion and absorption of food, regulation of body temperature and blood circulation, maintenance of normal bowel functions, transport of nutrients and oxygen to cells, and removal of toxins and other wastes from the body. Water contains minute amounts of minerals and has no caloric value, making it the best drink for those seeking to lose weight. More information about sugar-sweetened beverages and its implications is found in Appendix 6.

#### How does our body lose water?

On average, a healthy individual loses approximately 1.5 to 2.5 litres of water every day. Water is lost from the body at a very rapid pace as urine, and to a lesser extent via the skin as perspiration, although still considerably high as we live in a hot climate. The greater the body weight, activity level or temperature, the more water a person will lose. Small amounts are lost in faeces and from the lungs during perspiration. Water which is lost from the body must be constantly replaced in order to prevent dehydration.

#### The thirst signal

Contrary to our drive to eat, our drive to drink is not as keen. Our thirst mechanism kicks in when we are already mildly dehydrated, in other words, thirst is a late signal. The situation gets worse with age and as you get older your thirst mechanism is affected. The body usually needs water long before a person feels thirsty. That is why it is important to get into the routine of drinking water and to drink water before thirst is noticed.
# Dehydration

Most people do not drink enough water and the body responds to this water deficiency (dehydration) in a variety of ways, which we frequently see as symptoms such as: increased thirst, dry mouth, sticky saliva, light headedness, headache, dizziness, confusion, reduced urine output and dark yellow urine. One of the best ways to recognize dehydration is to pay close attention to the colour of one’s urine. Ideally, light yellow to clear urine indicates an adequate water intake. Dehydration is very dangerous for babies, small children and older adults.

## Water Recommendation

An average adolescent and adult require a minimum of 2.5 litres of fluids every day, out of which 1.5 litres must be met by the intakes of plain drinking water only. The exact water requirement will vary based on gender, age and body weight.

## Specific population groups

Pregnant women require additional fluid replacement to ensure that foetal needs are met, as well as providing for expanding extra-cellular space and amniotic fluid. Lactating women have additional water requirements, leading to an additional requirement of 1 litre per day for the first six months of lactation (total of 3 litres/ day). The elderly may not require additional volumes of water, but are at greater risk of dehydration due to decreased thirst sensations. Other susceptible individuals include those who sweat a lot e.g. those working in direct heat and athletes; smokers; people consuming alcohol; travellers on aeroplanes and those with severe vomiting and diarrhoea.

## Reduce salt and salty foods such as sauces, canned foods and processed meat

‘Salt’ is a common name for Sodium Chloride (NaCl) which is made up of 40% sodium (Na) and 60% chloride (Cl). Salt plays an important role in regulating fluid balance, maintaining normal blood pressure, allowing the muscles to contract and enabling nerve transmission. Diets rarely lack in salt, as foods usually supply more salt than the body needs. Salt is as a seasoning agent and is added while cooking the food, but importantly salt is naturally found in many foods. Since salt has the ability to draw moisture out of the food, it is used as a preservative as well. The amount of salt being consumed among Seychellois population is high and this is clearly shown by our high prevalence of hypertension and cardiovascular diseases as excessive salt intake has a tendency to raise blood pressure levels above normal values.

### How much salt should we consume?

As per WHO recommendation the daily intake of salt in adults should be ≤ 5g per day (i.e. ≤ 2g of sodium per day). In children the recommendation is ≤ 2g per day (i.e. about 0.8g of sodium per day). Among children the adjustment of recommended sodium intake based on energy requirements is recommended because of the positive association between energy requirements and sodium intake (World Health Organisation, Guideline: Sodium intake for adults and children, 2012). If children have too much salt, this could affect their health in future. Since the taste is acquired at an early age, it could make them develop a liking for salty foods, which means they will be more likely to continue taking excessive amounts of salt when they grow up. It is important to note that infants will get the right amount of salt through breast milk or infant formula. Thus, there is no need to add salt to their foods. Processed foods that are not made specifically for babies should not be given as they are high in salt. Although our hot climate encourages sweating, there is no need to consume more salt as we only lose a small amount through sweat. However it is important to drink plenty of water.

### Salt content in foods

Almost 75% of the salt we eat is already in everyday foods, with most of the salt coming from processed food. Even people who make their own meals from scratch will usually consume high salt foods such as bread and breakfast cereals might not necessarily taste salty. In order to know how much salt you are consuming, you need to know the rich sources of salt in the diet. A good way to do this is to read the food label. If salt is listed amongst the first ingredients this means the product is high in salt, since ingredients are listed from highest to lowest concentration in a product.

## Ways to add more flavour without using much salt

* Choose fresh foods rather processed foods.
* Gradually reduce the amount of salt you add to food. Your taste buds will quickly adjust.
* Add herbs, spices and other ingredients such as thyme, parsley, rosemary, lemongrass, cinnamon leaves/ bark, bilimbi, curry leaves, lime leaves, turmeric, Indian spices, lemon or lime juice, ginger, garlic, chili, onions and spring onions to dishes to enhance their flavours.
* Marinate fish and meat in advance to give them more flavour.
* Use cooking sauces like soya sauce, oyster sauce, tomato sauce etc. sparingly. As much as possible, try not to add extra salt if the food is being cooked in these.
* Make your own stock and gravy using vegetables, fish and meat instead of using cubes or granules.
* Always taste your food before adding salt. Salt should be the last ingredient added to foods because cooking brings out a lot of flavours in foods
* Do not add extra salt at the table.

### Remain physically active on most days of the week

Physical activity or exercise can improve your health and reduce the risk of developing several diseases like type 2 diabetes, cancer, respiratory diseases, gall stones and cardiovascular diseases. Physical activity and exercise can have immediate and long-term health benefits. With all the risks associated with obesity and carrying on too much fat, it can be of great benefit to your health to maintain a healthy weight for your height and stay within reasonable body weight ranges. Regular physical activity is helpful in promoting strong bones, muscles and joints and can significantly improve fitness and endurance.

**Figure 2: Calculating a reasonable body weight range**

Body Mass Index (BMI) is one of the methods used to assess whether a person is of reasonable boy weight. But BMI only gives the idea in totality not specifies the amount of fat or bone or muscle mass percentage.

**Formula for calculating BMI:**

Weight in (Kg) ÷ Height in (M) x Height in (M)

It is weight in kg divided by Height in meter square.

**For assessing abdominal obesity** single waist measurement can be done which is simple and easy. For an average man, a waist measurement greater than 94cm and above poses a health risk whilst for an average woman a waist measurement greater than 80cm poses a health risk.

Despite the proven benefits of physical activity, very few Seychellois adults engage in it. People are adopting sedentary lifestyle which is resulting in the increase of Obesity in Seychelles. A lack of physical activity is also prominent among children and adolescents which is alarming as the prevalence of obesity is going up every year.

The key recommendation for adults is to engage in moderate physical activity for at least ‘**30 minutes per day**’. It is important to note that 30 minutes of activity per day may be insufficient to prevent unhealthy weight gain in some people. For those people who are controlling their calorie intake and exercising for only 30 minutes additional exercise for up to ‘**60 minutes per day**’ is recommended.

### If you choose to drink alcohol do so within the recommended limits

In Seychelles, over the years there has been an increase in the proportions of adults aged 25-64 (both men and women) practicing moderate drinking (1-2 drinks per day) and marked drinking (3-5 drinks per day). In addition, the 2013 study revealed that high proportions of these adults drink on weekends, with 20% of all adults drinking more than 10 drinks on special occasions, showing high frequency of binge drinking patterns (Bovet, Viswanathan, Louange, & Gedeon, 2015). Binge drinking has been shown to be more harmful to health, thank drinking a few standard drinks throughout the week.

The only organ that can break down alcohol in the body is the liver, and it takes about an hour to break down one unit (10ml/volume or 8g/weight) of alcohol to produce energy. However, alcohol is a concentrated form of energy with one gram of alcohol providing seven calories. Therefore, drinking alcohol adds more energy to the diet than the body requires, and any surplus energy that the body does not need is converted into fat leading to weight gain. In addition, when consumption of alcohol is more than the body can handle the excess alcohol travels in the blood until it can be broken down. The alcohol circulating in the blood puts a strain on organs in the body including the brain resulting in a slowdown of bodily functions.

It is well known that the harmful use of alcohol is a causal factor for a wide range of diseases/ conditions such as cancers of the upper gastrointestinal tract, cardiovascular diseases, damage to the nervous system (brain), high blood pressure, gastritis, ulcers, gout, liver diseases, an increased risk of becoming overweight as well as injuries as a result of violence and road accidents (Marshall, 2014). Apart from health consequences, alcohol consumption also brings about significant social and economic losses to individuals and society at large. For that reason, it is recommended for someone who does not drink alcohol not to start at all. Those who choose to drink should do so sensibly and remain within the set limits.

**What are the set limits on drinking alcohol?**

* Men and women should not drink more than 2 units of alcohol a day and no more than 14 units each week
* Men and women who drinks should try to abstain from having any alcohol at least 3 days per week
* Pregnant women should not drink alcohol at all
* Alcohol passes readily in breast milk, therefore breastfeeding mothers should be cautious about having alcohol as it can have an adverse effect on the baby’s health.

**What is one unit of alcohol?**

A unit is 10ml by volume or 8g by weight of pure alcohol.

# Section 2: Guidelines for Infants and young children (0 – 5 years)

## 1. Give your child only breast milk for the first 6 months of life

Adequate nutrition during infancy and early childhood is essential to ensure the growth, health, and development of children to their full potential. Poor nutrition increases the risk of illness and death in children less than 5 years of age. Inappropriate nutrition can also lead to childhood obesity which is an increasing public health problem in many countries, including Seychelles. The first two years of life provide a critical window of opportunity for ensuring children’s appropriate growth and development through optimal feeding which helps to lower morbidity and mortality, reduces the risk of chronic disease, and fosters better development overall.

WHO and UNICEF’s global recommendations for optimal infant feeding as set out in the Global Strategy for infant and young child feeding (2002) is as follows:

* Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life (26 weeks or 180 days)

***Exclusive breastfeeding*** means that an infant receives **only** breast milk from his or her mother or expressed breast milk and no other liquids or solids, not even water, with the **exception** of oral rehydration solution, drops or syrups consisting of vitamins, minerals supplements or medicines. Most exclusively breastfed young infants feed at least eight to twelve times in 24 hours, including night feeds. Exclusive breastfeeding for 6 months has many benefits for the infant and mother.

### Benefits of Exclusive Breastfeeding for the infant

* **Provides ideal nutrition for optimum growth** - Breast milk is the most complete of all foods and it contains all the nutrients (protein, carbohydrate, fat, vitamins and minerals) and water in optimal quantity that an infant need for growth and development in the first six months of life.
* **Is the baby’s first immunization** - Early initiation of breastfeeding, within 1 hour of birth, protects the new born from acquiring infections and reduces new born mortality. The risk of mortality due to diarrhoea and other infections can increase in infants who are either partially breastfed or not breastfed at all.
* **Provides adequate water for hydration** - during illness helps keep baby well-hydrated.
* **Protects against infection and allergies** - breast milk contains many important growth factors and antibodies that will help an infant fight off diseases like pneumonia, diarrhoea, allergy and promote resistance against numerous infections like ear infections.
* **Promotes bonding and development.**
* **Increases mental development** - the unique combination of fatty acids in breast milk contributes to optimal brain development
* **Digests easily, efficiently used and does not cause constipation.**
* **Promotes proper jaw, teeth and speech development** - The action of breastfeeding helps the child’s jaw to develop as well as muscles such as the tongue. This development: reduces the incidence of ear infections; assists with clear speech; protects against dental caries and reduces risk of orthodontic problems.
* **Breast-milk is also an important source of energy and nutrients in children aged 6–23 months.** It can provide half or more of a child’s energy needs between the ages of 6 and 12 months, and one third of energy needs between 12 and 24 months. Breast-milk is also a critical source of energy and nutrients during illness.

### Benefits of Breastfeeding for the mother

* Reduces excessive blood loss after birth (immediate breastfeeding)
* Is readily available, needing no preparation
* Saves time and money
* Makes night feeding easier
* Delays return of fertility - Mothers who breastfeed exclusively and frequently have less than a 2% risk of becoming pregnant in the first 6 months postpartum, provided that they still have amenorrhoea.
* Reduces the risk of breast and ovarian cancer
* Promotes weight loss which accelerates recovery of pre-pregnancy weight
* **Promotes bonding** - Breastfeeding provides warmth, closeness and contact, which can help physical and emotional development of the child.

**The dangers from the use of breast-milk substitutes include:**

* Infant formula may be contaminated through manufacturing error.
* Powdered infant formula is not sterile and during manufacture may be contaminated with bacteria such as *Enterobacter sakazakii* and *Salmonella enterica*, which has been associated with serious illness and death in infants.
* Infant formula may contain unsafe ingredients or may lack vital ingredients.
* Water used for washing bottles or mixing infant formula may be contaminated.
* Errors in mixing formula, over concentration or under concentration, may cause infant illness.
* Families may dilute the formula to make it last longer.
* Formula may be given to settle a crying baby which can lead to overweight and food being seen as the solution to unhappiness.
* Water and teas may be given instead of breast milk or formula resulting in less milk consumed overall and low weight gain.
* Purchase of infant formula creates unnecessary expenses for the family and means less food for other members.
* Frequent pregnancies may burden the family and society.
* Hospital costs are higher for staff and supplies to treat health problems.
* Some of the risks from using breast-milk substitutes can be reduced by attention to the process of using breast-milk substitutes - the preparation and hygiene elements. However, the differences in the constituents of breast milk and formula still remain.

### 2. Introduce, safe and nutrient-rich foods & plain water to complement breastfeeding from the age of 6 months

***Complementary feeding*** is the process starting when breast milk is no longer sufficient to meet the nutritional requirements of infants, and therefore other foods and liquids are needed, along with breast milk. The target range for complementary feeding is generally taken to be 6 to 23 months of age, even though breastfeeding may continue beyond two years.

After six months of age, the baby needs other foods (complementary foods) while continuing to receive sufficient breast milk. This is called *complementary feeding* because it complements the breastfeeding; it does NOT replace breast milk. 6 months is the recommended appropriate age at which to introduce complementary foods. During the period of complementary feeding, children are at high risk of under nutrition. Complementary foods are often of inadequate nutritional quality, or they are given too early or too late, in too small amounts, or not frequently enough.

Appropriate complementary feeding promotes growth and prevents stunting among children 6-24 months old. Stunting (when children are short for age), which shows that children are malnourished, is permanent and affects intelligence. Rates of malnutrition usually pick during this time, with lifelong consequences.

Appropriate complementary feeding involves continued breastfeeding and giving the right amount of good quality foods. Babies 6-12 months old are especially vulnerable, because they are just learning to eat. Babies this age must be fed soft foods frequently and patiently. Weight gain is a sign of good health and nutrition. It is important to continue to take children to the health facility for regular check-ups, immunizations and to monitor growth and development.

**Guiding principles for complementary feeding of the breastfed child**

1. *Practice exclusive breastfeeding from birth to 6 months of age and introduce complementary foods at 6 months of age (180 days) while continuing to breastfeed* - According to the WHO growth standards, children who are exclusively breastfed have a more rapid growth in the first 6 months of life than other infants. By the age of 6 months, a baby has usually at least doubled his or her birth weight, and is becoming more active. Exclusive breastfeeding is no longer sufficient to meet all energy and nutrient needs by itself, and complementary foods should be introduced to make up the difference. At about 6 months of age, an infant is also developmentally ready for other foods. The digestive system is mature enough to digest the starch, protein and fat in a non-milk diet.
2. *Continue frequent on-demand breastfeeding until 2 years of age or beyond* - Breastfeeding should continue with complementary feeding up to 2 years of age or beyond, and it should be on demand, as often as the child wants. Breast milk can provide one half or more of a child’s energy needs between 6 and 12 months of age, and one third of energy needs and other high quality nutrients between 12 and 24 months. Breast milk continues to provide higher quality nutrients than complementary foods, and also protective factors. Breast milk is a critical source of energy and nutrients during illness, and reduces mortality among children who are malnourished. Breastfeeding reduces the risk of acute and chronic diseases.
3. *Practice good hygiene and proper food handling*

***Five keys to safer food***

* Keep clean
* Separate raw and cooked
* Cook thoroughly
* Keep food at safe temperatures
* Use safe water and raw materials

4. *Start at 6 months of age with small amounts of food and increase the quantity as the child gets older, while maintaining frequent breastfeeding* - The amount of food required to cover the gap increases as the child gets older, and as the intake of breast milk decreases. The quantity increases gradually month by month, as the child grows and develops.
5. *Increase the number of times that the child is fed complementary foods as the child gets older* - The number of times that the child is fed complementary foods will increase as the child gets older. The transition from smaller to larger meals, will happen gradually between those ages, depending on the child’s appetite and how he or she is developing. If a child eats too few meals, then he or she will not receive enough food to cover energy needs and may start to lose weight.

### From 6 months, other than breast milk, give your child water instead of sugar-sweetened beverages like fizzy drinks, juices and cordials

It is recommended that children over 12 months have 6 to 8 drinks per day from a beaker or cup, although more may be needed in very hot weather or when children are active. A drink for a child of this age range (12 – 48 months) will be 100 to 150 ml. An adequate fluid intake will prevent dehydration and help reduce the risk of constipation. Water is the best drink to give young children between meals and snacks other than milk as they are less likely to harm the child’s teeth when given from a cup or beaker.

Sugar and sugary drinks, such as lemonade/soft drinks, juices and cordials should be avoided because they decrease the child’s appetite for more nutritious foods. Sugar can damage children’s teeth causing dental caries and lead to obesity and its complications over time. Over the age of six months, water should be provided to infants. Tap water should be boiled and cooled before drinking below 12 months.

If sugar-sweetened beverages are given to young children they should be diluted and consumed with rather than between meals to help lessen the risk of dental caries. It is also important that such drinks are not given from a bottle but rather from a cup or beaker.

Tea, herbal infusions and coffee contain compounds that can interfere with iron absorption and are not recommended for young children below five years. The best beverage to give your child is breast milk, water and above 2 years of age, cow’s milk or fortified non-dairy alternatives e.g. Soy milk.

### Allow your child to touch, smell, taste and enjoy a variety of foods from different food groups to ensure they get all they need to sustain their growth

Including a variety of foods to children will ensure that they get all essential nutrients needed for their growth and development. Optimal complementary feeding depends not only on what is fed but also on how, when, where and by whom a child is fed.

**Responsive feeding involves:**

* Feeding infants directly and assist older children when they feed themselves
* Feeding slowly and patiently, and encouraging children to eat, but not forcing them.
* If children refuse many foods, experiment with different food combinations, tastes, textures and methods of encouragement such as eating with them, making positive comments about the food, etc.
* Minimize distractions during meals if the child loses interest easily.
* Remember that feeding times are periods of learning and love – talk to children during feeding, with eye-to-eye contact.

Gradually increase food consistency and variety as the infant grows older, adapting to the infant’s requirements and abilities. The most suitable consistency for an infant or young child’s food depends on age and neuromuscular development. Beginning at 6 months, an infant can eat pureed, mashed or semi-solid foods. By 8 months most infants can also eat finger foods. By 12 months, most children can eat the same types of foods as consumed by the rest of the family. If delayed beyond ten months of age, it may increase the risk of feeding difficulties later on and predispose to weight loss and malnutrition. Although it may save time to continue feeding semi-solid foods, for optimal child development it is important to gradually increase the solidity of food with age. 

The base of complementary foods should ideally include the local staple. Staples are cereals and tubers and in the context of Seychelles, options such as sweet potatoes, cassava and starchy fruits such as plantain and breadfruit that consist of not only carbohydrate but also fibre are encouraged. A variety of other foods should be added to the staple every day to provide other nutrients. These come from different food groups:

### Give young children whole, fresh and local foods instead of processed foods

Whole, fresh foods are encouraged because they are richer in nutrients in comparison to processed and refined foods. Whole, fresh foods include all foods that are as close to nature as possible, for example fruits, vegetables, tubers, milk, fish, meat, eggs, etc.

A processed food is any food that has been altered in some way during preparation and also includes the addition of other ingredients such as preservatives, flavour, additives, salt, sugar, fats, etc. Ingredients such as salt, sugar and fat are sometimes added to processed foods to make their flavour more appealing and to extend their shelf life, or in some cases to contribute to the food's structure, such as salt in bread or sugar in cakes.

Not all processed foods are unhealthy or a bad choice as some of them need processing to make them safe such as milk which needs to be pasteurised to remove harmful bacteria whilst others need to be in a more edible form such as wheat which is transformed into products likes pasta, bread, wholegrain breakfast cereals, etc. However most processed foods contains high levels of salt, sugar and fat.

Examples of common processed foods that you should not provide to children:
* Tinned vegetables
* Sugary breakfast cereals e.g. coco pops, fruit loops
* Savoury snacks, such as crisps, sausage rolls, pies and pasties
* Meat products, such as bacon, sausage, ham, salami, luncheon meat, sausage, nuggets, burger, paté, etc.
* "Convenience foods", such as microwave meals or ready to eat meals like frozen pizza, frozen lasagne, etc.
* Cakes, pastries and biscuits
* Sugar-sweetened beverages e.g. juice, fizzy drinks, etc.

Buying processed foods for the family can lead to overconsumption of sugar, salt and fat especially amongst young children which can lead to several health complications such as obesity.

### Give young children Iron- and Vitamin A- rich foods such as iron-fortified cereals, fish, meat, eggs, green leafy/ orange/ red vegetables and pulses

From birth to the first six months, the child’s storage of iron in the body will start to decline. This is why it is very important to start to introduce iron-rich foods to children from six months alongside breastfeeding. Rich sources of iron include fish, meat, pulses and green leafy vegetables. Iron from animal sources or haem iron is more readily absorbed by the body and should be included as early as possible when complementary feeding starts. Non-haem iron sources like beans, lentils, peas, green leaves like moringa and spinach are not as readily absorbed by the body and need to be combined with a rich source of vitamin C to improve their absorption. Examples of vitamin C sources include fruits and vegetables like tomato, orange, etc. Certain food sources like milk and milk products that are rich in calcium and tea which contains tannin hinders iron absorption and should not be given at the same time with iron-rich food sources. It is important to prevent iron deficiency anaemia in children as it can delay or permanently impair mental and motor development. Iron deficiency anaemia in children is associated with frequent infections, poor weight gain, developmental delay and behavioural disorders. Early diagnosis of iron deficiency anaemia is crucial below 36 months to prevent irreversible neurological damage.

Iron deficiency in young children is usually of dietary origin due to:

* The early introduction of cow’s milk as a main drink before 12 months of age
* Over-dependence on milk where it replaces iron-rich or iron-enhancing foods
* Lack of iron-rich food sources being introduced as part of complementary feeding

Vitamin A is required for the normal development and growth of children. Vitamin A describes the activity of retinol and its derivatives (preformed vitamin A) and some of the carotenoids found in plant pigments which have provitamin A activity. Preformed vitamin A (as retinol or retinyl esters) is primarily found only in foods of animal origin. The most concentrated sources are:

* Liver and liver products
* Milk and milk products
* Oily fish
* Egg yolk
* Fish liver oil
* Fortified products

Carotenoids are mainly found in orange and yellow-coloured vegetables and fruits. The most concentrated food sources of carotenoids are:

* Carrots
* Red peppers
* Dark green leafy vegetables
* Tomatoes
* Yellow fruits such as mango and papaya

To prevent vitamin A deficiency it is important to include rich sources of vitamin A in complementary foods given to children from six months. Dietary vitamin A deficiency is often associated with protein energy malnutrition, causing problems such as night blindness, growth retardation and increased susceptibility to infection.

### Above 12 months children can consume other types of milk other than breast milk

Alongside a varied and nutritious diet comprising of a diversity of foods to meet the energy and nutrient needs of the child, pasteurised full cream milk may be introduced to children over 12 months. Milk is an excellent source of several nutrients such as protein, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, and Vitamin D which are important for the mineralisation of bones and teeth.

Milk and yoghurt given to children under two years old should always be full-fat. Low-fat milks (semi-skimmed) should only be given to children over 2 years whilst non-fat (skimmed milk) should only be given to children aged above 5 years. Milk and other drinks should be offered in a cup rather than a feeding bottle to reduce the incidence of dental caries.

In the first 2 years of life, plant-based alternatives such as soy, almond and coconut milk as well as goat and sheep's milk should not be given to children as they are nutritionally incomplete milks and unsuitable alternatives to breast milk. Pasteurised full-cream milk, formula milk (including fortified soy formula where specifically indicated) may be used after one year.

Toddlers need less milk than in their first year of life. Excess intake may reduce appetite and also decrease their intake of other foods especially those higher in iron. Toddler follow-on formula or supplements are not required for healthy children with good appetite and dietary intake.
Serving size from this food group depends on the child’s appetite and growth. One standard serving is:

* About 120ml glass/ cup of milk
* About 120g of full-fat yoghurt
* About 25g of cheese

### 8. Children should remain physically active throughout the day

Physical activity is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure. Physical activity for children includes both structured activities and unstructured free play, and can be done indoors or out (Department of Health and Ageing, 2010).

Above 12 months of age children should engage in at least three hours of physical activity spread throughout the day. This can be in the form of active play outdoors e.g. playing tag, running, jumping, etc. which will develop their co-ordination and gross motor skills and allow them to progressively enjoy sports as they get older. For healthy development in infants, physical activity, particularly supervised floor-based play in safe environments should be encouraged from birth.

The benefits of physical activity for children include:

* Help achieve and maintain a healthy weight
* Build strong bones and muscles
* Improve balance, movement and co-ordination skills
* Promote social skills through interactions with people
* Support brain development
* Encourage self-confidence and independence

Use of electronic devices e.g. television viewing, playing video games, use of tablets, mobile phones, etc. is popular with kids but they usually involve sitting for long periods – time which could be spent playing active games or interacting with others. Children will benefit more from talking, singing, reading, listening to music and playing with family members or a friend. Regardless of how active kids may be at other times, it is still important to limit screen time.

Use of electronic devices has been associated with delays in language and cognitive development, and also attentional problems during childhood. There are proven benefits of interactions between children and their parents.

For children 2 to 5 years of age, sitting and watching television and the use of other electronic media (e.g. computer and other electronic games, etc.) should be limited to less than one hour per day. Children younger than 2 years of age should not spend any time watching television or using other electronic media (e.g. computer and other electronic games, etc.). It is very important to limit the screen time among children because ‘The International Classification of Diseases’ (ICD) and WHO has recently classified uncontrolled ‘Video-gaming’ or ‘digital gaming’ as a disease condition called ‘Gaming Disorder’ (World Health Organisation, What is gaming disorder?, 2018).

Infants, toddlers and pre-schoolers should not be sedentary, restrained, or kept inactive, for more than one hour at a time, with the exception of sleeping. ‘Sedentary behaviour’ refers to time spent being physically inactive. All children need some ‘down time’ but they are not naturally inactive for long periods of time. Sitting in strollers, highchairs and car seats (being restrained) for long periods is not good for children’s health and development and goes against their natural tendency to be active and play. The use of baby jumpers and baby walkers is also discouraged. The evidence shows they can restrict the muscle development required for independent walking and may cause injuries (Department of Health and Ageing, 2010).

# Section 3: Guidelines for pregnant and lactating women

### Eat plenty of fruits, vegetables and pulses every day

A balanced diet is very important when it comes to pregnancy and lactation, as it supports the mother and baby’s health. Consuming a variety of fruits and vegetables during pregnancy provides the body with a variety of vitamins especially vitamin C which acts as an antioxidant and necessary to make collagen which is a structural protein. They also provide minerals like potassium which plays an important role in acid-base regulation, fluid balance, muscle contraction and nerve conduction.

Fruits and vegetables also provide fibre for good digestive health. Including pulses regularly provides mother and baby with protein, vitamins like vitamin B1, which is important for energy production, minerals like iron which is crucial for blood oxygenation and both soluble and insoluble fibre.

During lactation it is also important to include a variety of fruits, vegetables and pulses as the baby’s main source of nutrient is breast milk and the nutrient content of the milk depends on the type of food consumed by the mother. By including a variety of foods the breast milk will become more nutrient-dense.

### Choose more whole grains, local tubers and other high-fibre starchy foods instead of rice

Choosing wholegrain varieties from this group will provide mother and baby with dietary fibre. Examples of wholegrains include oatmeal, cassava, sweet potato, breadfruit, plantains, brown rice, etc. Dietary fibre is important for the body’s digestive health and regular bowel movement. During pregnancy the chances of constipation is high because of an increase in the hormone progesterone, which relaxes smooth muscles throughout the body, including the digestive tract and also as the pregnancy advances there is more stress on the intestines. This means that food passes through the intestines more slowly. So it is always beneficial to include more high-fibre foods instead of refined food or food products. This group also provides carbohydrate which is the main source of energy and small amounts of B-vitamins essential for energy production in the body.

During lactation it is also important to include foods from this group as breastfeeding is an energy-consuming activity. The provision of fibre from wholegrain sources helps to keep the digestive system healthy and helps prevent constipation among mothers.

### Eat a variety of fish as they are safe, and provide important nutrients for mother's health and baby's development

Fish is a rich source of protein, omega-3 fatty acids, minerals like iron, magnesium, potassium etc. and vitamins like vitamin D and B12 which is important for the health of mother and the baby. Vitamin B12 is only found in animal products and is in higher concentrations in oily fish. It has an important role in pregnancy as it involved in amino acid metabolism and DNA synthesis. Inclusion of oily fish like mackerel, tuna, trevally, etc. also provide omega-3 fatty acids which contributes to the development of the baby’s brain and eyes. Consuming a variety of fish on a regular basis is recommended and it is safe as the mercury levels among fish in Seychelles is lower than many other countries.

During lactation it is also important to consume fish as the infants obtain all the nutrients especially omega-3 fatty acids through breast milk so the consumption should be continued even after the baby is born.
# Include iron-rich foods such as fish, meat, green leafy vegetables, and pulses every day to prevent anaemia

The increase in maternal blood supply during pregnancy greatly increase the demand for iron in the body. As iron is the main component of blood and helps in transportation of oxygen from lungs to other parts of the body, it is important to consume iron-rich foods regularly to ensure the body gets enough iron for the production of more red blood cells for the mother and baby, preventing anaemia. Anaemia is a condition that develops when the body lacks haemoglobin, reducing the red blood cell count. The symptoms of anaemia include fatigue, dizziness, shortness of breath, palpitation, pale skin, leg cramps etc.

Haem iron is the most bioavailable form of iron and is mainly found in animal sources such as red meat, fish and poultry. These foods should be included daily in meals consumed to ensure adequate iron intake. On the other hand, non-haem iron is mainly from plant sources e.g. beans, lentils, green leafy vegetables, etc. but is a less bioavailable form of iron. It is important therefore to consume these food sources with a vitamin C rich food source e.g. tomato, bell peppers, etc. as they improve the absorption of the iron. Whilst Vitamin C promotes iron absorption there are certain nutrients and substances in food which inhibits iron absorption. Food sources rich in calcium such as milk and milk products as well as beverages like tea which is rich in tannin, hinders the absorption of iron. Therefore it is best to avoid taking iron-rich foods or iron supplements with tea or milk, yoghurt and cheese.

It is important to continue to consume iron-rich foods during lactation to ensure that breast milk contains enough iron for both baby and mother to prevent anaemia in the mother.

# Include pasteurized milk and milk products every day

Milk and milk products include milk, yoghurt, cheese as well as plant-based alternatives like almond milk, soy milk, tofu, etc. Milk and milk products of animal origin are very nutrient-dense and provide important nutrients like protein, minerals like calcium, phosphorus and potassium as well as vitamins like vitamins A, D, B1, B2 and B12. Plant-based sources on the other hand are lower in these nutrients, and naturally lack vitamin B12 so it is preferable to choose those that are fortified. Additionally the bioavailability of calcium is much higher in milk and milk products from animal sources as compared to plant based sources.

During pregnancy it is important to consume milk and milk products since nutrients like calcium, vitamin D, and phosphorus contributes to the mineralisation of baby’s bones and teeth and others like protein aids in baby’s growth. Since the body becomes more efficient at absorbing calcium, calcium supplementation is not usually required. As the requirement of calcium increases slightly during pregnancy however if the mother is not taking enough calcium-rich foods the body will remove calcium from the mother’s stores, which is mainly her bones and teeth, for the baby’s development. To avoid or minimise this, calcium supplements are generally advised if the mother is not consuming or has very low intake of milk and milk products, has low vitamin D status, is vegetarian or a teenager.

Whilst breastfeeding, the mother should also consume milk and milk products regularly as they are nutrient-dense. For those who are unable to consume milk and milk products it is advisable to include plant-based sources like dark green leafy vegetables, almonds with the skin on, tofu etc. together with a calcium supplement.

It is also essential that milk and milk products are ‘pasteurized’ before consumption as fresh, unpasteurized products will contain bacteria that can be harmful during pregnancy or lactation and can cause food poisoning. Unpasteurized milk and milk products may contain *Listeria monocytogenes* which causes Listeriosis. Listeriosis can affect the fetus and lead to abortion, stillbirth or delivery of a sick infant.

# Reduce intake of salt and salty foods such as sauces, canned foods and processed meat

Salt plays an important role in helping to regulate and maintain your body fluid but taking too much salt affects the kidneys, causing the body to retain water. This extra fluid results in greater blood volume, which causes blood pressure to rise.

As such it is important to reduce intake of salt and salty foods like canned foods, crisps, sauces, processed meat e.g. sausages, luncheon meat, ham, bacon etc., sauces like tomato sauce, HP, soya sauce, etc., as well as Aromat, stock cubes, yeast extracts like marmite, pickles and salted fish/ meat during pregnancy and lactation as excessive salt intake can lead to high blood pressure which increases the risks of stroke and heart problems, as well as other complications. It is best to choose fresh products as often as possible and limit the intake of processed foods. It is also important to limit the use of salt when cooking and flavour the foods naturally with herbs, spices, lime, chili, etc.

Most of the processed meats are not thoroughly cooked during processing as they only undergo treatments like curing or smoking etc. therefore the risk of food contamination is high unless the product is cooked before consumption. Such products can lead to Listeriosis which will infect the foetus during pregnancy and cause food poisoning in the mother during both pregnancy and lactation. For this reason it is better to avoid them during pregnancy and lactation.

# Folic acid supplementation is compulsory in the first three months of pregnancy to prevent birth defects in the baby

Folic acid supplementation should begin prior to conception in planned pregnancies and continue in the first trimester of pregnancy. Supplementation is crucial because the amount of folic acid from food is insufficient to meet the demands of the foetus in that period. Folic acid deficiency can lead to ‘Neural tube defect’ (NTD) in which the baby’s spinal cord and brain fail to develop properly.

The recommended amount of folic acid that should be taken for the first three months of pregnancy from supplements is 400µg per day. Women with certain conditions like diabetes or a history of NTD in a previous pregnancy are advised to take 5mg or 5000µg per day to further reduce the risk of NTD in their baby. Alongside the supplements, it is always good to include foods that are rich in folic acid such as beans, tomatoes, lentils, oranges etc. throughout the pregnancy.

# Alcohol is unsafe during pregnancy & lactation

Prenatal exposure of the foetus to alcohol can lead to a condition known as ‘foetal alcohol syndrome’ which is characterised by a broad range of anomalies, including pre- and postnatal growth retardation, preterm delivery, central or craniofacial dysmorphia, neurological and behavioural disorders and disorders of cognitive function, which can persist throughout adulthood. Pregnant women are therefore advised not to consume any kinds of alcohol during pregnancy.

During lactation it is also not recommended to consume alcohol as the alcohol passes through the breast milk to the baby and can make the baby irritable and unsettled.

# Remain physically active throughout pregnancy but avoid strenuous exercise

Physical activity is an important part of any healthy pregnancy. It may help to reduce lower back pain during pregnancy by strengthening abdominal and back muscles. Regular physical activity amongst overweight or obese pregnant women may reduce the risk of gestational diabetes development. Reduced physical activity or sedentary behaviour during pregnancy increases the risk of constipation and back pain.

Start by exercising for 10 minutes every day, and as the body gets used to it increase to at least 30 minutes per day. Walking is usually the easiest type of exercise, but any strenuous exercise should be avoided as this can lead to complications.

Once the baby is born it is good to continue being physically active as this will help the mother to lose the weight gained during pregnancy.

# Choose water as your preferred drink to have every day instead of caffeinated and sugar-sweetened beverages like fizzy drinks, juices and cordials

Increased consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages e.g. fizzy drinks, cordials, juice, etc. leads to rapid increase in maternal and infant weight gain which can predispose the mother to gestational diabetes in pregnancy. Increased intake of caffeinated beverages like coffee, cola and tea, can raise the blood pressure as caffeine acts as a stimulant and also leads to heart burn by causing increased production of stomach acid. On the other hand, water helps prevent urinary tract infection (UTI), constipation and dehydration during pregnancy and lactation, so water should be chosen as the best choice for drink.
The water requirement of a woman increases during pregnancy and lactation. During pregnancy the minimum intake should be at least 2 litres per day whereas during lactation the minimum intake should be at least 3 litres of water per day.

During the lactation period, hydration becomes particularly important, since breast milk production significantly increases the mother's water loss, as the water from the mother’s body is lost through breast milk. Dehydration is most common among those who do not drink enough water while breastfeeding. The symptoms of dehydration include dry mouth, headache, dizziness, decreased urine output, etc.

## Rationale for revising the Seychelles food guide

### Renaming of the ‘foods containing fat and foods containing sugar’ food group to ‘oils and healthy fats’

Whilst it is important to promote the intake of small amounts of healthy unsaturated fats, it is equally important that there is no promotion for sugar intake. Sugar is a nutrient found naturally in many foods but this food group was initially promoting the intake of sugar added to food, although in small amounts. Hence the new group has been renamed as ‘oils and healthy fats’ and sugar has been removed.

### Fruits and vegetables as separate food groups

Fruits and vegetables have been separated as there is a lot of confusion at the moment on how much of each to have. Many people find it easier to consume fruits instead of vegetables due to palatability. As such it is important to educate the population on increasing their intake of vegetables which are often more nutrient-dense than fruits. The portion proposed is two portions of fruits and three portions of vegetables, hence maintaining the ‘5 portions a day’ recommendation from the previous guidelines as recommended by the World Health Organisation.

### Milk and milk products

Alongside milk, yoghurt and cheese, this group now also includes plant-based alternatives such as soy, almond, etc., which have been fortified with calcium and any other beneficial nutrient not normally found in plant foods. This change is relevant to reflect the usual intake of vegetarians, vegans, those with allergies and all others who choose not to have milk derived from mammals.

### ‘Rice, bread, cereals, pasta and tubers’ have been renamed as ‘Grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains’

Grains encapsulates rice, bread, cereals and pasta and breadfruit and plantains are not tubers but local produce frequently consumed by the population. The portion of this group on the plate has reduced from one third to one quarter as it is felt that many people have a sedentary lifestyle and therefore a lower energy requirement. This may also help in the fight against overweight and obesity which can be exacerbated with large portions of carbohydrate-rich foods.

### ‘Fish, meat and alternatives’ has been renamed as ‘Fish, meat, eggs, and plant-based alternatives’

Many individuals are unaware that egg features in this group, hence why it has now been included in the food group name. Plant-based alternatives includes pulses and nuts that are rich in protein and options for vegetarians and vegans who do not consume animal based products. Although pulses and eggs were already part of this group, it was felt that it needed to be more clearly identified by including these as part of the food group name.

### Appendix 4: Additional information for breastfeeding

#### Colostrum and mature milk

***Colostrum*** is the special milk that is secreted by the breast in the first 2–3 days after delivery which is a thick, sticky fluid high in protein, antibodies, white cells, growth factors and vitamin A. It is important that infants receive colostrum, and no other feeds, at this time because it helps protect babies against viruses and bacteria, and helps protect the digestive tract. It also has all the food and water the baby needs. Colostrum comes in very small amounts. This suits the baby’s very small stomach and the immature kidneys that cannot handle large volumes of fluid.

Milk starts to be produced in larger amounts between 2 and 4 days after delivery, making the breasts feel full; the milk is then said to have “come in”. From day 7 to 14, the milk is called *transitional*, and after 2 weeks it is called *mature milk*.

***Preterm breast milk*** – The milk of a mother giving birth before 37 weeks gestation, preterm breast milk, has more protein, higher levels of some minerals including iron, and more immune properties than mature milk, making it more suited for the needs of a premature baby. A mother's milk can even be used before the baby is able to breastfeed. The mother can express her milk, and it can be fed to the baby with a cup, spoon or tube.

***Mature breast milk*** – Mature breast milk contains all of the major nutrients – protein, carbohydrates, fat, vitamins, minerals and water in the amounts the baby needs. It changes in relation to the time of day, the length of a breastfeed, the needs of the baby, and diseases with which the mother has had contact.

The components of breast milk provide nutrients as well as substances that help in digestion, growth, development and provide protection from infections. Breast milk continues to provide these nutrients, protection, and other benefits as the child grows; these components do not disappear at a certain age.

It takes about 3 to 6 weeks for breastfeeding to become fully established and hence it is important that the baby is fed on demand during this period. The breast produce milk in response to the baby’s suckling and therefore the more the baby is put to the breast, the more milk will be produced.
# Importance of early contact

WHO and UNICEF recommend that women should begin to breastfeed within the first hour of birth which helps to establish breastfeeding early on.

## A good supply of breast milk will be established if the mother:

* Helps the baby to breastfed soon after birth.
* Feeds the baby as frequently as he or she wants, usually every 1-3 hours, for as long as he or she wants at a feed.
* Breastfeeds exclusively
* Feeds the baby at night, when prolactin release in response to suckling is high.
* Makes sure the baby is well attached at the breast and do not give any artificial dummies or teats that would confuse his or her suckling and reduce stimulation of the breast.
* Let the baby finish the first breast before offering the second breast; babies vary in their rate of breast emptying although most babies empty the first breast within the first 10-15 minutes. The baby should decide on the length of feed.
* If baby does not suckle, express the milk so that milk production continues.

## POSITIONING AND ATTACHMENT

A mother needs help in the first few days to make sure that she is able to position and attach her baby correctly to the breast, to ensure that her baby is suckling effectively. For a mother to produce the milk that her baby needs, her baby must suckle often and suckle in the right way. A baby cannot get the milk by sucking only on the nipple. Good positioning helps to ensure that the baby suckles well and helps the mother to produce a good supply of breast milk.

### How to help a mother to position her baby

1. **Baby’s head and body in line** - The baby’s body should be straight, not bent or twisted, but with the head slightly back.
2. **Baby held close to mother’s body** - The baby’s whole body should almost face his mother’s body. He should be turned away just enough to be able to look at her face.
3. **Baby supported** - the baby’s whole body should be supported with the mother’s arm along the baby’s back.
4. **Baby approaches breast, nose to nipple**

### Different ways to position the baby:

1. Cradle position (most commonly used)
2. Cross cradle position (good for small babies)
3. Side-lying position (use to rest while breastfeeding and at night)
4. Under-arm position (use after caesarean section, if your nipples are painful or if you are breastfeeding twins or a small baby)

### How a mother can help her baby to attach well by:

* Touching the baby’s lips with her nipple
* Waiting until the baby’s mouth is open wide
* Moving the baby quickly onto her breast
* Aiming her nipple up towards the roof of the baby’s mouth
* Aiming his/her lower lip behind her nipple, so his/her chin touches the breast.

### Signs of good attachment are:

* More of the areola is visible above the baby’s top lip than below the lower lip
* The baby’s mouth is wide open
* The baby’s lower lip is curled outwards
* The baby’s chin is touching or almost touching the breast.

# Causes of poor attachment

* Use of a feeding bottle before breastfeeding is well established can cause poor attachment, because the mechanism of suckling with a bottle is different.
* Functional difficulties such as flat and inverted nipples, or a very small or weak infant, are also causes of poor attachment.
* Inexperience of the mother and lack of skilled help from the health workers who attend her. Many mothers need skilled help in the early days to ensure that the baby attaches well and can suckle effectively.
* Health workers need to have the necessary skills to give this help.

## Signs of effective suckling are:

* The baby takes slow deep suckles, sometimes pausing.
* You may be able to see or hear your baby swallowing after one or two suckles.
* Suckling is comfortable and pain free for you.
* Your baby finishes the feed, releases the breast and looks contented and relaxed.
* The breast is softer after the feed.

## Signs that a baby is not suckling effectively

* Rapid, shallow sucks and smacking or clicking sounds
* Cheeks drawn in
* Baby fusses at breast or comes on and off
* Baby feeds very frequently, for a very long time, but does not release breast and seems unsatisfied
* Mother feels pain.

> ***Breastfeed the baby before he or she starts to cry. Earlier signs of hunger include a COMBINATION of the following: being alert and restless, opening mouth and turning head, putting tongue in and out, sucking on hand or fist.***

## How to tell if the baby is getting adequate breast milk

A thriving breastfed baby will show the following signs:

* Provide at least 6 to 8 very wet nappies /diapers in 24 hours
* Good skin colour and muscle tone
* Alert, responsive and contented
* Produce bright yellow stools (frequency is unimportant)
* Some weight gain and growth in length and head circumference

## USING EXPRESSED BREAST MILK

The ideal way to remove breast milk from your breast is putting the baby to the breast, however there may be times when this is not possible and feeding the baby with expressed breast milk using a cup, spoon or tube may be necessary. The following are some of the reasons of expressing and using expressed breast milk:

* Leave breast milk for a baby when his mother goes out or goes to work
* Feed a low-birth-weight baby who cannot breastfeed
* The baby is premature
* Feed a sick baby, who cannot suckle enough
* Keep up the supply of breast milk when a mother or baby is ill
* Prevent leaking when a mother is away from her baby
* Help a baby to attach to a full breast
* Help with breast health conditions like engorgement.
* Someone else is feeding the baby to provide rest to the mother
* Breast milk can be expressed manually or with the use of a hand or electric breast pump

### Appendix 6: Sugar and its health implications

Sugar is defined as any class of water-soluble crystalline carbohydrates obtained from various plants, especially sugar cane and sugar beet having a characteristically sweet taste and is classified as monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) or disaccharides (sucrose, lactose, maltose). Glucose and fructose are naturally found in fruits and vegetables, lactose is found in milk and milk products while maltose is naturally seen in cereal products.

Sugars can also be added to foods and drinks during processing in various forms such as white, brown and raw sugar, honey, syrups and extracts. There are many different names for sugar. Examples of common names are table sugar, brown sugar, molasses, honey, beet sugar, cane sugar, confectioner's sugar, powdered sugar, raw sugar, etc.

In March 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) published new guidelines recommending that adults and children alike reduce their consumption of free sugars.

‘Free sugars’ include monosaccharides and disaccharides added to foods and beverages by the manufacturer, cook or consumer, and sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices and fruit juice concentrates (World Health Organisation, 2015). Free sugars contribute to the overall energy density of diets, and may promote a positive energy balance leading to weight gain. Examples of foods/drinks with free sugars include chocolates, biscuits, ice cream, confectionaries, sugar sweetened beverages etc.

Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB) are drinks with added sugar including: non-diet soft drinks/fizzy drinks, fruit juices, flavoured juice drinks, sports drinks, sweetened tea, coffee drinks, energy drinks, and electrolyte replacement drinks. Such products are high in free sugars and contribute to weight gain as they are consumed in large amount by consumers.

***Recommendations for sugar***

* The World Health Organisation (WHO) recommends a reduced intake of free sugars throughout the life course as excessive intake promotes weight gain and obesity as well as dental caries especially in young children.
* In both adults and children, WHO recommends reducing the intake of free sugars to less than ten per cent of total energy intake (strong recommendation), which is equivalent to 12 teaspoons based on an average intake of 2000 calories per day.
* WHO suggests a further reduction of the intake of free sugars to below five per cent of total energy intake which is equivalent to six teaspoons of sugar per day.
* For a child it is considerably variable and based on gender and age but intake should be less than the above values for adults.
* Intake of free sugars is not considered an appropriate strategy for increasing caloric intake in individuals with inadequate energy intake if other options are available.
* A single teaspoon of sugar is around four grams of sugar. Therefore it is important to know how much sugar is in the food you are eating so you can ensure you stay within the recommended limits.

**How to calculate recommended amount of sugar from food and drinks?**

1 teaspoon of sugar = 4grams of sugar

For example if a product has 40grams of sugar then the number of teaspoons is equivalent to:

Number of teaspoons = 40grams ÷ 4grams

= 10 teaspoons

***Some strategies to reduce sugar intake:***

* Add little or no sugar to food and drinks.
* Choose water as the main drink instead of sugar-sweetened beverages like cola, sprite, packet juices, squashes etc. Other healthier options are those with no added sugar such as sparkling water, coffee and herbal tea, etc.
* Choose fresh fruits, low fat yoghurt etc. with no added sugar instead of high sugar desserts such as ice-ream, mousse, etc.
* Dried fruits are concentrated sources of sugar since the water has been removed, therefore making it a very high-sugar snack. It also sticks more easily to teeth hence increasing the risk of dental caries. It is therefore recommended that they are consumed in moderation.
* Check the ingredient list of food and drinks. Anything that ends with ‘ose’ is a form of sugar for e.g. maltose, galactose, sucrose etc. or anything which contains any form of syrups of concentrates contains sugar in them even if they claim to be sugar-free.
* While choosing breakfast cereals opt for whole varieties e.g. All Bran cereal, Weetabix, oatmeal etc. instead of sugar-coated ones like coco pops, fruit loops etc.
* Consume sugary foods (e.g. nougat, cakes, banana fritters, candies etc.) and sugary drinks (e.g. lemonades, lucozade, ribena, bitter lemon etc.) in small amounts and only occasionally.

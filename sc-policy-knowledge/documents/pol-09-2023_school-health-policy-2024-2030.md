## Executive Summary

The National School Health Policy 2023, developed by the Ministry of Health and Ministry of Education, has a vision that:

> *“All Seychellois children achieve their full development potential in life”.*

And a mission to:

> *“Work with school-going children, their parents/guardians, school staff and other stakeholders to create and maintain a conducive learning environment that supports healthy living, quality education and optimal child development”.*

### Specific Objectives

The specific objectives are to:

1. Create and foster sustainable and conducive school environments that protect and promote the health, safety, security and the well-being of children and support learning and optimal child development.
2. Improve availability and access to health education, health skills building, and promotive, preventive and rehabilitative health services to the children.
3. Engage all stakeholders especially school staff and parents to uphold a culture of healthy living, safety, and risk reduction in teaching and learning in the school environment.
4. Integrate any emergency and/or disaster prevention, preparedness and response into relevant sector/school emergency and disaster response plans.
5. Invest to support optimal child development to secure our future, the next generation.

The development of the policy was undertaken in consultation and collaboration with key stakeholders, including the Ministry of Employment and Social Affairs (MoESA) and the Ministry of Youth, Sports and Family (MoYSF), parents, teachers, students, school health nurses and the community.

Successful implementation of the policy will involve joint-actions across stakeholder MDA’s, NGO’s and the general society, teachers, schoolchildren and their parents/guardians.

To liaise with the Ministry responsible for the Youth and Adolescent Health Policy that is being drafted for implementation of Health, Safety and Wellbeing.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The Link between Health and Education

Schools are essential for young people to acquire knowledge, socio-emotional skills including self-regulation and resilience, and critical thinking skills that provide the foundation for a healthy future. Access to education, educational attainment and safe and supportive school environments have been linked to better health outcomes across the life course. In turn, good health is linked to reduced drop-out rates and greater educational attainment, educational performance, employment and productivity.

WHO has long recognized the link between health and education and the potential for schools to play a central role in safeguarding student health and well-being. In 1995, WHO launched the Global School Health Initiative, which aimed to strengthen approaches to health promotion in schools. Among those approaches, pairing children with health services occupies an important place.

School health services (SHS), as defined in this guideline, are services provided by a health worker or other trained staff, to students enrolled in primary or secondary education, either within school premises or beyond.

In all WHO regions, school-age children and adolescents (those aged 5–19 years) experience a range of largely preventable health problems, including unintentional injury, interpersonal violence, sexual and reproductive health issues, communicable diseases, non-communicable diseases and mental health issues. In addition, school-age children and adolescents have positive physical, sexual, psychosocial and neurocognitive health, and developmental needs as they progress from childhood to adulthood. Schools offer a unique opportunity to implement effective health services at scale for children and adolescents.

Social determinants of health define the environment in which children live, grow, and can affect their lives in both positive and negative ways. Access to education, safe and supportive school environments and safe and supportive families have been linked to better health outcomes.

Additionally, it is well known that the risk factors for non-communicable diseases, communicable diseases, and injuries among youth, are internalised during childhood and adolescence. These are caused by behaviours that contribute to unintended injuries and violence; sexual behaviours that result in unintended pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV infection; alcohol and other drug use; tobacco use; unhealthy dietary behaviours; and inadequate physical activity.

The school represents an ideal setting to reach a large group of the population, which include children, school staff and families. School is also the ideal environment to promote not just learning, but also to cultivate personal values for growth and development, healthy living habits and teach about safe or unsafe practices..

## 1.2 The Seychelles Context

The Government of Seychelles values child health and well-being, optimal child development, and children achieving their full potential in life. Since independence, the country has provided universal access to free health and education for its citizens. This commitment is further reflected in the Government spending on health and education, which routinely rank amongst the top three amongst annual budgets for all sectors.

Education is compulsory for the first 11 years, up to Secondary 5, and provided free of charge across a network of state schools. Tertiary education is also provided free of charge, but on a merit-based system.

Health care services are also free at the point of use for all citizens, spanning preventive, promotive and curative services. There is a robust school health programme, encompassing nutrition, vaccinations, regular medical and health examinations and a dental health service. There are district health centres, which tend to be close to schools, providing easier access and availability of medical and health services.

## 1.3 Policy Context
The government of the republic of Seychelles remains committed to the advancing of health and education to optimise child growth and development. The **Constitution**[[1]](#footnote-1) of the land stipulates the responsibility of the State to protect, provide and promote the rights of every citizen. **Article 29** outlines the right for every citizen to enjoy the highest attainable standard of health, and **Article 33**, the right to free education of its citizens.

The **National Development Strategy 2019-2023**[[2]](#footnote-2), which sets forward a promising path for Seychelles, echoing the common desire to see the country develop further, with the Seychellois people owning and actively participating in the development process.

The **National Health Policy (2016)**[[3]](#footnote-3) to further ensure the effective realization of the health rights, with the principle of “*Health for all, Health by all and Health in all*”. This is reinforced through the six strategic directions (SD) in the **National Health Strategic Plan (NHSP) 2022-2026**. **SD2 Protect and Improve Universal Health Coverage** (UHC) supports the strengthening of primary health care, **SD3 Improve Health Security** ensuring public health standards and services in educational settings and the community, and **SD4 Promote healthy populations**, which attempts to address the social determinants of health.

Primary health care is an integral part of the country’s health system. The **Primary Health Care** (PHC) **Package[[4]](#footnote-4)** sets out the services that the State offers, free at the point of use in state health institutions. The programmes in the PHC Package are organised along a life course approach (maternal, child, school and adolescent, sexual and reproductive health, men’s health and healthy ageing), and are further supplemented by disease-specific programmes for key health conditions (diabetes, cancer, HIV/AIDS, mental health, cardiovascular diseases, and nutrition)[[5]](#footnote-5).

The **Education Act** 2004[[6]](#footnote-6), and amendments in 2005 & 2017[[7]](#footnote-7), make provision for the establishment of a comprehensive system of education and training. The Act and amendments reflect universal and national values, which promote the complete development of the person and equip the person to participate fully in social and economic development. MoE is further guided by the **Education Sector Medium Term Strategic Plan 2018-2022 and Beyond**[[8]](#footnote-8), **Strategic Plan 2021-2024 (raising aspirations and ensuring worthwhile learning outcomes)** which are the main drivers to institutionalize a culture of achievement and excellence for all at all levels of the education and human resource development system.

HPS Committee TOR and Guideline is referred to in the ANNEX for details of work undertaken jointly.

Additionally, the National School Health Policy 2023 is in line with the **Health Promoting School Guidelines** published by the MoE in 2019. The guidelines provide direction to schools and partners on how to move towards adopting a more holistic and integrated approach to health and learning. The guidelines are informed by the WHO Ottawa Charter, which proposes five key action areas for health promotion:

1. Build healthy public policy;
2. Create supportive environments;
3. Strengthen community actions;
4. Develop personal skills; and
5. Reorient health services

Built on the **WHO’s Health Promoting Schools Framework**[[9]](#footnote-9), the guidelines serve as a strategy to help school communities manage health and social issues, enhance student learning and improve school effectiveness.

A number of other policies internal to the education sector exist, which support children’s health, wellbeing and optimal development:

* **School Nutrition Policy 2008**, which aim to utilize schools as sites for improving the nutritional well-being of all schoolchildren by providing a framework to coordinate all aspects of school nutrition, E.g. curriculum, school tuckshops, provision of school meals, and training of school personnel. The School Nutrition Policy requires that all school set up their School Nutrition Action Group (SNAG).
* **Inclusive Education Policy (2015)**, to ensure special education needs and disability issues are well integrated in policies, practices and procedures in across sector.’
* **National Anti-Bullying Policy and Strategy for Primary, Secondary School and Professional Centres in 2018**, to provide guidance on preventing and tackling institution-based bullying behaviour amongst students and also help address the negative impact within institution of bullying behaviour that occurs elsewhere.
* A **Whole School Behaviour Management Policy (WSBMP)** was also developed in 2021 to promote discipline and ensure effective behaviour management in the schools.
* All primary and secondary schools are also expected to adhere to a **Code of Conduct**.
* Seychelles Educational Institutions Emergency Planning Guide 2017
* Tuck Shop Breakfast Guidelines, 2007.
* Coronavirus Guidelines, 2020.
* Road Safety Guideline.
* Fire Safety in School, 2005.
* Teenage Pregnancy Policy, 2005.
* Guidelines for School Lunches, 2008.

Seychelles is also committed to global and regional long-term development agendas, which are critical in aspiring direction. Seychelles is committed to the **Sustainable Development Goals** (SDGs), more specifically SDG3, which focuses on good health and wellbeing, and SDG4, on ensuring inclusive and equitable quality education[[10]](#footnote-10). Regionally, Seychelles is also committed to fulfilling the **African Union Continental Education Strategy for Africa 2016-2025**[[11]](#footnote-11), a strategic framework in the realization of the **African Union’s vision in Agenda 2063**.[[12]](#footnote-12)

With increasing emphasis on Education system resilience following the series of school interruptions during the recent COVID-19 epidemic, as well as the foreseeable implications of climate change, understanding, prevention, preparedness and response to any disasters and recovery thereafter are key additional considerations for this plan.

## 1.4 Policy Development Process

The development of this policy was initiated in December 2016, through the school health programme manager and the World Health Organisation country office (WHO-CO). The development process can be divided into two phases.

An initial period (2017-2020) where a first draft of the policy was developed, led jointly by the family health and nutrition directorate of the Health Care Agency (HCA) and members from the Ministry of Education, with the support of external technical assistance.

The process was at a halt from mid-2020 to mid-2022, in view of staff redeployment during the COVID-19 pandemic. A new team from the Family Health directorate of the HCA, in partnership with the policy unit of the MOH, reviewed the policy process, took inventory of the steps completed to date, and resumed stakeholder engagement, policy revision and finalisation.
In September 2022 a series of stakeholder consultations was initiated to gather inputs towards the development of the policy. List of stakeholders in Annex.

From January 2023 collaboration with MoE was further strengthened to finalize the policy. To add validation of policy.

# 2. Situation Analysis

## 2.1 Health of the Seychelles Population

### General health

The mid-year population estimate for 2021 is 99,258, representing an increase of 0.8% over 2020. Of this, 51% were males, 49% females, 22% aged below 15, and 12% 65 years and above. About 14% of the population are of school-going age (5-14y)[[13]](#footnote-13).

#### Births and Deaths

A total of 1,665 live births were registered in 2021, 51% males, 49% females. Crude birth rate was 16.8 per 1,000 population[[14]](#footnote-14). Birth rates are noted to be relatively stable.

A total of 925 deaths were registered in 2021. Of these, 480 (51.2%) were amongst males, 445 (48.1%) amongst females. These numbers represent a 16.4% and 38.5% increase over 2019 and 2020, respectively[[15]](#footnote-15).

#### Life Expectancy

There was an increase in overall life expectancy (LE) in 2020 over 2019 (from 73.9y to 77.3y), followed by a significant drop in 2021 (to 73.5y).

The drop was particularly notable for women, whereby there was a drop of 6.5y, from 82y in 2020 to 75.5y in 2021, to the lowest level since 1990. LE in men increased to a peak in 2020 (from 69.7y in 2019 to 72.7y in 2020), with a drop in 2021 (to 71.3y) – see Figure 1.

Seychelles also has a long-standing, wide, gap in life expectancy between males and females, of eight to 11 years.

### Child health and health risk behaviours

The obesogenic environment poses great threats to child health and development in the Seychelles context; and the mix of poverty and social ills compounds on the wellbeing of children and adolescents.

The combination of these factors and others lead to unsafe sexual practices, unhealthy lifestyles, violence, bullying and mental stress; contributing to adverse health and developmental consequences.

#### Substance Abuse, including Alcohol
Substance abuse appears to **start from an early age** in Seychelles (typically teenage, in late primary to early secondary school)[[18]](#footnote-18). Based on information on referrals to substance abuse services, children from as low as age 9 onwards were referred for care (Table 3)[[19]](#footnote-19),[[20]](#footnote-20). Many referrals of minors come from schools. Across all age-groups, significantly **more males access services** for substance abuse.

A survey in 2017[[21]](#footnote-21) estimates population size of heroin users as 4,318 users (5.4% of the total population in 2017), with about half of them being injection drug users. The first use of heroin (28.25%) was between the ages of 15 to 17 years. In the same survey 71.9% of respondents had their first alcohol drink before the age of 18 years.

In the same study, substance use other than cannabis was reported at 14% and as many as 21% of students smoked a cigarette on one or more days during the past 30 days. Cannabis use before the onset on the survey was 9%.

#### Diet and Exercise

According to the Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) 2015, only 45% of students were physically active on at least 4 days a week, for at least 60 minutes per day. The same survey estimates 39% of students surveyed reported eating five portions of fruits and vegetables per day, on at least five days in a week; and high consumption of sugar-sweetened carbonated drinks and packed fruit juices.

#### Sexual behaviours

Under-aged and unsafe sexual behaviours contribute to HIV and other sexually transmitted infections, as well as unintended and/or teenage pregnancies. According to the GSHS (2015), 40% of students had had sexual intercourse previously. Among students who had had sexual intercourse, only 50% used a condom at their last sexual intercourse. Substantial proportions reported sexual abuse, with 46% having been bullied at least once during the past 30 days.

Overall, 21% of students considered attempting suicide during the past 12 months before the survey. Certain other factors of note, 30% of students missed classes or school without permission during the past 30 days; and only 31% of students reported that their parents or guardians understood their problems and worries, most of the time or always, during the past 30 days.

#### Oral health of schoolchildren

In 2018, 10,468 visits for curative interventions on carious teeth were recorded in existing dental facilities across public schools. Dental caries is the predominant condition affecting school children in Seychelles based on historic data, although there are no recent surveys. Periodontal conditions affect schoolchildren, however the severe cases are rare and sparse. In 2010, 87% of school children showed visible debris on teeth due to poor oral hygiene practices. Bleeding of gums (32.2 % of 12 year olds, 2010) and calculus deposits on teeth (26.1% of 15 year olds, 2010) are more commonly noticed in adolescents.

#### Accidents and injuries

Injuries and accidents affect children of all age groups in Seychelles. Mortality data show (see Table 4) that many deaths in children and adolescents result from accidents and injuries; the key causes are road traffic accidents in 15-19y age group and drownings in 5-9y olds. The actual incidence of accidents, injuries and violence affecting this group is definitely far higher, but not captured under current health and education information systems.

#### Mental health

Behavioural disorders are the most common reasons for children (<18y) seeking mental health care services. Certain behavioural and emotional disorders with usual onset in childhood and adolescence ranked as the most common group of mental health conditions for which children are referred for mental health consultations. This is followed by mental and behavioural disorders related to psychoactive substance abuse and schizophrenia, schizotypal and delusional disorders.

Children admitted to the service are admitted due to neurotic, stress-related and/or somatoform disorders[[23]](#footnote-23).
#### Mortality amongst schoolchildren

Through an ad-hoc analysis of mortality data from 2015 to 2022, deaths amongst 5-19y olds (school-going age group) were analysed. On average, about 1.2% of all deaths (about 9 to 10 deaths annually) occur in the age group 5-19y; majority are amongst boys (72%), with girls contributing 28%. This excess ‘premature deaths’ amongst males is a pattern reflected into adulthood[[24]](#footnote-24).

## 2.2 Education in Seychelles

To ensure that Education in Seychelles remains relevant and receptive to change, the national education sector is effectively developing and growing progressively in line with local and global dynamic. The Ministry of Education works to ensure that there is effective running of all state schools, professional centres and universities, such that quality learning, instructions and training reach all the learners.

Emphasis is on schoolchildren’s well-being, safety, attainment, both academic and physical performances. Altogether, there are nine learning areas, which are broken into different subjects, taught, on a progressive level at both primary and secondary level.

Special provisions are made to support children with a learning difficulty (or other special needs), such as Special Education Needs teachers to work with them with the aim of helping them succeed in their own way.

However, there is still room for improvement as behavioural issues might play a major role on children’s poor attainment in general, as children’s home and community environment has direct impact on teaching and learning.

### PSCE Programme

PSCE, which focuses on personal, social, and citizenship education in general, helps school children understand the need for them to develop holistically in a conducive environment with the help of adults and their community. PSCE helps a lot with children’s behaviour in and outside the classroom, hence support teaching and learning fully.

In this subject, children learn about their development, and how to take care of themselves, and others through topics such as growing up, living healthy, sex education, healthy eating, drugs and alcohol, and understanding and managing emotions, etc. All children participate in PSCE lessons, like any other academic subjects.

### Physical Education

This is another significant subject in the school curriculum, as it helps schoolchildren with their physical development, mental health and contributes towards maintaining a healthy lifestyle and weight. This subject complements the PSCE, as the Physical Education instructor also provide advice on healthy eating and living.

All children participate in this outdoor curriculum-based physical activity, unless a valid reason is produced. As at 2023, compulsory physical education is 60 minutes per week.

### Home Economics

In this subject, secondary-school students learn about safe food preparation, focusing on environmental education too. Hygiene and cleanliness is one of the key components in this subject. Apart from that, they also learn about Safety and Prevention.

## 2.3 Health system

Health services are delivered principally through the Government health care facilities: a network of 17 integrated primary health care centres, supported by one tertiary hospital with multiple specialised annexes. These cover services across the health-disease spectrum, from promotive, through preventive, to restorative services, as well as across the levels of care: primary, secondary, tertiary and emergency care. Certain services are delivered in the school settings (school dental health and see later for services under the school health programme). The youth health centre, situated centrally, also serves as a clinic delivering services tailored to needs of youth.

These are supplemented by a network of private clinics, dental practices, optometry services and pharmacies, although private sector services focus principally on curative care.

In addition to clinical services, the MOH uses a series of programmes to provide leadership and drive for planning, coordination and implementation of preventive and promotive interventions. These programmes are spread across the life-course and key disease conditions to cover the key identified health needs of the population[[25]](#footnote-25). ## The school health programme

The school health programme is a long-standing collaboration across the health and education sectors to provide health education and health services in the school setting. It is a long-running programme and caters for children in Crèche, through primary, to secondary schools (covering all children aged 4 to 17 years, in public and private educational settings), as well as schools for children with special needs.

The programme is implemented by a network of fifteen school health nurses, led by the school health programme manager, in collaboration with the individual school management and teaching teams. The nurses are, however, not dedicated to school health work, but are rather based at the regional health centre, and also assist with other clinic-based nursing duties. This latter point often leads to interruption of school health activities at times of high demand for staffing in regional health centre networks.

The school health are supported by other relevant health professionals such as Community Dental Health Professionals, Speech Therapists and Social Workers. The health professionals seek parental consent at each stage of their interventions and they also provide counselling and social support including home visits.

The programme takes a whole-of-school approach, catering for health needs of students, parents, school staff and the school environment, and where possible, is involved in the wider community. The programme undertakes certain core activities routinely, these are summarised next.

### Health screening and medical examination

Screening aims to monitor physical, mental, emotional and social health of school-going children periodically across the age-range: at Crèche induction, Primary 4, Secondary 1 and Secondary 4 levels in the education system. Appropriate involvement of parents are done as may be the case, and relevant referrals are made to access services in other parts of the health and social systems.

Activities include anthropometric measurements (height, weight, mid-arm circumference), vision and hearing screening (the latter using an oto-acoustic emission screener). A general head-to-toe examination is also carried out to identify our health concerns, and certain screening examinations such as breast and testicular examination are carried out for Secondary 4 students.

The health screening also provides an opportunity for health promotion, counselling and interactions between students, parents, health and teaching staff. See **Annex 3: School Health Screening Assessment** for a full list of activities undertaken as part of health screening.

Health screening in schools was initiated since 1998, with periodic broader surveys which include tobacco use, alcohol use, physical activity, blood pressure, etc. The school health platform is also leveraged on by Institute for Early Childhood Education for data collection to feed into relevant early childhood research, as well as the UPCCD, to estimate prevalence of overweight and obesity on an annual basis, as well as for periodic Global School-based Health Surveys (GSHS – carried out in 2007 and 2015).

### Immunisation

Immunisation is a key undertaking of the school health programme (in collaboration with the expanded programme on immunisation and school management). Vaccines administered in school settings include:

* **Primary One:** Measles, Mumps and Rubella, Diphtheria, Tetanus and Polio (IPV); spread out across the three school terms.
* **Primary Six:** Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) Vaccines.
* **Secondary Three:** Tetanus and Polio vaccines are also administered in secondary three.

Immunisation coverage for primary one vaccines are high (>90%); but challenges exist for achieving and maintaining high coverage for vaccines administered in later ages (e.g. HPV).

In 2021 and 2022, COVID-19 vaccination of 5-11y olds (in 2023) and 12-17y olds (in 2021-2022) were carried out through school-based campaigns, with joint mobilisation of resources and coordination of intervention between the two Ministries.

### Health Education and Promotion

Health education and promotion are conducted by school health nurses as one-on-one session with school children or as groups or by classroom. Health education forms part of the Personal, Social and Civic Education (PSCE) curriculum which is implemented from primary one to secondary five. The programme equips students to take responsibility for their health and general wellbeing. Other ministries, groups and organizations are also involved in teaching health education/health promotion.

### Staff Wellness

The School Health Programme also caters for the wellness and health of staff members through health screening activities, training in stress management, organizing physical and social activities, counselling etc., according to identified and/or expressed needs.
More recently, staff wellness initiatives are further supported through involvement in the Workplace Wellbeing programme framework, although this project is still at a nascent phase.

#### Promoting healthy school environments

School health nurses advocate for, and support, the creation and maintenance of a health-supporting school environment. The school health programme synergises with education-sector led initiatives, such as health-promoting schools, to realise this objective.

#### Nutrition in schools

Nutrition services is integrated in the School Health Programme. Nutritionist and nutrition technicians work in close collaboration with the School Health Nurses and Ministry of Education. They provide nutrition education to children, parents, and school staff, nutrition assessment and counselling, nutrition management, they advise MoE on meal provision in schools.

#### Collaboration with youth and sports and Non-Governmental Organisations

The Ministry responsible for Youth and Sports work in close collaboration with MoE to implement various initiatives to promote physical activity. This is done mostly through the National Sports Council. Past initiatives included SEYX30 which is a movement to motivate adolescent and youth to do physical activity at least 30 minutes a day. The parents welcome fitness Programme was also initiated to redress the issue of obesity among children in Seychelles to get them involve in Physical activity. Physical Education is a compulsory subject of a duration of 80 minutes per week in the curriculum of the Ministry of Education for both state and private schools.

## 2.4 Education system

There are twenty-five primary state schools, eleven secondary state schools, and nine private schools in the Seychelles education system. Additionally, there are two schools, which cater for children with special needs. There are eleven professional centres and one university (together referred to as tertiary education centres or TECs).

Each school has a management team headed by a head teacher. A school council committee has form with the aim of supporting the school to function more effectively. Education is free and compulsory in state school from primary one to Secondary 5. Primary school, that is Primary one to six, is six years schooling. The medium of instructions is Creole for primary one and two, apart from Mathematics, which is taught in English. English becomes the language of instructions as primary three onwards, with French included as a second language.

Secondary schooling takes five years, secondary one to secondary five. However, children having problem academically have the option to leave school at secondary four to venture in other vocational and educational activities. Most children complete secondary five, as it is an entry requirement for most post-secondary institutions, and later graduate education.

All through the education system, the schools try their best to help children live a healthy lifestyle whereby health promoting programmes and activities are promoted in the school environment. The teaching of health education is integrated into various subjects, more apparent in some than in others, as seen in the list below:

* **Physical education** (P.E) – This programme helps school children improve their physical health and well-being.
* **Physical, Social, Citizenship Education** (PSCE) – aims to empower learners of all ages to assume active roles, both locally and globally, in building more peaceful, tolerant, and inclusive and secure societies.
* **English** - Some themes such as ‘my body’ is taught to promote personal health.
* **Science** - Some topics taught are related to health education such as pollution to make the children more aware of their immediate environment.
* **School meals** – The school meal programme includes both healthy provision of breakfast and lunch. The government of Seychelles subsidizes school meal for primary students only, and breakfast for all students.
* **Sexual education** – Sexual education forms part of the PSCE programme. Students are sensitized about the changes happening in their body at a very young age and learn about how to protect themselves from sexual abuse. This is continued at secondary level.
* **Dedicated Fund** – Vulnerable students are assisted from this programme, through which costs of school meals and certain other social supports are covered.
* **Tuck-shop/ healthy eating** – All schools have a school tuck-shop whereby students and staff can purchase snacks. To be able to operate operators need to have a license for the business, a lease agreement with the school, and a medical certificate certifying that they fit for the business. They need to operate according to the tuck-shop guidelines (2018) and School Nutrition policy, 2008.
* **Anti-Bullying** – There is a policy on anti-bullying. All schools are to be bullying free zones.
* **Whole School Behaviour Management programme** – This programme ensures that all members of school staff are involved in addressing behavioral issues.

### Other Stakeholders Working in Collaboration with Schools

Schools are supported by a number of key stakeholders, in various activities and programmes; this includes government organizations, NGOs, private organizations and parents. External partners also participate in school projects, such as networking programmes, sponsorships and volunteer work.

## 2.6 Priority issues identified for school health
* Staff diversion to routine clinic work and/or ad-hoc activities by external partners (e.g. by NGOs, community events/activities, school-based surveys/research) contributes to inadequate dedicated staffing for effective delivery of school health programme activities.
* The social degradation of the community from a combination of factors, including poverty, substance abuse, weak family structures, and inter-personal violence and abuse, is driving poor child physical and mental health, teenage pregnancy, poor educational performance and school completion rates, and faltering child development and life achievements.
* An obesogenic societal environment is driving an epidemic of childhood overweight and obesity, presenting inter-generational consequences to society.
* The social and economic crises ensuing from the COVID-19 pandemic imposes further budgetary constraints on the public health and education systems, possibly hindering restoration of interrupted school health services, and threatening sustainability of a strong school health programme, including past health gains from it.
* Accidental deaths (road incidents, drowning, unintentional poisoning) need to be addressed.
* Capacity constraints in terms of numbers and expertise for both teachers and school health nurses to deliver fully on their school health responsibilities.
* Inadequate support/engagement of parents in school health related activities.
* Need for further training for School Health Nurses and teachers.
* Challenges in cleanliness/ hygiene and suboptimal infrastructure in schools.
* Reinforce school health activities/ services to children with special needs.

# 3. Policy Directions

## 3.1 Vision

All Seychellois children achieve their full development potential in life.

## 3.2 Mission

Work with school-going children, their parents/guardians, school staff and other stakeholders to create and maintain a conducive learning environment that supports healthy living, quality education and optimal child development.

## 3.3 Objectives

The specific objectives are to:

1. Create and foster sustainable and conducive school environments that protect and promote the health and well-being of children and support learning and optimal child development.
2. Improve availability and access to health education, health skills building, and promotive, preventive, and rehabilitative health services to the children.
3. Engage all stakeholders to uphold a culture of healthy living, teaching and learning in the school environment.
4. Integrate health emergencies prevention, preparedness and response into relevant sector/school disaster response plans.
5. Invest to support optimal child health and development to secure the future of the next generation.

## 3.4 Policy values

The core values of the National School Health Policy 2023 are the same as those of the National Health Policy 2015. These are listed below.

1. **Caring**: We consider health as our business and caring as our vocation.
2. **Development**: We shall always develop the health staff and educators to lead our people to healthier lifestyles.
3. **Excellence**: Anything we do now, we shall try to do it better the next time.
4. **Partnership**: We believe in a perpetual partnership with our stakeholders.
5. **People**: We value our health workforce as our most important asset.
6. **Productivity**: We shall strive untiringly to deliver both quality and quantity in health care.
7. **Professionalism**: We believe that skills professionalism is a sine qua non for health improvement.
8. **Reward and Teamwork**: We believe that teamwork brings about better results and that team achievements bring rewards to everyone.
9. **Service**: We shall serve with both our heart and our mind.

## 3.5 Principles

The policy upholds the following guiding principles:
1. Holistic government approach to school health
2. A whole-school approach
3. Targeted and timely interventions, and
4. Meaningful engagement of parents, students, teachers, the community and other stakeholders.
5. Leave no one behind.

#### Holistic approach to health

The Policy recognizes that health is an interaction of physical, mental, emotional and social factors. Thus, its approach is to improve social determinants of health and to focus on all aspects of health; hence, the need for the school health nurse to work closely with the school counsellor, social worker, school staff and other actors to ensure that health is seen and treated in its widest sense possible.

The Policy recognizes and seeks to align itself with the international commitments made by the Government through the ratification of human rights instruments, and regional economic groups’ protocols, as well as the various sectoral national policies from different ministries and agencies. These international and national obligations are outlined in **Annex 2: National laws and policies**. It is essential that proposed guidelines are coordinated and that each national policy finds its place in the overall picture of ensuring that health in the schools is seen, treated and implemented in a comprehensive manner.

#### Whole-school approach

The NSHP will be implemented in a comprehensive manner that takes into account the needs of the various partners and stakeholders. Piece-meal programs do not fully cover the needs of its intended beneficiaries and end up responding to only some of the needs identified and expressed.

To ensure success in its implementation, the NSHP considers issues such as the culture of the Seychellois, the religious particularities and sensitivities of the community.

#### Targeted and timely interventions

The NSHP seeks to implement a well-designed School Health Program which will focus on early identification of health issues in all its dimensions and thus, is preventive in nature. When there are rehabilitative interventions to undertake, these will be done in a timely manner taking into consideration of needs, to avoid a one size fits all approach. It is expected that timely referrals will be done in a two-way manner, with regular feedbacks from all parties. Thus, interventions need to be based on identified needs and evidence-informed decisions and they are just enough of what the community needs, rather than an overkill.

The Program will ensure that students with physical impairments or mental health conditions will be included as fully involved participants. Programs will be developed together with them and their inputs considered primordial to the whole process.

#### Meaningful engagement of all partners

The NSHP seeks to meaningfully engage all stakeholders in the whole process, from conception to design, from implementation to monitoring and evaluation. It is important to especially engage the parents, teachers, students and the community in the complete process and procedures used to develop programs and interventions.

Meaningful engagement can be at different levels, from providing technical, material and financial support to the MoE and the MoH and to the schools directly. Stakeholders may choose to donate whatever they are able to: time, effort, funds, human resources and others to ensure that their roles are truly participatory. The actions and interventions to support the NSHP must be inclusive and adopt the “leave no-one behind” principle involving all students, staff of the school, parents, the community and other stakeholders.

#### Leave no one behind

Lastly, the importance of taking needs of people with disabilities and from vulnerable and deprived backgrounds into consideration is emphasised. Relevant additional supports need to be provided to ensure that these groups are not left behind, to ensure equity in educational and developmental outcomes across all population groups.

## 3.6 Policy statements

### Objective One: Health-supportive school environment

**Create and foster sustainable and conducive school environments that protect and promote the health and well-being of children and support learning and optimal child development.**

Therefore, schools shall address the following issues:

1. **A.** MoE shall maintain cleanliness in all schools and monitor with the support of relevant partners.
2. **B.** MoE shall ensure safety in all schools, including when engaging in physical activities and sports, with adequate supervision, and removal of any physical hazard.
3. **C.** MoE shall endeavour to maintain and improve services, which provide nutrition for school children, such as dining halls, tuck shops and other facilities.
4. **D.** MoE shall maintain security in all schools.
1.E. MoE shall emphasize more on high-quality physical education lessons, active learning and physical activities in schools, that are age-appropriate and accessible for people with disabilities.

1.F. MoE shall ensure that adequate and appropriate venues/ infrastructure are available for high-quality physical education.

1.G. The MoE shall phase out all non-school-sanctioned takeaways and refreshments in accordance with the School Meal Guidelines.

1.H. MoE shall ensure that all school curricula are aligned with a health-promoting lifestyle and school environment.

1.I. MoH and MoE shall work in partnership with other relevant partners to develop and enact regulations to enforce minimum distances between all schools and sales outlets for health-detrimental products (including unhealthy food and drinks, alcohol and tobacco).

1.J. Schools shall make provisions to ensure adequate ventilation and acceptable ambient temperatures are maintained in classrooms, to ensure an optimal learning environment.

1.K The MoE shall ensure that all schools infrastructure is built according to the Planning Act, cater for inclusivity of all children and staff living with functional impairment, maintain standards for public building.

1.K. Schools shall make provisions for easy and affordable access to safe potable water for students and staff; and adequate and acceptable sanitation facilities.

1.L. Schools shall implement and enforce relevant policies and legislation, to protect the physical and mental health of students and staff (e.g. anti-bullying policy) at all times.

1.M. Schools shall foster a conducive environment to encourage, engage and promote positive interactions between students and staff, to nurture optimal academic, physical, mental, social and cultural development.

1.O. Schools shall ensure that the privacy and dignity of students and staff are respected at all times.

1.P. Schools shall endeavour to involve parents in all school health related activities.

### Objective Two: Improve access to quality school health services

**Improve availability and access to health education, health skills building, and promotive, preventive, and rehabilitative health services to the children.**

The MOH and its partners shall deliver comprehensive school health services guided by this policy, as well as the Primary Healthcare Package (MOH, 2020) comprising of; but not limited to:

* **2.A.** Health Promotion (health information and education, counselling, and health skills building).
* **2.B.** Health screening (as per Annex 3: School Health Screening Assessment), including referrals to further indicated services (e.g. nutrition, oral health, audiology screening and psychologists).
* **2.C.** Immunization services (as per National immunisation schedule).
* **2.D.** Public health services: covers water access, sanitation, hygiene, and health emergencies preparedness and response.
* **2.E.** Foster linkages to relevant programmes to help address key health issues, such as (non-exhaustive):
    * Malnutrition (Nutrition Unit).
    * Alcohol and substance abuse services (DSAPTR).
    * Tobacco use (UPCCD).
    * Sexual health (sexual and reproductive health programme).
    * Mental health issues (mental health services, mental health programme).
    * Child abuse (child protection services, social workers).
    * Developmental delays (early childhood intervention centre).
    * Hearing (e.g. Audiologists).
    * Vision (e.g. eye clinic, optometrists).
    * Speech (e.g. speech pathologists).
* Physical disabilities (e.g. occupational health services).

2.F Support children (and staff) with chronic health conditions and special health needs, in the school setting and beyond. Coordinate with other relevant care providers (such as Paediatricians and Rehabilitation services) to ensure the most supportive school environment feasible for the child.

2.G MOH, MOE and Department responsible for social affairs shall ensure early identification of developmental delays, learning failures, health conditions and social problems in school-going children, with appropriate referral to support services.

2.H Build capacity in school personnel (especially teachers and school management staff) to identify issues and initiate appropriate responses within their expertise.

2.I Support the provision of social and other health related professional services as needed in the school setting.

2.J All schools and school health services shall apply an equity lens to provision of health services, ensuring extra efforts to help and support children from deprived or vulnerable backgrounds, and those with disability.

2.K All schools shall initiate and adopt the health-promoting schools guideline (2019), to supplement and complement the school health programme.

2.L The School Health Services shall be promoted to stakeholders and adapted for all children, including children with special needs.

2.M The School Health Services and PSCE curriculum shall be periodically reviewed, in partnership with key stakeholders, to tailor it to evolving needs of school children.

### Objective Three: Engage stakeholders to support school health

**Engage all stakeholders to uphold a culture of healthy living, teaching and learning in the school environment.**

Therefore;

3.A Leverage of internal partners (school children, staff and parents) to support implementation of the school health programme and the health-promoting schools initiative.

3.B Bolster support of community leaders and community organisations to drive changes that ensure a health-supportive school community environment.

3.C Schools shall engage internal and external stakeholders to conceptualize, design, implement and monitor health behaviour change initiatives for the school community.

3.D Schools shall provide training to build staff knowledge on health and health-skills.

3.E The MOE, in collaboration with MOH, shall include health-related topics in relevant school curricula, including the PSCE and PE curricula, with relevant capacity building for staff.

3.F PSCE and PE shall become examinable subjects.

3.G MOE in partnership with relevant partners shall develop a formal training programme for PE teachers.

3.H The School Health Programme shall support external partners, such as the Ministry responsible for Youth, Sports and Family, to implement relevant health-supportive activities in school-settings.

3.I All relevant stakeholders shall support the implementation of the Comprehensive Sexuality Education Programme in schools.

### Objective Four: Education System Security, Safety and Resilience

**Integrate disaster risk reduction and health emergencies prevention, preparedness and response into relevant sector/school disaster response plans.**

Therefore:

4.A Schools shall integrate health emergencies prevention, preparedness and response into wider crises or disaster response plans.
4.B MOE, in partnership with relevant authorities, shall carry out periodic risk assessments in all educational settings, for fire risk, flooding risk, and other hazards.

4.C The MOE, in collaboration with relevant partners such as MOH, Disaster Risk Management Division (DRMD) and Ministry responsible for environment and climate change, shall make available guidelines, and provide relevant school-based teams with appropriate skills, equipment and knowledge, to ensure effective implementation of emergency response plans.

### Objective Five: Invest in Children

**Invest to support optimal child health and development to secure the future of the next generation.**

Therefore:

5.A The MOE and MOH shall explore the potential feasibility for introduction of health promotion officers in schools to complement school health nurses and teaching staff.

5.B The MOH shall endeavour to ensure adequate numbers of school health nurses, with appropriate capacity-building and maximise their availability for school health activities. Where capacity is limited, explore supports from health care assistants for school health programme.

5.C The school health programme shall ensure coordination and delivery of continuous professional development (CPD) services for all school health nurses.

5.D The MOE shall involve school health nurses systematically (e.g. through representation in relevant committees) to inform school-level decision-making in matters with implications for health of school children, and the school health programme manager in relevant sector-level decision-making.

5.E MOH and MOE shall ensure adequate funding for provision of staffing, equipment (medical equipment, vaccines, documentation, educational materials, Information Technology equipment and software), infrastructure (designated room, hall for Physical Education, utilities), and ad-hoc project-specific funds, tied to appropriate monitoring and evaluation, and outputs and performance indicators.

# 5. Coordinating Mechanism

The Health Promoting School Committee will be responsible to oversee, monitor and evaluate the National School Health Policy 2023. Members of the Committee will comprise of officials from the Ministry of Education and Ministry of Health. Other organizations will be co-opted as required.

The members of the Health Promoting School Committee will ensure that decisions of the Committee are communicated to their respective organizations for improved coordination and coherence.

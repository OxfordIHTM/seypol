# Seychelles National Plan for People With Dementia

## The Current and Foreseeable Future Impact of Dementia in Seychelles

Dementia is a general term used to describe a group of conditions characterized by the *loss of memory and other cognitive abilities, severe enough to interfere with daily life*. This might include changes in language, problem-solving, or the ability to recognize or identify familiar people, places, or objects.

Alzheimer's disease is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60-80% of cases, and vascular dementia is the next most common at around 17% of cases.

The Alzheimer's Association identifies ten early signs and symptoms of dementia:

1. Memory loss that disrupts daily life;
2. Challenges in planning or solving problems;
3. Difficulty completing familiar tasks at home, at work, or at leisure;
4. Confusion with time or place;
5. Trouble understanding visual images and spatial relationships;
6. New problems with words in speaking or writing;
7. Misplacing things and losing the ability to retrace steps;
8. Decreased or poor judgment;
9. Withdrawal from work or social activities; and
10. Changes in mood and personality.

## Risk Factors for Dementia

The impact of Alzheimer's disease is already being felt across Seychelles - on individuals and families, as they bear the greatest emotional and financial responsibility, on businesses through lost productivity of family caregivers and state-funded caregivers, and on local communities and the State, which incur significant costs for care and services. And, the greatest challenges are yet to come.

While Alzheimer's disease is not a normal part of aging, age is the greatest risk factor for developing Alzheimer's or other dementia. The risk of developing Alzheimer's increases exponentially after age 65. With our ageing population, the prevalence of Alzheimer's disease is predicted to increase in coming years.

Poor lifestyle (smoking, alcohol abuse, and lack of physical activity) and other co-existing medical conditions (diabetes, hypertension, depression and obesity) are other risk factors which increase chances of developing Alzheimer's disease. A family history of Alzheimer's and genetic influences associated with this also increase risk.

## Trends and Service Needs of Alzheimer's Population

While the greatest risk factor is advancing age, many older adults who are in their nineties
do not have Alzheimer's disease. And while family history and associated genetic influences
are important predictors, many family members of those with Alzheimer's will not develop
the condition. This makes it important to identify and address other disease modifying
factors.

Research is starting to reveal clues about risk factors that we may be able to alter through
lifestyle, wellness, and managing other health conditions – such as head trauma, the heart-
head connection, and healthy aging. Many healthy lifestyle and chronic disease
management strategies are also good for healthy aging and may directly protect against
cognitive decline and Alzheimer's disease.

The population of Seychelles is estimated at 95,843 people as of November 2017. Latest
figures show that there are 15,052 persons above 60 years of age, which represents 16% of
the total population. It is difficult to make estimates on the number of people with
dementia in Seychelles, given small population, demographic factors and high income.

## Mortality from Alzheimer's disease

Alzheimer's disease is among leading causes of death. Whereas globally death rates of
cancer, stroke and heart disease have declined, the death rate for Alzheimer's is on the rise.
Currently, an estimated more than 500 people in Seychelles have Alzheimer's or other forms
of dementias may be expected to double by 2040.

However, it is difficult to determine exactly how many deaths are caused by Alzheimer's
each year, because of the way causes of death are recorded on death certificates. It is likely
that Alzheimer's is a contributing cause of death in many cases, but is not recorded, making
any official data vast underestimates. The level of detail for collecting and analyzing data on
deaths can vary widely between countries.

## Impact on Individuals

Studies suggest that people 65 and over, live on average four to eight years after a diagnosis
of Alzheimer's disease, but some survive as long as 20 years. The progressive nature of
dementia, its long duration, and effects on decision-making, self-care and behavior create
particular challenges for individuals.

In the early stages, declining memory may lead to challenges in managing work, finances or
health care issues, including taking medications. As the disease progresses, people with
dementia may also experience difficulty in initiating activities, decision making and
behavioral symptoms, such as depression and agitation.

Over time, they *depend more and more on family* or others, to help them with daily living.
More than 60% of people with dementia may *wander away* from home at some point in the
disease. And *falls* are more frequent for people with dementia – and one of the most
common reasons for hospitalization of people with Alzheimer's. Eventually, people with
dementia need total help with bathing, dressing, eating and toileting.

The symptoms of Alzheimer's and other dementias, such as impairments in memory,
judgment, and the ability to plan and carry out activities, create greater risks for *self-*

*neglect, malnutrition, injury, medication errors, financial exploitation, unmet care needs, and nursing home placement* – which is even greater for those who live alone.

Those with dementias who live alone and have no one who visits, helps, or checks in regularly are the most likely to go undetected and unsupported. Their unmet needs become clear only when they have a medical or other care-related crises, and emergency response from health care, law enforcement, or Social Service agencies has been required.

During the later stages of the disease, a large percentage of people with dementia can no longer live at home. A person with Alzheimer's will spend 40% of the total number of years with Alzheimer's in the most severe stage of the illness.

## Impact on Families and Care-givers

Dementia affects the whole family. Family members provide the vast majority of care for those living with dementia. Family members caring for a person with dementia in America, provide an average of 21.9 hours per week of care.

While most family members provide such care willingly and experience benefits through this role, for many the demands of caregiving lead to negative outcomes – *stress, health risks, depression, and financial strain*. The greatest impacts on dementia caregivers are increased stress, not enough time for one's self or family, and adverse impacts on family relationships. Findings also indicate that caregivers of people with dementia are twice as likely, as compared to caregivers of people without dementia, to experience a serious mental health condition such as *depression and/or anxiety*. They are also more likely than non- caregiving peers to report their health as fair or poor.

6
These emotional and physical impacts lead to increased health care costs for dementia
caregivers nationwide.

### Impact on Health Care Systems

As the number of people with Alzheimer's and other dementias increases, so will their
presence in health care systems, which will lead to an *increase in potentially avoidable
emergency department visits or preventable hospitalizations.*

People with Alzheimer's and other dementias are more likely to have other chronic
conditions than those without. Dementia thus influences quality of care of these co-
morbidities such as diabetes and hypertension, leading to more frequent visits to clinics and
hospitals.

### Impact on Government, Other Payers and Society

Dementia, including Alzheimer's disease is one of the costliest diseases to society. It poses a
*financial burden*, similar to that of heart disease and cancer. The largest component of costs
attributable to dementia is the *cost for institutional and home-based long-term supports* and
services.

Dementia also impacts employment and economy, as more family caregivers need to miss
work days, reduce work hours or leave their jobs completely.

## Stakeholders in Development of Services for Persons with Dementia
## and their Caregivers

While community based long-term supports are a critical (and the most costly) component
of dementia care, a much more comprehensive set of strategies to respond to dementia are
needed, as future needs expand. This includes a change in thinking in terms of medical care
for dementia and closer coordination between Ministry of Health, Ministry of Family Affairs,
relevant Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) and community health and social
services.

The state plays a major role in the administration and provision of long-term supports and
services for individuals with chronic illnesses or conditions and disabilities, including those
with Alzheimer's disease and other dementias of all stages, and their caregivers.

Seychelles have a long and strong history of providing an array of long-term social services
and supports, which are easily accessible and highly equitable. Providing a holistic
framework for care for the elderly suffering from dementia will allow individuals to choose
among settings of care most suitable for their circumstances.

### Elderly Council

The Council for the Elderly is a stakeholder with a strong potential for assisting with public
education and awareness-raising through a number of mechanisms. Additionally, they can
act as patient advocacy bodies, to highlight any issues faced by patients with dementia in
current and future health systems.

### Seychelles Alzheimer's Foundation

Recognizing that members of the Alzheimer's Foundation have been the key advocates for
creating awareness and pushing for improvements in dementia care, the foundation will
continue to be a key partner in the process of establishing any National action plans.

The body is a perfect platform through with public education on recognizing early dementia
and avenues for help can be conducted. It also constitutes a strong body for advocating for
the rights of patients with dementia.

The status of the foundation also allows for lobbying of Politicians and Governmental
Organizations to commit more resources to the care of the elderly, as well as facilitate
acquisition of additional finances from the private sector – a key bottle-neck to tackle for
the timely recognition of the vision of an improve care system for the elderly.

The Alzheimer's Foundation, through its International partners, can also help identify
avenues for training nursing staff who will be working in any facilities with patients suffering
from dementia.

## Development of National Policy Regarding Persons with Dementia,
## Including Alzheimer's Disease

The idea for developing a Dementia-care Action Plan for Seychelles followed lobbying and
work done by the Alzheimer's foundation to engage the Ministry of Health on the modern
aspects of dementia-care through thorough workshops for health professionals, public
education campaigns, as well as participation and networking with International bodies
involved in advocacy to improve dementia-care. The proposed steps for developing an
action plan are based on plans used in the state of Washington, in the United States, to set-
up the 'Washington State Plan to Address Alzheimer's Disease and Other Dementias'¹.
Permission to use this particular plan was obtained from the authors of Washington State
Plan, who are willing to cooperate and provide any kind of support to draft the plan for
Seychelles.

The state currently has limited policy language on Alzheimer's disease in legislation, relating
to required training on dementia for adult family homes and assisted living facilities.

country's commitment to prepare for Alzheimer's.

## The Importance of Integrating Alzheimer's/Dementia care into the National Healthcare System

Proposed state level health care reform should be focused on changes in the health system
that include a strengthened patient-centered medical home model concentrating on the
concept of chronic care management.

As health care system improvements evolve in Seychelles, it is critical that emerging data
and best practices around the identification, treatment and management of dementia and
co-occurring chronic illnesses are integrated into systems improvements and transformation
efforts at the state and local levels. Improvements must include the capacity to:

- Detect early signs and symptoms of cognitive impairment;
- Make and disclose a timely diagnosis;
- Promote advance planning for substitute decision-making and end-of-life care;
- Coordinate care for wellness, medical and support service needs;
- Provide early and ongoing education and support of family caregivers;
- Promote independent and active engagement of persons with dementia in the
  community.

dementia, it is necessary to create a working group to determine the needs of individuals
with Alzheimer's disease, the services available to meet these needs, and the capacity of the
state and providers to meet current and future challenges. The working group will also make
recommendations on needed policies and responses.

The working group should focus on specific topic areas to identify key issues and priority

At the heart of the group's mission will be to create a vision for the future, *on changing how

This vision includes critical *support for the person with dementia and their family*, shortly
after diagnosis. Family caregivers need a "road map", offering simplified information about
what to expect as the disease progresses and where to turn for help at any stage. Families

also need to know that others providing for their family members are doing so within

Our communities, Government, the private sector, civil society, religious bodies, health care,
and social service organizations need to be better prepared for the growing number of
elderly citizens, including those who will be affected by Alzheimer's or other dementias.

Community organizations need help providing public education about healthy ageing and
maintaining a healthy brain, the importance of early detection and diagnosis, and where to
turn for assistance. Together, we need to create more "dementia-friendly" communities
that promote engagement, well-being, and safety.

Families are and will remain the bedrock of support and long-term care for people with
Alzheimer's. The intent of the working group is that the concept of family be taken broadly
and be clearly integrated into any proposed action plan. Our rich cultural heritage provides
countless examples of families, partners, long-term companions, and relationships between
people with a common history, emotional bond and commitment to an individual's well-
being. In this document, family is essentially taken as any person who plays a significant role
in an individual's life.

The action plan will need to be consistent with ideas for a wider strategy on healthy ageing,
integrate into the broader vision and goals for health and social care in Seychelles, and align
with the National development agenda, as well as the Sustainable Development Agenda
2030.

*Vision: "Dementia-Capable" Communities*

Ensuring that state and local service systems are dementia-capable and friendly is critical.
Many who seek assistance from these systems have dementia or may have cognitive
disabilities resulting from other conditions. In addition, caregivers of people with dementia
regularly contact these systems seeking assistance in coping with their loved ones' special
needs. These needs relate to ever more dependence on others for help with daily activities,
short-term memory loss, impaired decision-making capacity, and behavioral symptoms. The
types of supports that people with dementia and their caregivers use include assistance
with planning for service needs, identifying dementia-capable services, counseling about
service options, and home and community services providers who understand dementia.

The key aspects of building a dementia-capable care system are:

1. Educate the public about brain health. This would include information about the risk
   factors associated with developing dementia, first signs of cognitive problems,
   management of symptoms if individuals have dementia, support programs, and
   opportunities to participate in research.
2. Identify people with possible dementia and recommend that they see a physician for
   a timely, accurate diagnosis and to rule out reversible causes of dementia or
   conditions that resemble it.

12
3. Ensure that program eligibility and resource allocation take into account the impact
   of cognitive disabilities.
4. Ensure that staff communicate effectively with people with dementia and their
   caregivers and provide services that: (a) Are person- and family-centered; (b) Offer
   self-direction of services; and (c) Are culturally appropriate.
5. Educate workers to identify possible dementia, and understand the symptoms of
   dementia and appropriate services.
6. Implement quality assurance systems that measure how effectively providers serve
   people with dementia and their caregivers.
7. Encourage development of dementia-friendly communities, which include key parts
   of dementia-capability.

## *Members of the working group*

Working group will be formed by the order of the Minister of Health. It should gather
representatives from all interested parties, who will be chosen after consultations with and
within the institutions and organizations previously identified as relevant and eventually
approved by the Minister of Health. The members of the working group are to define time
frames and frequencies of the meetings. Working group will be formed and supervised by
the minister of health. It will be presided by the chairman who will inform and consult
minister on the progress. Minutes will be made by the secretary of the working group.

Proposed members of the working group:

- Representative of Ministry of Health
- Representative of Health Care Agency
- Representative of Ministry of Family Affairs
- Representative of Elderly Council
- Geriatric nurse
- Psychiatrist
- Representative of Seychelles Alzheimer's Foundation
- Caregiver of a person with dementia

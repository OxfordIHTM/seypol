In crafting the new guidelines for Seychelles, considerations
have also been given to the nutritional transition. The shift
from predominantly unprocessed traditional foods such
as fish, locally produced fruit and vegetables, tubers,
roots and breadfruit, to a more westernised dietary intake
consisting mainly of refined and processed foods has also
contributed to the increase in obesity and its complications
such as non-communicable diseases (NCDs.
Reducing the risk or better management of obesity and
NCDs can be achieved by simple lifestyle changes. These
include eating more healthily, through the inclusion of
more local and diversified foods, reducing intake of ultra-
processed foods often high in sugar, saturated fat and
salt, reducing time spent sitting and increasing physical
activity in all age groups.
Tackling the issue of over-nutrition remains one of the
top priorities of the Government of Seychelles. This new
edition of the Food-Based Dietary Guidelines not only aims
to provide practical guidance on how to address some of
the root causes of obesity and its comorbidities, but also
how to prevent them in the first place.
The guidelines have been developed in conjunction with
the existing realities of the country from an economic,
social, cultural, environmental and agricultural perspective.
They are also in line with relevant and existing strategies,
plans and policies that are nutrition specific and nutrition
sensitive.

It is well documented that the first 1,000 days (which is
from pregnancy until two years of a child’s life) are critical
to lifelong nutrition outcomes of overweight, obesity and
stunting. It is for this reason that the revised guidelines
have a separate focus on children below five years as well
as pregnant and lactating women. This addresses the
limitations of the 2007 guidelines which included everyone
above two years of age.
With the continued burden of obesity and non-
communicable diseases in Seychelles amongst all age
groups, it is necessary to address the problem in the
very early ages of the human lifespan. Currently the
rates of obesity, gestational diabetes and type 2 diabetes
continues to increase amongst pregnant women, further
predisposing the child to obesity and its complications.
Healthy dietary practices during pre-conception and
pregnancy are therefore important to improve neonatal
outcomes.

Ranges of population nutrient intake goals
Population nutrient intake goals represent the population average intake that is judged to be consistent with the
maintenance of health in a population. Health, in this context, is marked by a low prevalence of diet-related diseases
in the population.
Dietary factor Goal (% of total energy, unless otherwise stated)
Total fat 15 - 30%
Saturated fatty acids <10%
Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) 6 - 10%
n-6 Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) 5 - 8%
n-3 Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) 1 - 2%
Trans fatty acids <1%
Monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) 10 - 12%a
Total carbohydrate 55 - 75%b
Free sugars c <10%
Protein 10 - 15%d
Cholesterol <300 mg per day
Sodium chloride (sodium)e <5 g per day (<2 g per day)
Fruits and vegetables ≥400 g per day
Total dietary fibre >25g/day
Non-starch polysaccharides (NSP) >20g/day
a This is calculated as: total fat - (saturated fatty acids + polyunsaturated fatty acids + trans fatty acids).
b The percentage of total energy available after taking into account that consumed as protein and fat,
hence the wide range.
c The term «free sugars» refers to all monosaccharides and disaccharides added to foods by the manufacturer,
cook or consumer, plus sugars naturally present in honey, syrups and fruit juices.
d The suggested range should
be seen in light of the Joint WHO/FAO/UNU Expert Consultation on Protein and Amino Acid Requirements in Human
Nutrition, held in Geneva from 9 to 16 April 2002.

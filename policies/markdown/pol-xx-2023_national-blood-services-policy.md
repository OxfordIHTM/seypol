FOREWORD
Blood saves life and the gift of blood is a philanthropic offering of the highest order.
Our country and our Ministry of Health take absolute pride in the fact that we have
always sought to consistently provide safe blood to all recipients and to a large
extent, we have succeeded in doing so with flying colours. Kudos goes, first and
foremost, to our generous voluntary blood donors.
Where we have not entirely succeeded thus far, is in permanently having adequate
supplies of all blood groups and blood products in the blood bank. Thankfully, every
flash appeal for blood donations, that we have made at critical times, have always
fallen on willing ears and our fellow citizens have come forward, often in droves, to
offer this profound gift of life.
Our modus operandi, as caregivers, is to be proactive rather than reactive, to be
ahead of the curve rather than behind it, to be ready rather than apologetic. This new
policy aims at ensuring that our country has adequate supplies of blood and blood
products at all times, night and day, in good times and bad times, so that we can
continue to provide safe blood on demand, to all residents and visitors in this
country, whenever and wherever they need it.
The government is closely watching the rapid evolution of the national health
system, with the increasing number of health service providers, some of which are
now venturing into post-basic or even advanced health care. This National Blood
Services Policy provides for a new front to be rapidly opened in the area of
legislation, regulation, and robust standard setting, all in the interest of patient safety
and higher quality of care.
As I talk of patient safety and higher quality of health care, I cannot omit to steer the
conversation also in the direction of prevention. A road traffic accident prevented
means less blood is needed to address its consequences. Health and safety at work
and play mean fewer misfortunes and less blood and blood products needed. Many
diseases that need blood transfusion can also be prevented by sustained preventive
actions at the level of the individual, the family and the community. If we continue to
work on prevention, early diagnosis and health and safety, less blood will need to be
transfused and whatever supplies we have will reach much further forward.
I thank all the voluntary blood donors who are ever ready to help our fellow citizens.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Blood and blood products is an important component of modern health care. Blood
transfusion may decrease mortality, and prolong and improve the quality of life of
many people with different pathologies. Timely access to safe blood products, and
the optimal use of such products, are essential for effective health service delivery
and the achievement of universal health coverage.
The Clinical Laboratory at the Seychelles Hospital has ran a blood transfusion services
unit for many years to respond to an increasing demand for blood and blood
products.
In 2001, the WHO Regional Office for Africa adopted a strategy for blood safety with
the aim of improving both the quality and adequacy of blood supply through
sustainable implementation of blood transfusion policies in all countries in the
region.
In line with WHO recommendations, Seychelles has developed this first National
Blood Services policy to guide the organisation of blood services, and address many
of the challenges and gaps in the existing service.
The ultimate aim is to ensure universal access to, and rational use of safe blood and
blood products, for the people of Seychelles. The policy statements are set out under
the following priority directions:
1. Establish an appropriately structured, well-coordinated and sustainably
resourced national blood system.
2. Establish appropriate national framework of regulatory controls, standards
and quality assessment programmes.
3. Ensure well-functioning and efficiently managed blood services.
4. Implement effective patient blood management to optimize clinical practice of
transfusion.
5. Implement effective surveillance, haemovigilance and pharmacovigilance,
supported by comprehensive and accurate data collection systems.
6. Build active partnerships to jointly address existing challenges and emerging
global threats.
Required implementation arrangements are outlined for effective service delivery
and defines key indicators to support effective monitoring and evaluation of the
service.
5

The policy also provides recommendations for the sustainability and better
institutionalisation of this crucial service situation through the provision of relevant
infrastructure, governance mechanisms, human resources, funding and monitoring
and evaluation.
Ministry of Health will ensure all the recommendations of this policy are
implemented and will develop a strategic framework to strengthen the national
blood transfusion services to ensure continuous, sustainable and quality blood and
blood products.

GLOSSARY
Autologous Blood Transfusion: Blood or blood components collected from one individual that are
intended solely for later transfusion or any human applications for the same individual.
Blood Components: A blood constituent that can include plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells
and platelets.
Blood Donor: A person who gives blood for transfusion.
Blood Products: Components that can be derived from donated blood and transfused individually. This
includes red cells, platelets and plasma.
Blood Transfusion: A potentially life-saving medical procedure in which compatible donated blood is
provided to a receiver in-need, through an intravenous infusion.
Cryoprecipitate: Cryoprecipitate is the part of plasma that contains a number of clotting proteins that
help control bleeding made by thawing fresh frozen plasma between 1 and 6˚C and then collecting
the insoluble proteins. This is then refrozen for storage and thawed again when someone needs it.
Fresh Frozen Plasma: A blood product most commonly made by separating plasma from whole blood
collection and placing it in freezing conditions (-18°C or less).
Family Replacement Donor: A donor who gives blood when a member of the donor’s family or
community requires it.
Haemovigilance: Haemovigilance is a set of surveillance procedures covering the entire transfusion
chain, from the donation and processing of blood and its components, to their provision and
transfusion to patients and their follow-up.
Packed Red Cell concentrate: Packed red blood cells (PRBCs) are made from a unit of whole blood by
centrifugation and removal of most of the plasma, leaving a unit with a haematocrit of about 60%.
Plasma Derived Medicinal Products: Plasma-derived medicinal products are prepared industrially from
human plasma by pharmaceutical companies and include products such as albumin, coagulation
factors and immunoglobulins, which are life-saving therapeutics.
Platelets Concentrates: Separation of whole blood to get platelets that are small, colourless cell
fragments in our blood that form clots and stop or prevent bleeding.
Quality Management System: The overall process of ensuring that the National Blood Service operates
in a way that ensures maximum quality in all aspects of its operations.
Regular donor: Donor who gives blood on a regular basis (at least twice per year).
Transfusion Transmitted Infections (TTIs): Infections resulting from the introduction of a pathogen into
a person through blood transfusion.
Voluntary Non-Remunerated Blood Donors (VNRBDs): A donor giving blood, plasma or cellular
components on his/her own free will and receives no payment for it, either in cash or in kind.
Whole Blood: Whole blood is made up of red and white blood cells, and platelets, all of which are
suspended in plasma. It is collected in a standard blood donation.

1. INTRODUCTION
Blood and blood products is an important component of modern health care. Blood
transfusion may decrease mortality, and prolong and improve the quality of life of
many people with different pathologies. When the supply of these products is
insufficient or unsafe, the effectiveness of key health services and programmes to
provide appropriate care to patients who need these products may be negatively
impacted.
Timely access to safe blood products, and the optimal use of such products, are
essential for effective health service delivery and the achievement of universal health
coverage. The safety of the donor in the blood donation process is as important as
the safety and efficacy of the blood and blood products themselves.
1.1 Background
Seychelles provides comprehensive health services, including blood transfusion
services, to all citizens, free at point-of-use. The National Universal Health Coverage
(UHC) service coverage index is estimated at 71, and reflects good access to essential
health services. Seychelles has not had a formal blood services policy in the past, but
the service has strived to follow WHO recommendations and utilise internal
standards and operating procedures.
Blood transfusion services in Seychelles have been operational since at least the
1970s, and at its current location since 1987, attached to the Clinical Laboratory.
Initially, only whole blood was available for transfusion, however, production of
different blood components was made possible as the service evolved, with the
acquisition of specific equipment and training of personnel in this field.
The World Health Organisation (WHO) has urged Member States to formulate
national blood policies to ensure blood safety and ethical standards in the practice of
blood transfusion. The World Health Assembly (WHA) and the Regional Committee
for Africa have endorsed various resolutions to promote safe use of blood and blood
products (Table 1).
12

In 2005, an annual World Blood Donor Day1 was established – it is celebrated on 14
June each year, and this day is an integral part of the national blood donor
recruitment programme.
Formulation and subsequent implementation of this NaBS policy is a key step
towards guiding further quality improvement in the blood services.

1.2 Policy Context
The right to health is a fundamental right of every human being. Article 29 of the
Constitution of Seychelles spells out the right to (and responsibility towards) health
of every citizen of Seychelles, with a role for the state to provide essential health
services.2
The National Development Strategy (NDS), 2019-2023, provides strategic guidance
to achieve ‘Vision 2033’, through work across six key pillars: Good Governance;
People at the Centre of Development; Social Cohesion; Innovative Economy;
Economic Transformation and Environmental Sustainability; and Resilience.
Improvement of health services falls primarily under pillar 2 of the NDS: people at the
centre of development, and Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3: good health and
wellbeing.
The NDS pillar 2 has two broad strategic priorities: firstly, to pursue effective health
protection, promote empowerment and personal and societal responsibility for
holistic health, and, secondly, to build a high quality integrated healthcare system
with a focus on people-centred care.
The national vision for health is the attainment, by all people in Seychelles of the
highest level of physical, social, mental and spiritual health and living in harmony
with nature. The National Health Policy (2015) reaffirms this vision and positions
health at the centre of national development, both as a beneficiary of and a
contributor to socioeconomic development.

2. SITUATION ANALYSIS
2.1 Organisation of Current Blood Transfusion Services
The Blood Transfusion and Serology Unit of the Clinical Laboratory delivers Blood
Transfusion Services (BTS) in Seychelles. The Clinical Laboratory is led by the Chief
Technologist, who reports to the Director of Seychelles Hospital, and there on to the
Chief Executive Officer of the HCA. A Head of Unit oversees daily operations of the
BTS.
Infrastructure
There is one blood donation centre located at the Seychelles Hospital, which also
houses the blood bank for the country, supported by a mobile blood donation
vehicle. The unit is well equipped with basic equipment required to collect blood in a
safe manner as per standard procedures, and for production of blood components.
Comfortable donor chairs are available to accommodate three donors at any one
time and portable donor chairs are available for donation outside the BTC. The MOH
has in-house biomedical engineers that provide support in terms of preventive
maintenance and as and when repairs are required.
The infrastructure is out-dated, with limited space, and with sharing of workspace
with certain other service areas. This presents certain challenges such as for effective
physical organisation of services, often leads to overcrowding during periods of
higher demand (e.g. during blood donation drives), and presents difficulties to
ensure adequate privacy for donors.

Despite the expanding range of blood services offered and the increasing clinical
demand for blood products, staffing at the unit has remained relatively constant.
Service-specific organograms are not defined to-date. Present staffing and key
responsibilities can be summarised as in Table 2.

Information Systems
There is a basic blood bank software in use, which acts as a register for donors,
generates a unique number for labelling of each blood unit upon successful
collection, maintains records of donations, and provides summary statistics for
routine reporting. The blood transfusion services team also maintain a database of
donors.
2.2 Blood Donation
The BTC organizes on average between 30 to 40 blood drives annually, either at the
BTC or outside the compound of the hospital. Blood drives are a major component
to ensure there are sufficient blood in the bank, and are organised in collaboration
with private organizations, schools, associations, religious groups, ministries and
departments and other non-governmental bodies.
On average, 1500 to 1900 units of blood are collected annually; in 2021, 1849 units
of blood were collected (Figure 1). More males come forward to donate blood; they
make up 70% of the total donors. The highest number of donors are aged between
30 to 39 years. Local protocols allow people up to the age of 65 years to donate
blood, although some can donate up to 70 years if they are still in good health. The
most common blood group collected is group O+.

Recruiting blood donors is a challenge in Seychelles and strategic efforts are required
to increase voluntary donation, and eventually decrease family replacement. Since
blood donation is purely on a voluntary basis and is non-remunerated, retention
strategies are required to ensure donors keep giving blood on a regular basis.
Current recruitment strategies include advertisements using social media, TV and
newspapers, presentations in schools and organizations to sensitise on blood
donation, and participating in TV programmes such as Bonzour Sesel.
There are also direct engagements with private sector organisations and non-
governmental organisations, to recruit donors and support public sensitisation. As
much as possible, subject to sponsorship, a small token such as T. shirts, mugs, caps
amongst others are given to the blood donors after donating blood as an
encouragement. The tokens are usually printed with messages about blood donation.
As described in Table 3, about half of donors are voluntary non-remunerated donors
(VNRBDs) in 2020. The other half are from family replacement donors who only come
to donate blood specifically for a patient who is either a relative or a friend.
Certain challenges exist with (non-regular) family replacement donors, as their self-
account of past medical and personal history may not always be reliable, which may
constitute a higher risk for transfusion-transmissible infections. In the past decade,
prevalence of HIV and Viral hepatitis C, both transfusion-transmissible infections,
have been increasing in the population, particularly in younger age groups4.
To-date, the number of people who participate in autologous blood donation
remains low (less than 1% of total blood donations). This is at least partly due to this

2.3 Quality Management
Draft standard operating procedures exist for daily operations of the BTS.
Introduction of more comprehensive quality management system is a gap that can
possibly be addressed, with external technical assistance.
2.4 Blood Supply, Storage and Transport
The BTC is equipped with sufficient blood bank capacity to store 500 units of whole
blood and blood components at a time – a capacity that is more than adequate for
current levels of demand. The capacity for storing FFP is 250 units at -200C. Platelet
agitators available can store up to 48 units of platelets.
The BTC supplies blood to the various government hospitals on Mahé, Praslin and
La-Digue, based on demand. The transportation of blood to the inner islands takes
place at the optimal temperature, in minimal length of time, and maintaining the
cold chain until it reaches its destination.
2.5 Blood Processing
All blood collected undergo screening for HIV, viral hepatitis B and C, HTLV-1 and
syphilis. There are no reports of documented transfusion-transmitted infections
(TTIs).
Until recently, the majority of transfusions were with whole blood. Production of
blood components began in 2011, after one Technician attended appropriate
training and the required equipment was installed at the main laboratory for
component production of Red Blood Cell Concentrate and platelets.
The BTC currently manufactures Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP), Packed Red Cell
concentrate and Platelets, and continues to offer whole blood. Blood grouping and
cross match prior to transfusion is done by trained and competent laboratory
personnel. Figure 2 provides an overview of the blood component production by
year since 2011.

2.6 Use of Blood and Blood Products
The demand for blood and blood components has gradually increased over the last
decade, and particularly over the last three years from 2019 to 2021 (Figure 3). This
increase has primarily been due to the expansion of clinical specialties at the
Seychelles Hospital, and provision of more specialized treatment options for patients
suffering from blood related disorders. Packed Red Cell concentrate remains the
product that is most commonly requested (Figure 2).
A significant increase in demand for blood components is noted over the past five
years. The Intensive Care Unit is the unit with the highest and most rapid increase in
demand for blood, followed by the Hermitte Ward (female surgical ward). Some
severe cases of Covid-19 were also assisted with blood transfusion at the Family
Hospital and New Coast Guard Isolation facilities (Figure 4).
Although there are no National guidelines on clinical use of blood, blood and blood
products are used based on disease- or condition-specific guidelines and protocols.
Standard operating procedures exist for blood transfusion in a clinical setting,
with relevant documentation in patient medical records.

2.9 Emerging Policy Priorities
The following key priorities/needs were identified based on the situation analysis and
SWOT analysis:
1. Upgrade and expand the unit responsible for the provision of blood services,
with clearly defined infrastructure, human resources and a dedicated budget
line.
2. Ensure effective leadership, governance and management for the
development of a sustainable National blood service.
3. Develop robust legislative and regulatory framework to guide blood donation
and transfusion practices in Seychelles.
4. Ensure sustainable donor education and motivation, and expanded voluntary
non-remunerated blood donor recruitment and retention programmes.
5. Develop strong quality management system for blood banking processes:
from donor recruitment through to post-transfusion, aligning it with
international standards.
6. Develop and implement practical and clear guidelines on the clinical use of
blood and blood products (including indications, SOPs and recording/
reporting tools), to guide rational and safe use of blood and blood products.
7. Develop and implement strong haemovigilance guidelines and practices.
8. Develop strong partnerships with local and international organisations to
support donor recruitment, and capacity building of the service.

4. POLICY FRAMEWORK
4.1 The Vision
The vision is to have a sustainable and quality National Blood Services (NaBS) that
complies with international standards and meets the increasing demand for blood
and blood products for the health system of Seychelles.
4.2 The Mission
The mission of the NaBS is to provide adequate and timely supplies of safe blood
and blood products for use, aligned with guidelines for clinical use of blood.
4.3 Main Objectives
The NaBS shall ensure the safety, quality, accessibility and timely availability of blood
and blood products to meet the needs of all patients who require transfusion.
Therefore, the main objectives of this Policy are:
1. To strengthen leadership, governance and management of the NaBS.
2. To develop a new and modern structure, fully integrated into the
healthcare system.
3. To enable sustainable financing and human resources for an effective
NaBS.
4. To ensure the safety, quality and availability of blood and blood products.
5. To ensure the proper, effective and ethical use of blood and blood
products.
4.4 Guiding Principles
The NaBS policy upholds the following guiding principles:
1. A good blood policy should facilitate the provision of safe and adequate
blood and blood products for the population.
2. There is no effective or acceptable alternative to human blood for
therapeutic use.
3. Human blood and blood products are scarce national resources and must
be obtained and utilised equitably at all times and used in the most
effective and efficient manner.
4. The safety of blood transfusion recipients, blood donors and healthcare
professionals shall be given the utmost consideration at all times.
25

5. The collection and administration of blood must protect and promote the
health of not only the recipient but also the donor.
6. The appropriate clinical use of blood and blood products at all times.
7. Blood from regular, voluntary, non-remunerated blood donors, from low-
risk groups is the safest for transfusion practices.
8. A national, well-organised, coordinated and regulated blood service is the
best way to achieve the desired goals.

5. POLICY STATEMENTS
The key policy statements are described under five policy directions (PDs), adapted
from the WHO Action Framework for 2020-20235.
PD 1: Establish an appropriately structured, well-coordinated and
sustainably resourced national blood system
 The NaBS shall be the National blood bank service in Seychelles.
 MOH and HCA will guide the establishment of a clear structure for the NaBS.
As part of the structure, the following shall be clearly defined:
o Infrastructure for blood collection, testing, processing and storage;
o Key functions of the Unit;
o Human resource needs; and
o Budget line.
 A multi-disciplinary Hospital Transfusion Committee (HTC) shall be set up to
provide clinical governance for ensuring safe and appropriate processes and
practices in the NaBS, in accordance with guidelines for the clinical use of
blood.
 Ensure external assessment of blood regulatory system maturity.
 The NaBS shall be fully funded by the government, under the HCA’s budget;
however, it can solicit the assistance of international agencies and external
donors in some capital projects such as provision of infrastructure and
equipment.
 The NaBS shall be organised in a manner that ensures cost-effective use of all
resources.
PD 2: Establish appropriate national framework of regulatory controls,
standards and quality assessment programmes
 The highest standards of care shall be given to all prospective donors, by
appropriately trained professional staff in a congenial environment.
 Through the public health authority (PHA), develop and enforce appropriate
regulatory requirements for the collection and use of blood and blood
products, and related equipment and diagnostics, in Seychelles.
 Ensure regular external quality assessments shall be conducted.

 The NaBS shall strive to comply with all national policies and international
recommendations, to ensure the implementation of standards for the
provision of safe blood and blood products.
 Blood for transfusion will be screened for TTIs such as Hepatitis B and C, HIV,
HTLV-1, syphilis and any other transfusion transmissible agents that may be
recommended by HTC/WHO. The list of infectious agents to be screened and
relevant laboratory techniques and blood typing will be included in a technical
manual for the NaBS.
 The NaBS shall ensure effective cold chain for safe storage and distribution of
blood and blood products.
 HCA shall develop Memorandum of Understanding with private health
facilities that meet required standards and reporting requirements, for access
to blood and blood components as indicated by guidelines for clinical use.
 The NaBS/HCA shall ensure that quality assurance systems (including with
external partners/regulatory bodies) are applied at all levels of supply,
processing and use of blood and blood products, including clinical transfusion
process (standardised procedures, blood request forms, and labels and
records).
PD 3: Ensure well-functioning and efficiently managed blood services
 The NaBS shall ensure the availability of safe blood, blood components and its
products at all times, for transfusion and other clinical uses.
 The HCA shall ensure the provision of relevant infrastructure, equipment and
consumables needed for the proper running of the NaBS, as part of the
Seychelles Hospital Master Plan.
o Appropriate equipment shall be installed to ensure that recommended
conditions of storage for each blood component are met.
 The HCA shall ensure the provision of competent and adequate work force for
the NaBS to meet the blood service needs of modern medical practice.
o All staff in the NaBS such as donor recruiters shall be adequately
trained and follow continuous professional development.
 The NaBS shall encourage a system of blood donor recruitment based on
VNRBDs. These donors will be encouraged to become regular donors and the
maximum frequency of donation shall be stipulated by relevant guidelines of
the NaBS.
28

o The NaBS shall engage the community, district health centres, media,
NGOs and the public sector, in public education, motivation and
recruitment of VNRBDs.
o Blood drives shall be organized periodically as a means to ensure
continuous blood supply to meet the demand.
o Develop and implement operational strategies to reduce family
replacement and move towards more VNRBDs.
 In view of scarcity of blood as a resource, the BTS shall take all necessary
measures to ensure the maximal utility of donated blood.
 MOH and its entities have the ultimate responsibility for ensuring an adequate
supply of safe blood and blood products and their safe and rational use.
PD 4: Implement effective patient blood management to optimize
clinical practice of transfusion
 HCA shall provide systems for safe blood collection, including donor selection,
deferral, care, notification, counselling and referral, in confidentiality.
o The NaBS shall ensure that only appropriately trained and competent
staff carry out compatibility testing and issuing of blood and blood
products using approved SOP.
o Preparation of blood components, testing and processing of blood
shall be done using the most appropriate and effective methodologies
and best laboratory practices.
o The NaBS shall ensure appropriate system for storage and handling of
blood units in blood banks and clinical areas.
o Ensure a positive donor and staff experience throughout the donation
process.
o Ensure patient involvement in blood management through information
and consent.
 Ensure an effective quality management system within which all activities are
performed in a quality-focused way and are continuously monitored.
 Modernise the blood service through introduction of relevant new evidence-
based practices, under guidance of the HTC.
 Appropriate equipment and consumables shall be made available to ensure
that blood components are prepared under the highest quality standards.
 The NaBS shall improve on the current system of labelling and identification
to have a uniform system for blood units and blood products.
29

 The HTC, in collaboration with partners, shall provide guidance through a
technical manual on:
o Clinical guidelines on the appropriate use of blood and blood products
o Collection of blood/ blood components for transfusion
o Pre-transfusion procedures
o Administering of blood/ blood components
o Monitoring of patients during transfusion
o Management and investigation of transfusion reactions
o Follow-up of patients’ post-transfusion
o Sensitize clinical hospital staff on the manual as and when necessary.
o HTC will plan and conduct dissemination and training of relevant staff
based on the technical manual.
PD 5: Implement effective surveillance, haemovigilance and
pharmacovigilance, supported by comprehensive and accurate data
collection systems
 Develop appropriate Monitoring & Evaluation framework.
 The NaBS shall establish an efficient data management system for its efficient
operation and planning purposes:
o Ensuring alignment and integration with the wider electronic health
information system (eHIS).
o The NaBS shall strengthen the information management system
encompassing all activities from blood donors to distribution of blood
and blood products, issue and transfusion of blood and blood products
to patients.
 Research shall be encouraged in all aspects of transfusion medicine based on
identified priorities in the local context.
o Collaborate with clinical service areas in relevant clinical research and
quality improvement.
 HCA will adopt a stepwise approach in establishing a haemovigilance system,
which will include appropriate reporting mechanisms to record and review all
blood transfusion reactions. Such incidents must be reported to the HTC for
investigation and reporting to the HCA management.
 Put in place processes to assess current and future blood needs.
 The HTC shall be responsible to ensure appropriate use of blood by
monitoring the adherence to guidelines and regularly reviewing blood
transfusion practices in the hospitals.
30

 HTC shall lead any investigations of reported transfusion reactions and/or
cases of TTIs linked with blood transfusions in Seychelles.
 Ensure efficient blood stock management for timely availability of compatible
blood and reduce wastage.
PD 6: Build active partnerships to jointly address existing challenges
and emerging global threats
 There shall be collaboration with relevant international organisations to
promote the highest standards of blood transfusion practices.
 The private sector and civil society will be sensitised on options to support the
NaBS through various donations and/or support programmes.
 Create and maintain partnership with international blood service standards
bodies to achieve and maintain international standards of practice.
 In the event of health-related emergencies where demand for blood and
blood products far outweighs supply, ad-hoc arrangements can be made for
acquisition of blood from external sources, under the relevant legislations, in
partnership with PHA and Department of Risk and Disaster Management
(DRDM).

7. MONITORING AND EVALUATION
Regular monitoring and evaluation are essential for the successful implementation of
the NaBS policy. A national Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) plan will be developed
and focal persons appointed to carry out this activity. This plan will include quarterly
monitoring with field visits and an annual evaluation. In addition, relevant research
initiatives will be undertaken to provide evidence for blood safety planning.
7.1 Data Collection and Reporting
Relevant blood safety indicators will be included in the Health Management
Information System. The following core blood safety indicators will be monitored:
1. Percentage of voluntary blood donations amongst all donations.
2. Blood donor deferral rate and reasons (e.g. low weight, low Hb, low or high
BP, other medical reasons, recent travel, other reasons)
3. Percentage of blood units reactive to/with confirmed TTIs
4. Total number of blood components prepared disaggregated by type
5. Proportion of blood collection separated into components
6. Proportion and reasons for discarding blood (incomplete collection, TTIs,
Expiry, issues with transport, storage or processing).
7. Proportion of transfusions by blood components
8. Number of reported transfusion reactions
9. Number of adverse reactions during blood donation
10. Participation in external quality assurance schemes.
11. Number of completed SOPs as fraction of identified SOPs needed.
Reporting will be through quarterly and annual National Blood Service Reports to the
HTC, HCA and MOH. Relevant information will also be disseminated to staff within
the NaBS and clinical areas. See Annex 1 for additional details on the indicators.
7.2 Regulatory Inspection and Control
Regulatory inspection and control mission of interventions resulting from this blood
policy will be entrusted to the regulatory units of the Public Health Authority.
7.3 Policy Review
Considering this will be the first NaBS policy, it is suggested that the NaBS policy be
reviewed after 5 years, or where indicated.

8. CONCLUSION
It is hoped that with the implementation of this policy framework, the NaBS will be
further institutionalised, and recruitment, donation, storage and clinical utilisation of
blood, will be more effective and efficient. This will bring about maximum benefits of
utilisation of available blood, at minimum risks. It lays the groundwork for preparing
(evidence-based) guidelines for clinical use of blood, which, in turn, will improve
clinical care and outcomes.
Additionally, the oversight committees proposed will help steer continuous service
improvement and develop additional capacities for the NaBS as indicated.

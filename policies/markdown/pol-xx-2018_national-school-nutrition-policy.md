Executive summary
The need for the revision of National School Nutrition Policy comes at an opportune
time, now that there is a huge number of products available on the market and
consumers often make unhealthy choices face to these situations. Adequate knowledge
on healthy eating pattern is crucial among the population now that the rates of obesity
is on the rise in both children and adults in the Seychelles. Based on latest results,
approximately 22% boys and girls are overweight and obese in the Seychelles. In
addition, childhood obesity tends to track in adulthood and more emphasis needs to
begin as early as childhood years.
In order to prevent or reverse the adverse health effects of overweight and poor eating
habits, interventions targeted at healthy nutrition need to occur early in childhood and
adolescence. Childhood and adolescence is a critical period as the physiological need
for nutrients is high relative to energy needs. A diet of high nutritional quality is
therefore particularly important. Additionally, eating habits, lifestyle and behaviour
patterns are developed and may persist throughout adulthood.
Schools provide the most effective way to reach large portions of the population,
including young people, school personnel, families and community members. It is a
perfect setting where students can be reached at influential stages in their lives, during
childhood and adolescence when lifelong nutritional patterns are formed. Schools offer
more effective, efficient and equal opportunities than any other setting to promote
health and healthy eating.
Healthy food and nutrition should be a high priority on every school agenda because of
the positive effects on child well-being. A vast evidence suggests healthy foods and
good nutrition improves learning ability, leading to better academic performance. In
order to improve children’s nutritional behavior patterns and knowledge, the
community must also participate actively in promoting health. And the best way to
achieve community participation is to collaborate with different sectors and
stakeholders. Parents and care givers are examples of important stakeholders and need

1 Introduction
1.1 What is the policy about?
A National School Nutrition Policy represents efforts to utilize schools as sites for
improving the nutritional well-being of all school children. It can provide a framework
for implementing nutrition strategies which would ensure that students receive
nutrition education messages that are reinforced throughout the whole of the school
environment. The school environment is known to powerfully influence students’
attitudes, preferences and behaviours and as such can promote and positively support
healthy eating.
The policy will be used to coordinate all aspects of school nutrition including the
curriculum, the school tuck shop, provision of school meals, and training of school
personnel, establishing linkages with families and school personnel as well as
evaluation. The policy will have a very wide reach, targeting not only students but also
teachers, school personnel, families and other community members, all of which are
directly or indirectly part of the school community.
1.2 Goals, objectives and outcomes
1.2.1 Goal
The main goal is to improve the nutritional well-being of school children in the
Seychelles through a school environment conducive to healthy eating.
1.2.2 Objectives
 To implement nutrition education from crèche through to secondary schools as
part of a sequential comprehensive school curriculum;
 To harmonize the school’s food provision and environment with nutrition
education in the curriculum;

2 Health and Nutrition of school children
2.1 Obesity
Overweight and obesity have increased drastically among the general population and
is no exception among children and adolescents. Obesity and overweight are
considered major public health concerns, and are described as a global epidemic
affecting many low-and-middle income countries, particularly in urban settings. The
prevalence has increased and keeps increasing at an alarming rate (World Health
Organization 2006)
Overweight and obese children are likely to stay obese into adulthood and more likely
to develop non-communicable diseases such as; diabetes and cardiovascular diseases
at a younger age. Additionally, it is emerging more convincingly that Type 2 diabetes
and Coronary Heart Disease begins in childhood, with childhood obesity being an
important factor.
2.2 Dental carries
Oral health is part of general health and wellbeing and contributes to the development
of a healthy child (Public Health England 2017). Additionally, tooth decay is the most
common oral disease affecting children and adolescents, however it is largely
preventable. There is enough evidence to show that sugar is the most important factor
in dental decay/caries. In 2010, the School Dental Service (SDS) conducted an in-service
study to assess the oral health status of 6, 12 and 15 year olds in all state schools and
the three main contributing factors that is dental visits, oral hygiene and dietary
practice. The results showed 75.4% of the 6 year olds have one or more Decayed,
Missing, Filled permanent teeth (DMFT) whereas 59% and 70.4% of the 12 and 15 year-
olds respectively have DMFT of one or more. This is an alarming results for a small
country as Seychelles and calls for decisions to control or even ban certain sugary
products constantly used by children.
Dental caries are highly prevalent in school children. The most important dietary cause
is sugar, particularly sucrose- found in confectionery, soft drinks, biscuits, cake, fruit

juices, honey and added sugar. The frequency of consumption as well as the total
amount of sugars consumed is important in the aetiology of caries. Furthermore, the
rates of dental erosion, related to extrinsic and intrinsic acids, appear to be rising. This

Oral health is closely linked to the diet and nutrition as poor dietary practices increase

the 12 year old.
2.3 Lack of physical activity
World Health Organisation (WHO), defines physical activity as any bodily movement
produced by skeletal muscles that require energy expenditure. Physical inactivity has
been defined as the fourth leading risk factor for global mortality causing an estimated
3.2 million deaths globally and one of the main risk factors for communicable diseases
and consequently the burden of overweight and obesity.
Physical activity is believed to be an important factor for determining the weight of
children. Generally, activity levels among children and adolescents are low, however,
sedentary behaviours such as playing electronic games, watching TV are the general
norms and are believed to be directly associated with obesity.
In addition, inactive children and adolescents who consume small amounts of food may
compromise the range of nutrients they consume and fail to meet necessary

requirements. Therefore, to maintain a healthy weight, children and adolescents need
to balance energy intake versus energy expenditure.
2.4 Eating habits
The ultimate issues behind public health’s major concerns for children and adolescents
are the changes in food habits that have developed over the years as a result of
globalisation of food. Over the past few decades, substantial changes have taken place
in eating habits and home environment.
The fact that nowadays both parents are working in a family setting, plus time
constraints, have led to the ‘convenience revolution’ with pre-packed processed
products forming the basis of the majority of meals prepared in the home.
Furthermore, the tendency to eat out has also risen, and Seychelles is no exception.
Subsequently there have been an abundance of restaurants and take away outlets
including food vans across the country leading to an increase in the consumption of fast
foods and take away meals and a decrease in fruits and vegetable intake. Additionally,
the GSHS report of 2015 revealed that only 39% of all students were eating fruits and
vegetables at least 5 times a day (Lucia et al. 2015)
Social factors influence dietary intake, especially during adolescence. Eating habits,
food choice and meal patterns of young people reflect the weakening influence of the
family and increasing peer pressure. Furthermore, changes in eating habits can be
associated with the need to express freedom from parental control. The various
psychological, social and environmental factors that influence food preferences
increase with age as children and adolescents are subject to an increasing array of
developmental changes and influences outside the home.
2.5 Good nutrition in schools
Healthy nutrition improves child well-being and learning ability, leading to better
academic performance. Evidence shows positive links between children who are well

nourished and improved learning attendance, behaviour and consequently good
teacher and child relationships. Good nutrition is also known to foster mental, social
and physical well-being, leading to increased self-esteem and positive body image.
However, despite the evidence, the recognition of the benefits of good nutrition have
been largely lost as a result of globalisation and the developments of international food
markets.
Most people have the misconception that the nation’s diet is a matter for the Ministry

according to the latest heart study results that was done in 2013 (Bovet and

children, from a total of 10.6 % to 22% in both boys and girls (Aly et al. 2018).
Obviously, obesity is not the only serious consequences to an unhealthy diet. Junk foods
make a person ill without necessarily being obese. Many patients treated for metabolic
syndrome or pre-diabetes are not overweight at all. And eating insufficient amount of
fruits and vegetables can lead to illnesses whatever your weight.
Eating a balanced diet reduces chances of falling ill with cancer, heart disease,
Cerebrovascular Accident/stroke (CVA) or diabetes- whatever the weight. However,
this is not just about physical well-being. A balanced and nutritious diet feeds the body
as well as the mind. Many studies have shown positive outcomes for better diet quality
in terms of behaviour, cognitive, and school performance (Adolphus, Lawton and Dye
2013)
There is also evidence that practical cooking and gardening lessons help to develop
children’s scientific and environmental understanding. Hence it is not surprising that
some successful academic schools worldwide have a good food culture.

There are many benefits to a good food culture. When children sit down to eat with
friends and teachers in a civilised environment, it strengthens relationships, helps them

3 The roles of nutrition in schools and community
3.1 The school community
Schools and communities have a shared responsibility to provide children access to high
quality foods and positive nutrition experiences that will have a lifelong impact on their
health and education. Family and community involvement is vital in supporting and
reinforcing nutrition education in the schools.
3.2 Parents and caregivers
Children need support from their parents to implement nutrition knowledge acquired
in schools. In addition, changes in positive behavior last longer if interventions are
aimed at family attitudes and habits rather than individual alone. Furthermore, parents
NATIONAL SCHOOL NUTRITION POLICY 2018

can benefit from the same information to improve their own health and nutrition
behavior. Parental involvement is crucial as it enhances the effectiveness of programs
by stimulating awareness about food and nutrition so that the variety in the diet and
the availability of healthy foods at home can be increased.
There is a relationship between mother’s health motivation and the quality of their
children’s diets. Children’s food preferences change through observation of peers and
elders. Research indicates that children not only model their parents’ food intake, but
also their attitudes to food and body dissatisfaction. Additionally, many studies indicate
that children reflect their parent’s unhealthy eating habits. Therefore, the need to
strengthen the pathway of information that parents obtain regarding food and
nutrition. Furthermore, evidence shows that after-school clubs play a crucial role in the
development of nutritional health and eating behavior. Consequently, the meals and
nutrition messages provided contribute considerably to the child’s knowledge and
future health habits.
3.3 Teachers Role Model /collaborations
The school environment is a powerful influence on the student’s attitudes,
performances and behaviors. Classroom lessons alone might not be enough to effect
lasting changes in student’s eating behaviors. Aside from availability of a healthy variety
of foods, children also need to be influenced by role models around them. In addition,
all teachers and other school staffs should at all times be complying with the sets of
guidelines found within the national school nutrition policy. The influence of school
goes beyond the classroom and includes normative nutrition messages from both peers
and adults. In that respect, teachers and other school personnel have a major role in
shaping the eating habits of children by also consuming high nutritional value foods
from the GREEN category. Furthermore, it is a common practice for both parents and
teachers to use food to reward or punish children, and this should be DISCOURAGED at
all times.

All schools hold a ‘Dedicated Fund’ for the purposes of assisting students whose families

Head teachers in consultation with all parties concerned decide on the type of
assistance needed by individual students, in consultation with their parents and
according to an agreed set of criteria. The funds are managed by the office manager,
under the supervision of the Head and they are audited by the Ministry.

4 Guidelines for Food Provision
4.1 School Meal standard
The school meals program has been in existence since the early sixties. At that time,
most schools were owned and run by the Roman Catholic Mission and school meals
were provided by the Catholic Relief Services (CRS). In the late seventies, all the schools
were transferred under the Government of Seychelles, hence the school meals program
was transferred under the state. School meals were provided free until the 1990s when
parents were asked to contribute SR. 2/- towards the meal except for students on the
Dedicated Fund (meaning those with difficult economic background)
School meals are more popular within the crèche and primary student population, and
aims to provide regular, nutritious food to children. Based on scientific evidence, proper
nutrition can improve children’s ability to concentrate, improve disruptive behavior and
encourage children to attend school. Hence, the school meals program is considered as
both an educational and student welfare program (Attorney general office 2017).
Establishing healthy eating practices in childhood is vital in promoting healthy growth,

development and helping children attain their full education potential. Diet is also key
in preventing both numerous short-term and long-term diet related diseases in
childhood and adulthood. It has been established that schools provide the most
effective way to reach large portions of the population, including young people, school
personnel, families and community members. Additionally, schools offer more
effective, efficient and equal opportunities than any other setting to promote health
and healthy eating. They reach young people at a critical age of development at which
lifestyles, including eating patterns, are developed, tested and adapted in schools.
Through social interactions between students, teachers, parents and the community,
schools provide excellent opportunities, as it is well established in literature that eating
habits are formed early in life. Therefore, it is vital that students are provided with at
least one plate of quality food per day at school in a safe, hygienic and conducive
environment. To date, around 6000 children and 45 teachers are using the school meal

Healthy school meals can provide children with a third of their daily nutritional
requirements. Without nutrient standards and portion size guidelines, it is impossible
for caterers to provide healthy meals on a daily basis.
Simply improving the quality of school meals is not adequate; children should be

planning meals; and having teachers, and other school staffs eat in the dining room and
speak favourably about the healthy meals available there. Students should also be given
adequate time and space to eat meals in a pleasant and safe environment. Healthy and
appealing meals should be available in the dining rooms.

4.3 Tuck shop Guidelines
Tuck shop guidelines

and update as necessary to reflect the current status of commodities in Seychelles.
Furthermore, following emerging recommendations from WHO with regards to the
Sugar Sweetened Beverages (SSB), it was imperative to ensure that these products are
excluded in the schools given their potential harm on health. Since the policy already
existed, some changes have been made to the document to meet the
recommendations and standards. These standards will apply to all food items that are

sold on school premises as well as school meals in all schools across Mahe, Praslin and
La Digue. The National School Nutrition Policy is introducing the Traffic Light
Classification system in order to better classify the products according to their
nutritional values (see below) (Australian goverment department of health 2013.)
‘The Traffic Light Classification’ System
The traffic light classification system adopted in many developed countries worldwide,
is a simple way to rate foods and drinks as either; Green, Amber and Red based on their
nutritional value. The traffic light system makes it easy to plan healthy menus by
following the colour coded signals.
Green – indicates healthier choices and should make at least 70% of the tuck shop
menu. For example; if a tuck shop sells 10 food items = 0.7 x 10= 7. This means that the
tuck shop will have 7 food items from the Green category and 3 from the Amber
category
Amber – means that the food items in this group should be selected carefully. Food and
drinks from this category should make about 30% of the tuck shop menu.
Red – foods and drinks in this group should NOT be provided at all on the school
premises, as there is ample opportunity for students to enjoy these types of food and
drinks outside the school setting and with the supervision of their parents
Foods and drinks from the GREEN category
These foods and drinks should be encouraged and promoted in unlimited amount.
Should make at least 70% of the tuck shop menu at all times. In general, these foods/
drinks are excellent sources of important nutrients; low in saturated fats and added
sugar and salt and help to avoid an intake of excess energy. Foods from this groups
should be available daily and should always be at the top of the menu list in all tuck
shops.
Foods and Drinks from the AMBER category
Amber foods and drinks should be selected carefully and should only be consumed in
moderation. Choices should NOT be dominated by these food and drinks because

although they provide some good nutrients, overconsumption of items from this group
can lead to excess energy (weight gain) if taken in large serving sizes. Foods in this
category are usually palatable due to moderate to high amount of fats and salt in the
products. This is why most children tend to buy mostly from this category rather than
the green category, therefore a limit on the variety of snacks had to be imposed as a
way of regulating intake of these foods during school hours.
Foods and Drinks from the RED category
Foods and drinks in the RED category are not essential and are usually eaten to satisfy
cravings hence the reason they are called ‘extra’ foods. These should not be offered in
schools because they lack adequate nutritional value; are high in saturated fat and / or
added sugar and salt and can contribute to excess energy, and tooth decay/ erosion.
These foods and drinks can be consumed outside of school hours when children are
physically active and are able to balance their energy expenditure. Nonetheless, these
food items are still recommended to consume in moderate amount as an excess will
definitely lead to weight gain and other metabolic disorders.
New to this list are all packet juices, regardless of the manufacturing processes. This is
due to emerging recommendations from World Health Organization (WHO) with
regards to the sugar sweetened beverages and its direct association to obesity, dental
caries and other metabolic complications. All processed meats have been added to this
list based on their high saturated fats and salt contents, and their association to colon
cancer according to recent studies.

5 Children with medical conditions
5.1 Children with Diabetes
Because the diet of people suffering from diabetes is based on the principles of healthy
eating, children with diabetes can eat the same school meal provided by the school as
other children. However, these diabetic children should have a meal plan, which has
been prepared especially for them by a Dietician/Nutritionist to guide them on their
portion sizes and snacks options as these will vary given their conditions. Diabetic
children should ensure they are taking their treatments if any, correctly as instructed
by their doctors. The school should be informed of any medical conditions.
5.2 Children Suffering from Food Allergies
The most common food allergens include milk, wheat, peanuts, fish, eggs and shellfish).
Children suffering from food allergies and medical conditions (excluding diabetes)
requiring medically-prescribed special dietary requirements, need to know the exact

ingredients in their food, because even a tiny amount of particular food could affect
them. Parents should try as much as possible to accommodate their children’s special
dietary requirements by providing them with their lunch and snacks. In all cases, the
schools should be informed about the medical conditions.
5.3 Nutritional needs of exceptional children
The nutritional needs and eating capacity of exceptional children are not always the
same as those of the ordinary children. It is important that all institutions
accommodating these children have a policy for delivering, in partnership with parents,
carers and nutritionists, medically prescribed diets. Furthermore, anyone involved in
caring for and providing school meals to these children should be trained to ensure that
the most appropriate meal and assistance is given. These eating difficulties should not
be a barrier to enjoyment and participation in meals and food choice or to learning
about healthy eating. Thus, school meals provided to these exceptional children should
comply with their special needs. No additional charge should be passed on to parents.

7 Food Safety
It is important that environmental conditions in schools ensure a safe food supply. Such
conditions are supported by sufficient treated water, safe means of water disposal, safe
foods and ingredients, adequate food contact surfaces, good personal hygiene,
cleanliness, tidiness and adequate ventilation. Rules and regulations for food safety are
needed to minimize the risk of contamination and food-borne infection. Applying rules
for safe food preparation is also essential for students, teachers and service providers.
a) Awareness of the importance of basic food hygiene is an important aspect in
food handling. Arrangements should be in place to promote hand washing prior
to the consumption of meals and snacks or even when sharing foods.
b) Leftover food should not be stored and served to the students.
c) Schools should be encouraged to purchase foods from reliable licensed sources
complying with national food regulations. Transportation of foods to the schools
should be done through safe and hygienic conditions.
d) Schools should comply to the recommendations set by regulatory bodies and
adopt a code of hygienic practices at primary stages of food production based
on the Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) concept (Ref. Hygienic
Practice For Food Premises).

8 Physical activity
Currently, all schools have a physical activity program which aims at increasing
knowledge and participation in sports. Physical Education (PE) occupies 80 minutes on
school time table every week and all children are encouraged to participate. However,
studies have shown that not all students are taking advantage on these classes, and
that half of the population of school- aged children are not doing PE. Based on the
latest GSHS results, about 49% of school children are not participating in any sports
team at the school with the highest rates being mostly in girls. A total of 41% boys and
girls are physically active at least once a week and 19% are not active at all according to
the same report. Regular physical activity is one of the most important things you can
do for your health at it helps reduce risks for developing cardiovascular diseases,
diabetes mellitus, some cancers, fractures, strengthens bones and muscles and also
improve mental health and moods.
9 Access to water
According to GSHS 2015, only 37% of students aged between 11 and 17 drink at least 5
or more glasses of water a day. This is a common practice among children and
adolescents worldwide including Seychelles, and it may be due to the fact that access
to water in many schools are unsatisfactory. In addition, many schools in Seychelles
NATIONAL SCHOOL NUTRITION POLICY 2018

have started having water fountains, although many schools are yet to adopt this
facility.
Children need access to adequate amounts of fluids throughout the day to maintain
proper bodily functions. Thirst signals the first sign of dehydration, at which point
mental performance and concentration will already have decreased by 10%. This can in
turn have serious negative effects on academic performance at school. Hence, it is
important to create awareness among school staffs of the need for adequate fluid
intake and to encourage school children to drink enough water.
Water should always be adequate and easily accessible for all school children. Water
fountains are encouraged on the school premises. Additionally, chilled bottled water
should be sold at all times in the tuck shop. Ideally, every child should be encouraged
to take a bottle of water to school every day.
Ensuring free access and promoting regular intake throughout the school day, should
be promoted in schools as water is the best choice for meeting daily fluid requirements.

1. Introduction
This document supports the implementation of the National School Nutrition Policy, first drafted in
August 2008 and revised in 2018, to provide a framework for implementing nutrition strategies to
ensure students receive good nutrition in parallel to education messages that are taught in class
throughout the whole of the school. This guideline aims at:
1. Providing healthier choices to school children in line with the National School Nutrition Policy
2. Ensure maximum food choices are from the Green category of foods
3. Promoting healthy eating habits in school children and reinforce what is taught in the
classrooms so as to create a snowball effect in our population
4. Ensure that food and safety aspect is well respected and practiced at all times
5. Reducing the incidence of foodborne illnesses among school children
2. Health and safety guidelines
2.1 Food handlers
Schools should be encouraged to purchase foods from reliable licensed sources complying with
national food regulations.
Hence, a complete food handler medical examination should be carried out and a certificate of fitness
should be provided.
All food handlers should practice good personal hygiene;
a) They should be healthy, and not be suffering from diarrhea, vomiting, flu etc.
b) They should wear clean suitable protective clothing including apron and hair cover and
in certain cases use of disposable gloves

c) They should avoid coughing, chewing gum, wearing of jewelries, spiting and smoking
while handling food.
d) They should wash hands as often as they can
2.2 Food Safety
 Rules and regulations for food safety are needed to minimize the risk of contamination and
food-borne illnesses. Applying rules for safe food preparation is also essential for students,
teachers and service providers.
a. All food service personnel (including tuck shop owners) should have adequate training
in safe food handling and medical certificate of fitness valid for six months. In addition
to this, a written list of all personnel who qualify for assisting in food provision should
be available at school level.
b. Schools should ensure that personnel involved in domestic duties specifically cleaning
of WC and gardens do not assist in food provision at school.
c. All food, cooked or uncooked should be properly covered in covered containers, or
cling/ plastic film or aluminum foil.
d. All prepared, cooked and ready to eat foods should be placed in suitable covered and
well protected against all forms of contamination.
e. Hot foods should be kept at a temperature of at least 600c at all times to prevent
microbial growth.
f. Avoid direct and indirect contact between raw and cooked or already prepared ready
to eat foods.
 Indirect contact may be caused by improper storage facilities, dirty hands, and dirty
equipment.
a. Foods to be eaten raw (e.g. shredded vegetables) should be prepared with special
attention and cleanliness.

Appendix 4- School Meal Standards
School Meals standards
Healthy school meals can provide children with a third of their daily nutritional requirements provided
it consists of a variety of foods from different food groups. The provision of healthy school lunches will
help ensure that students establish healthy eating habits early on and are provided with a range of
essential nutrients to aid their growth and development.
These school meals standards and portion size guidelines, are intended to assist caterers in providing
healthy meals on a daily basis. Although the nutritional quality and palatability of school meals are
important, children should also be educated on the many benefits of consuming school meals.
Some of the strategies that can be used include teaching about health benefits of school meals in class;
raising awareness of parents about the nutritional value and importance of school meals for growing
children as compared to food from external caterers; the cost-effectiveness of choosing school meals
daily; learning about what ingredients are used in the different meals on offer; having access to the
school meal menu; and having the whole school community (teachers, school management, and
parents eat in the dining room and speak favorably about the healthy meals available there.

Students should also be given adequate time and space to eat meals in a pleasant and safe
environment. Healthy and appealing meals should be available in the dining rooms. There should also
be emphasis placed on the serving of meals especially in ensuring the students are given the correct
portion size for their age.
Menu Planning by Food Group
Foods are classified into the following food groups according to the nutrients that they contain:
 Grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains
 Fruits
 Vegetables
 Pulses
 Milk and milk products
 Fish, meat and alternatives
Foods found in each group provide a similar range of nutrients.
To ensure that students are meeting their nutritional requirements daily without too much salt, fat and
sugar, it is important to consider what proportion of the overall diet is coming from each of the food
groups.
A quarter of the total food intake should be made up of foods from the grains, tubers, breadfruit and
plantains food group. This group is the main source of energy but also provides additional vitamins,
minerals and fibre (especially wholegrain varieties). The portion should not be too large as excessive
energy intake can promote weight gain over time.
One third of the daily intake should come from fruits and vegetables groups with the aim of consuming
at least two portions of fruits and three portions of vegetables, coming up to a total of five portions of
fruits and vegetables. Fruits and vegetable are rich in vitamins, minerals, fibre and phytonutrients. A
variety of fruits and vegetables should be eaten to increase the amount and type of nutrients gained.
Additionally pulses such as lentils and beans are also encouraged daily as they are not only rich in
vitamins and minerals but also in protein and fibre.
Fish, meat and alternatives as well as milk and milk products need to be eaten in moderate amounts as
they provide essential nutrients in concentrated amounts. Fish, meat and alternatives provide protein

children and also assist in overall growth. Milk and milk products also provide protein but additionally
supply calcium, vitamin D and other essential nutrients for mineralization of bones and teeth.
Oils and healthy fats provide essential fats for the growing child but should make up a relatively small
proportion of the total food and drink consumed. Therefore a small amount should be used in meal
preparation.

Reducing the Fat Content of Meals
Whilst the choice of food items or ingredients is very important, healthier cooking methods and
preparation techniques must be used to reduce the fat content of meals. Easy ways to cut down on fat

 routinely grilling, steaming, stir-frying or oven baking rather than frying
 trimming visible fat from meat before cooking
 removing the skin and fat from poultry before cooking
 adding less oil in meal preparation by measuring out the amount of oil being used
 skimming fat from the surfaces of sauces before serving
 preparing lower fat salad dressings (No mayonnaise should be used)
 using a thin batter for fish
 using only a small amount of oil to prevent bulk quantities of food items (e.g. pasta) from

 limiting frying to only twice per week.

Appendix 9- Terms of Reference School Nutrition Committee (TOR)
TERMS OF REFERENCE
SCHOOL NUTRITION POLICY COMMITTEE
1.0 INTRODUCTION
The School Nutrition Committee is set up through collaborative efforts of the Ministry of Health and
Ministry of Education to ensure the effective implementation of the School Nutrition Policy (drafted in
2008 and revised in 2018). The aim of the policy is to help provide a framework for the implementation
of nutrition strategies to ensure students receive nutrition education and message that are reinforced
throughout the whole of the school environment (see appendix A for an overview of the School Nutrition
Policy).
2.0 AUTHORITY OF THE COMMITTEE
The committee has the immediate role of executing its responsibilities as defined in the TOR/plan of
action. All new recommendations will be submitted to the ministry or organization concerned for final
decision.
3.0 RESPONSIBILITIES OF THE COMMITTEE
To ensure effective implementation of the School Nutrition Policy, the committee shall:
 Advise the Ministry of Education and Ministry of Health on issues related to implementation of
the policy
 Review the plan of action at least twice a year in light of development and changes in schools
 Review and update the monitoring tools of the National School Nutrition Policy
 Action upon the evaluation results administered at school level
 Based on the evaluation results, propose recommendations for improving the implementation of
the policy
 Propose strategic actions and awards based on the evaluation results
 Co-ordinate and assist in strengthening capacity building in all schools
 Interview and select tuck shop operators for all primary and secondary schools as per set
guidelines and procedures (see appendix E for guidelines)
 Revise the lease agreement for tuck shop owners so that it complies to the policy
 Review the list of external food suppliers that provide school lunches
NATIONAL SCHOOL NUTRITION POLICY 2018

74
 Source out funding for school nutrition award to be held every two years
 Identify and reward the school that is best at implementing the policy with the School Nutrition
award
 When violations of this policy are noted, advise and recommend corrective actions to the Ministry
of Education
 Submit reports to the Ministry of Health and the Ministry of Education
4.0 CHAIR AND CO-CHAIR
The committee will be chaired by the Ministry of Health and Co-chaired by the Ministry of Education.
4.1 DUTIES OF CHAIR AND CO-CHAIR
 Prepare the agenda and chair of meeting in collaboration with the Secretary
 Co-ordinate the work of the committee
 Follow up duties designated to committee members
If the Chair is unavailable, the Co-chair assumes the role of Chair
5.0 SECRETARY
4.1 ROLE OF SECRETARY
 Coordinates preparation of meeting agendas
 Takes and drafts minutes of meetings and distribute to all committee members
 Acts as a liaison between committee members and the committee
 Acts as a resource for members and works closely with the Chair
 Generates any correspondence as appropriate
6.0 COMMITTEE MEMBERS
The committee members represent the Ministry of Health, Ministry of Education and other
organizations and stakeholders interested and committed towards the enforcement and
implementation of the School Nutrition Policy, which include:
1. Nutrition Unit, Ministry of Health
2. Dental Section, Ministry of Health
3. Health Promotion Coordinator, Ministry of Education
4. Inspectorate Unit, Ministry of Education

7. Food Control Unit, Ministry of Health
8. Unit for the Prevention and Control of Cardiovascular Diseases (UPCCD), Ministry of Health
9. School Health Program, Ministry of Health
10. Seychelles Licensing Authority
11. PAM’S Catering
7.0 ROLE OF COMMITTEE MEMBERS
Members of the committee are accountable to their individual organization. Specific roles include:
 Attend meetings as scheduled
 Support implementation of the School Nutrition Policy
 Report on activities or any issues related to the School Nutrition Policy
 Represent the committee on advisory committees, sub committees or working
groups
 Being able and prepared to attend any activity related to the committee’s mandate
as necessary
8.0 MEETINGS
For the first 6 months, the committee will convene on a monthly basis. From then on, the committee
will convene three times every school term, at the beginning, mid and end of school terms. Special
sittings of the committee may be called to undertake specific tasks as and when necessary.
9.0 ATTENDANCE
If a member is unable to attend a meeting he/she will advise the Chair or the Secretary, and make every
effort to send a designate if possible.
10.0 REVIEW OF THE COMMITTEE
The Committee's mandate, activities, Terms of Reference and relevance will be reviewed every year to
ensure that it continues to meet ongoing needs. Recommendations for improvement will be considered
on an ongoing basis.

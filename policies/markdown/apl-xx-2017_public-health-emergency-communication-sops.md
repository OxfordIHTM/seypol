Ministry of Health – Seychelles

Public Health Emergency
Communication SOPs

1. Purpose

1.1

These crisis communication Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) serve as an
additional component of the Risk Communications Plan. The SOPs define key
protocols and responsibilities in the event of a public health emergency, but are
to be utilized flexibly with specific emergency decision-making procedures as
outlined in the general response plan and according to identified needs in the
event of any actual or potential public health crisis.

2. Overview

2.1   During  public  health  emergencies,  early,  credible  and  frequent  exchange  of
information and advice between health officials and the public is fundamental
to the success of the response, and should employ a diverse range of techniques
and tools ranging from media and social media communications to interpersonal
dialogue  and  community  engagement.  Rumours,  misreporting  and  other
communication risks must also be closely monitored and managed in line with
the
communication  best  practices.  Risk  communications  is  ultimately
responsibility  of  all  responders,  partners  and  management,  and  all  must  be
familiarized with their respective roles and responsibilities in relation to Crisis
Communications, as outlined in the Ministry of Health’s Risk Communications
Plan.

3. Activation

3.1

The Lead for Risk Communications will sit on the EOC and will work with the
Public Health Commissioner in a swift and thorough assessment of the situation
to determine whether a communications response is warranted, and if so, the
type of communications and scope of commitment needed.

Action:  The  Risk  Communications  Lead  will  be  responsible  for
activating the Emergency Communications Committee as appropriate to
the situation at hand.

4. Guiding Principles

4.1

Five essential practices and guiding principles must inform all communications
during a public health emergency: build trust, announce early, be transparent,
respect public concerns, and plan in advance.

Build trust
As  the  foundation  for  effective  outbreak  communication,  the  most
critical objective is to build, maintain, or restore public trust in those
responsible for managing the outbreak and issuing information about it.
This  means  establishing  effective  systems
two-way
communications with those facing public health risks, including through
community engagement and dialogue.

Announce early
Early  announcement  contributes  to  early  containment  in  a  situation
where every day counts. Equally important, early announcement wins
public confidence that authorities are openly reporting what they know
when  they  know  it,  setting  expectations  that  information  will  not  be
concealed. This means being ready to communicate even in uncertainty:
aim to communicate fast and communicate first.

Be transparent
Transparency  characterizes  the  relationship  between  the  outbreak
management and preparedness teams and the public. Transparency can
be defined as communication that is clear, candid, easily understood,
complete, and accurate.

Respect public concerns
The  public  is  entitled  to  information  that  affects  their  health  and  the
health of their families. Public concerns should be treated as legitimate,
explored,  and  respected  as  a  force  that  will  influence  an  outbreak’s
impact.  Today,  effective  risk  communication  is  viewed  as  a  dialogue
between  technical  experts  and  the  public  and  rumour  management
(listening  and  responding)  is  fundamental  to  effective  emergency
preparedness and response.

Plan in advance
Outbreak  communications  must  be  a  part  of  outbreak  management
planning from the outset. This means being prepared: having protocols
in place as well as coordination mechanisms, with plans, materials and
messages ready for dissemination for identified public health risks.

6. Role of the Communication Committee

6.1   Once activated, the Emergency  Communications Committee must ensure the
following actions are taken in the event of any potential or confirmed public
health emergency:

  Collaborate and communicate across all appropriate channels to ensure

effective ongoing communications to targeted audiences.
  Ensure spokespeople are mobilized, briefed and prepared to
communicate with key stakeholders and the general public.

  Develop, test and disseminate concise, clear public health messages

and materials.

  Undertake timely communication with the mass media.
  Engage and orient community stakeholders and external partners to
support public awareness on the response and preventive/protective
public health actions.

9. Social media and online

9.1

The Ministry of Health website and social media accounts are managed by the
Communications/Public Relations Unit. During an emergency, the
Communications Committee has primary responsibility for ensuring regular
and timely posts on the Ministry of Health website and social media channels
with the assistance of other members.

9.2

During an emergency, web and social media messages and materials must be
approved by the Communications Committee Lead, under the guidance of the
Public Health Commissioner and IMS.

9.3  Ministry of Health staff should conduct themselves with required discretion on

social media channels at all times. They should never share confidential or
sensitive information on their personal accounts, or information that may
otherwise compromise the success of the response. Employees unsure about
the confidential nature of information they are considering posting should
consult their department leader or the Communications/Public Relations Unit.

9.4

Individual staff should not use Ministry or government logos in their social
media profiles, link to their professional email addresses, or make
representations that personal accounts or sites are in an official government
capacity.

During the preparation phase all must consider all available methods of communications
with the PHEOC and therefore NEOC. This can include phone connection, email and any
other means of communicating effectively to ensure efficient information flow between
risk communications and PHEOC/NEOC.



Information flows.

It is very easy in a complex and chaotic situation for organizations to become overloaded
with  information.    This  situation  can  be  exacerbated  by  the  same  information  being
transmitted via different routes.  It is therefore important that information distribution
process outlined above is reinfroced.

Consideration must be given to making all reports/communications as brief as possible,
whilst not omitting any key information.  Consideration must also be given to whether a
development necessitates and immediate situational report or whether it would be better
to wait and report it in a fuller report when more information is available.

In making this decision Communications Lead and PHEOC need to consider the following
things: do I need someone to take action on this information or does the information have
wider ramifications which others need to consider.  If not then a single, later, fuller report
is better than multiple step by step reports.  Once an incident is covered by a mission
there should be no further need to report on that incident to the NEOC, though Incident
reports will need to continue to be passed on enable other risk communications members
to produce their own reports.

  Progress Reporting.
NEOC will need to provide daily reports to the Communications Lead.  To facilitate
this, milestones may be set and the following guidelines may be used

o  Numbers of Casualties.  The total number of cases with the number of

new cases since last report and the number of cases discharged.

o  Numbers of individuals at Quarantine facility.  The Total Number of
people  at  quarantine  facilty  with  the  number  of  new  arrivals  and  the
number who have been able to return to their own homes.

o  Resources  Remaining.    Totals  of  key  resource  types  available  with
forecast of when key resources will be exhausted if not replenished.

2)  Establish credible, transparent, early, and empathetic messages. In the
first 24 hours of a response, trust in leaders can be established or destroyed

what  is  known  as  soon  as  possible,  even  if  not  everything  is  certain!

can end up costing lives due to messages not being believed. Being open
and transparent is key to winning trust.

Appendix 2: Media Guidelines

Role of the media

During  a  crisis  responding  to  the  media  can  consume  your  time,  but  proactively
engaging them is vital to the success of the response. Mass media outreach is especially
critical during the first hours or days of an emergency, when it enables you to reach
large numbers of people quickly with key information. Because of their immediacy,
television and radio are particularly important in crises that develop quickly. In the past,
organizations had 24 hours to get information to media outlets. Now, media outlets can
provide  immediate  and  continuous  updates  on  a  crisis  through  contributions  from
people  experiencing  the  crisis,  which  has  increased  the  demand  on  organizations  to
keep pace with information delivery.

What do reporters want during an emergency?

Reporters want and need the following:

  Timely answers to their questions
  Access to experts
  Visuals to support their news stories

These needs are the same outside of an emergency, but the time pressure becomes much
greater  during  times  of  crisis.  Anticipating  questions  from  the  media  can  help  you
prepare and respond.

The more you anticipate what the media needs, the more effective you will be at the
following:

Informing the public


  Helping them understand public health actions or recommendations
  Gaining  public  acceptance  for  public  health  activities  during  response  and

recovery

Messaging during emergencies

Assembling the facts is a priority for your team, and retrieving background information
will give you a head start. This is the information that will not change during a crisis.
For example, if an outbreak involves an organism that is not a new form, its description,
incubation period, and methods of treatment will stay the same. It is easily retrievable,
as  technical  agencies  like  WHO  and  other  sources  have  developed  much  of  the
background information reporters need.

As general guidelines for an initial response:

20



---

  Remain open with, and accessible to, reporters.
  Respect their deadlines.
  Release some information initially, but be honest that you are still gathering
information. The following are suggested responses for the media that give
the time necessary to collect the facts:

o  “We’ve just learned about the situation and are trying to get more

complete information right now.”

o  “All  of  our  efforts  are  directed  at  bringing  the  situation  under
control. I’m not going to speculate about the cause of the incident at
this time.”

o  “The situation is developing and we don’t yet have all the facts.

Based on what we do know, we expect....”

  Do  not  give  in  to  pressure  to  confirm  or  release  information  before  it  is

confirmed.

  Say only those things that you are willing to have repeated.  Work on the

assumption that there is no such thing as “off the record”.

  Keep interviews short.
  Don’t over-reassure: Consider statements such as: “This is a very

dangerous winter storm, but people can take actions to limit their risk. If at
all possible, stay at home and off the streets until the storm passes and
roads are clear.”

  Emphasize that a process is in place to learn more: Describe that process in

simple terms: “Samples are taken from each person reporting x-like
symptoms. These samples are being tested now to identify the exact
strain.”

  Acknowledge people’s fears: Don’t tell people they shouldn’t be afraid.
They are afraid, and they have a right to their fears. Use statements like,
“We understand people are concerned and afraid, and it is normal to be
frightened when facing x.”

  Be willing to address the “what if” questions: These are the questions that
everyone is thinking about, and to which they want expert answers. Use
statements like, “We have considered the possibility that the situation will
get worse and we have identified additional locations for treatment sites.”

  Give people things to do: In an emergency, some actions are directed at
victims, those exposed, or those who have the potential to be exposed.
Simple actions in an emergency will give people a sense of control and
will help motivate them to stay tuned to what is happening.

  Ask more of people: Perhaps the most important role of the spokesperson
is to ask people to manage the risk and work toward solutions with you.
“By staying informed and following instructions from health officials, you
can protect yourself, your family, and the community against this public
health threat.”

Talking to the media (general do’s and don’t’s)

Before the interview

  Know your topic – Do your research and anticipate the questions that you may

be asked.

  Know what you want to say in one minute or less. If you can’t summarize your

news in one minute, it’s too complicated or it lacks focus.

  Prepare a limited number of positive key messages in advance and repeat the
messages  several  times  during  the  interview.  If  key  messages  have  not  been
developed, then it may not be the right time for an interview.

  Anticipate  tricky  questions  -  Know  what  those  who  will  argue  against  your

position will say, and address their points.

During the interview

  Be  concise  and  on  message  –  Skillful  speakers  keep  their  responses

straightforward and to the point.

  Keep it simple - imagine you are describing the situation to a grandparent, and

use appropriate tone and language. Avoid jargon and acronyms.

  Be positive - Be friendly, but don’t be flippant or ‘funny’. State your point in

simple, positive language and tone.

  The reporter may use inflammatory or emotional words. Avoid repeating them





with the journalist; use your own words.
If  the  question  contains  leading  or  loaded  language,  reframe  the  question  to
eliminate the language and then answer. Sometimes it’s helpful to restate the
question in neutral terms.
If the reporter claims someone has lodged an allegation, don’t assume he or she
is correct. Don’t react to new information a reporter presents. Instead, you can
say, “I have not heard that” or “I would have to verify that before I can respond.”
If  a  reporter  holds  a  microphone  in  front  of  you  after  you’ve  answered  the
question, resist the temptation to add information. Do not answer the question
again or add to your answer. Instead say, “That was my answer. Do you have
another question for me to address?” Say it matter-of-factly, without sarcasm or
annoyance.

  Understand  there  is  no  such  thing  as  “off  the  record.”  Do  not  say  anything
before, during, or at the conclusion of an interview that you are not prepared to
see in print the next day or uploaded to social media in the next hour.
  The interview is not over until the reporter and all equipment have left.
  Never lie or speculate. If you are not sure about something tell the reporter you
do not know and that you will get back to him/her when you have gathered the
information.

  Correct misinformation immediately. Don’t be afraid to rebut a summarizing
statement made by the journalist. If you don’t correct them, the information may
be attributed to you as a quote.

  Be  careful  about  seeming  to  criticize  another  agency  or  organization.
Remember that you are a voice for the Government and this can very quickly
become the sole focus of a story. The media may not be the best platform to air
your grievances!

Useful lines and phrases for interviews

  Don’t let the reporter switch topics: "Before we tackle that, I need to add…”

o  “I think it would be more accurate to say…”

the Communication Committee and under the directive of the Public Health

for practice and actual mobilization. Mobilization can occur anytime and
requires both your and your employer’s full support.

4.  Hours of duty during normal time: 24 hours for 7 days.

5.  Duty Rotation frequency during normal time: Every week. To hand over the

7.  Number of people per team on duty: Minimum 2 and maximum 3 per shifts.
1 can log all calls and the other 2 answering calls and forwarding for reference
if needs be.

8.  Hotline  backup  during  normal  time:  To  have  a  backup  officer  to  forward

calls to when line is busy.

9.  Duty responsibility:
  To answer hotline,
  Record incidents reported,
  Contact the appropriate officer to respond to the incident reported and
  Follow through with actions taken.
  To give an end of duty report of all the calls that came through the hotline.

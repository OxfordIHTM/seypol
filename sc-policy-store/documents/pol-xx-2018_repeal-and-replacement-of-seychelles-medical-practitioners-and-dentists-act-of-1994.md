# REPEAL AND REPLACEMENT OF SEYCHELLES MEDICAL PRACTITIONERS AND DENTISTS ACT OF 1994

## POLICY INSTRUMENT

### INTRODUCTION

As this discussion begins to delve into the rhyme and reason for the repeal and replacement of the Medical Practitioners and Dentists Act of Seychelles, it is imperative and helpful to go back into history to see what prompted regulation of the medical and dental professions in the first place. A publication of 1925 from the United States speaks volume. It states:

> … “Later, medical schools grew up, largely owned, operated and controlled by medical men. These throve, flourished, and many went sadly to seed, putting out very poorly trained men to the dissatisfaction of the profession and public alike. In casting about to find some remedy for this evil, some of our fathers hit upon the idea of having laws passed and licenses issued to all who were worthy. This placed the affairs of medicine into the hands of the states, that is to say, the legislatures, the governors, and, in certain states and of late years, the electors…
>
> …… In great measure, perhaps, on account of the presence of unskilled and irresponsible practitioners, there has ever been a more or less vigorous effort to regulate the practice of the healing art. (GUNDRUM, p.595, 1925)

Regulation is recognized internationally as a simple approach to ensuring that health care professionals are safe in their practice and remain safe to practise throughout their career. Self-regulation by the medical and dental professions is no longer seen as the best option for ensuring high quality of care and most countries have moved to robust statutory control of the medical and dental professions, involving key stakeholders. Such control must be designed to protect the public by limiting the risks the public faces when receiving treatment and care.

Nevertheless, there is consensus that statutory control should not be so overwhelming as to represent a perpetual sword of Damocles hanging over the neck of medical and dental practitioners (Ash and Jo, 2004). A regulation only works when it solves, or at least reduces or ameliorates, the problem or problems that prompted government to adopt it in the first place (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat quoted in ElKhouly, Sayed Elsayed and El Sedfy, Hala, 2015).

### PURPOSE

By a Cabinet decision of 2014, Government has authorised the Ministry of Health and other Stakeholders to review the regulatory framework for medical and dental practice in Seychelles. Subsequently, the main stakeholders agreed that the new legislation must be fit for purpose and consistent with current best practice in the regulation of medical and dental professions while taking into account the specificities of Seychelles. The main stakeholders agreed that the current legislation must be repealed and replaced.

Following requests from the Cabinet Committee for progress reviews on the project, the Ministry of Health has informed the Committee, that a first set of documents for the consideration of the Attorney General’s Office could be ready by December 2018. The attorney General’s Office is responsible for producing the first official draft of bills in Seychelles.

Given the complex technical considerations for such a legislation, external expertise has been judged imperative to produce documents in depth and breadth for the consideration of the Attorney General’s Office. The General Medical Council of UK has been an able and willing partner of the Seychelles Medical and Dental Council in this effort.

The overarching justification for the existence of the Council is to protect the public, safeguard the medical and dental professions and promote optimal health care.

### SCOPE

The purpose of this policy paper is to present the main policy pointers as to the kind medical and dental regulation needed in Seychelles bearing in mind the country’s peculiarities and some of the overarching policy principles in the health sector and in the country.

The Constitution of Seychelles (1993) provides for a charter of fundamental human rights and freedoms in which the right to life, dignity, privacy, education, shelter, employment, health care, social security etc. are indelibly enshrined.

The mission of the national health strategy (a consensus document published in 2016) is to relentlessly promote, protect and restore the health, quality of life and dignity of all people in Seychelles, with the active participation of all stakeholders, through the creation of an enabling environment for citizens to make informed decisions about their health.

The vision of that strategy is the attainment, by all people in Seychelles of the highest level of physical, social, mental and spiritual health and living in harmony with nature.

### Background

Seychelles is a group of 115 small islands (with an area of only 459 sq. km of land) in more than 1 million sq. km of the South West Indian Ocean. Seychelles is located more than 1000 km from east coast of Africa.

The country’s governance is assured by a President elected by universal suffrage for a maximum of two five year terms, a National Assembly of 33 members and a judiciary with tribunals, magistrates’ courts, a supreme court and an appeals court. Currently, the party in Government does not have a majority in the National Assembly so there is an unprecedented (for Seychelles) power sharing arrangement dubbed locally as “cohabitation”.

#### Demography

The population of Seychelles is multi-ethnic and multi-cultural. Seychelles’ 95,000 people live harmoniously with each other. There are around 20,000 immigrant workers in the country, counted among the 95,000 people who form the population. In a year around 250,000 tourists visit the country most of whom come from Europe, the Middle East and Asia.

The fertility rate has decreased to 2.1 and the population is aging rapidly. Average life expectancy at birth is 75 years.

#### Economy

Seychelles has been recently classified as a high income country with a per capita income of just over USD15000. Most of the country’s earnings come from tourism, fisheries and off-shore financial services.

#### Social

An Agency for Social Protection exists to provide financial assistance to the needy. That Agency also finances “care at home” services who persons who cannot adequate perform their activities of daily living. Persons with disabilities, physical or mental, and who cannot work, are supported by the state.

Since the past five years the number persons living in relative poverty, the number of homeless persons and the number of abused persons (children and elderly especially) have tended to increase. The social malaise is often a symptom of abuse of drugs and/or alcohol.

#### Education

Literacy rate is very high in Seychelles with 95% of the population completing 6 years of primary education and 5 years of secondary education. Government operates a very generous overseas scholarship programme for university studies at graduate and post-graduate levels. The University of Seychelles is still a very small training institution offering only a small number of degrees in mostly legal, environmental and financial studies.

#### Health Status

Public health indicators in Seychelles are generally very good due to significant investments in the social determinants of health. Life expectancy is at 79 years for women and 70 years for men. Infant mortality rate is around 12 per 1000 live births. Maternal mortality can only be measured in absolute numbers with the number of maternal deaths in any given year ranging from zero to about 2 out of around 1700 live births per year.

The non-communicable diseases are the main health challenges with high rates of diabetes, hypertension, chronic respiratory illnesses and cancers.

The priority communicable diseases are leptospirosis, HIV/AIDS and Hepatitis C. Sexually transmitted infections are relatively high among the productive and reproductive age groups.

Intravenous drug use (5000 users) and alcohol abuse and their consequences have a strong bearing on the health care landscape.

#### The Health System in Seychelles

Health and education receive the highest portion of Government’s budget (together about 25%). Government spends about USD800 per person per year for health. There is a health centre within 5km of every home. Health centres are usually manned by a team of professionals which include doctors, dentists and allied health professionals.

The health system was initially modelled on United Kingdom’s NHS. Health care is free at the point of use at all levels of care although the Constitution of Seychelles prescribes only free primary health care in state institutions. Government continues to be the most prolific health care provider, although private health care services (mainly primary care services for now) are growing rapidly.

There are three distinct levels of care and each level acts as a gate-keeper for the next level. Patients have to be referred by doctors/dentists to move from the primary level to the secondary level and from the secondary level to the tertiary level.

The focus of the health system is meant to be on primary health care, where most of the efforts are supposed to take place but as in other places, health spending is heavily skewed towards the curative secondary and tertiary care levels. There is an elaborate Overseas Diagnosis and Treatment Programme (which has been recently regulated by an Act) and which sends about 255 patients who cannot be treated locally, for treatment to overseas hospitals. Most of these patients are sent to India and Sri Lanka.

Medical and Dental Practitioners are among the most well paid professionals in the Seychelles although they lag far behind some other professionals like lawyers and pilots, for example. In some specialities medical and dental practitioners work far above the recommended International Labour Organization maximum number of hours per week.

Compared to the international salary levels, doctors are significantly underpaid. Consequently, it is quite difficult for the local labour market to attract the best medical and dental professionals that the world can offer.

Throughout the health system, both in the public and private sector there are clear deficiencies with clinical care quality. During the last twenty years the country has become very litigious. Complaints about quality of care are increasingly becoming common occurrences. Most of these complaints are settled out of court but lawsuits for clinical malpractice are on the increase. Some of these cases are also referred to the Council. The ministry of health and the professional councils are now working hard to institutionalize person-centred care and whole person care.

#### Medical and Dental Practice

Seychelles has a very small population of active doctors (150) and dentists (50). Only 40% of the population of medical and dental practitioners are have Seychelles nationality. About 25% of those who have Seychelles nationality have gained Seychelles nationality through the process of naturalization.

The turnover of the 60% of doctors who do not have Seychelles nationality is very high. Most of those doctors remain in Seychelles for two years and leave at the end of their two-year contract to never return. Foreign medical and dental practitioners have great difficulty communicating in the three languages (English, French and Creole) that are used in Seychelles. Interpreters assigned to do translations for the doctors have little or no specific training and therefore they translate as they can to the doctors who, in turn, understand what they can.

Seychelles does not have a medical or dental school therefore all doctors are trained outside of Seychelles. Some of the doctors are trained in western countries but the majority are trained in countries of the former Eastern Europe, Cuba, India and Malaysia. Foreign medical and dental practitioners come mainly from Cuba, India and countries of Western Europe.

Over the years, the Government of Seychelles has gone to great lengths to train medical and dental practitioners. In the 70s and 80s most doctors went for training in the socialist countries around the world. In the 90s after the collapse of the Soviet Union and kindred countries, most doctors were trained in New Zealand. Most of the new wave of medical and dental practitioners who trained in New Zealand never returned to work in Seychelles. The consequence is that one or two generations of doctors have been lost. Their absence is sorely felt in the hospital where there is no practically no native doctors aged 35 to 50 years old.

There are currently some 55 Seychellois medical and dental practitioners on training in various medical schools around the world. This yields a return of about 8 doctors completing graduate training every year. Specialisation of these doctors is not as straightforward as it should be. Most of them have to opt to specialize in a country different from the country where they obtain their first medical degree. Consequently, there are challenges with registration in the country identified for specialisation.

Since 2013, the Seychelles Medical and Dental Council has structured an internship programme supervised by Consultants of Seychelles Hospital for those doctors who wish to or who have to do their internship in Seychelles. Efforts are underway to partner Seychelles with the University of Reunion (an Indian Ocean Department of France) to facilitate specialization of some doctors through the intermediary of the University of Seychelles.

Degrees conferred by a Medical School called USAIM set up in Seychelles in the early 2000 were considered inadequate for registration by the Seychelles Medical and Dental Council. The Supreme Court of Seychelles vindicated the decision of the Council in one of the landmark cases. It must be said however, that some of the doctors who were conferred a graduate degree by USAIM have gone on to pursue other training and other careers in other jurisdictions after sitting and passing various admission examinations for further training. The confusion arose with USAIM because of the lack of an a priori adequate regulatory framework for medical education.

In Seychelles Hospital most of the doctors are specialists with very few junior doctors. Some specialties have only one or two doctors doing all the work that needs doing within that specialty. There are many challenges in practising the medical and dental profession in Seychelles that do not necessarily exist in other countries.

#### Expectations of patients in Seychelles

Patients in Seychelles are well travelled, highly educated and very aware of what is going on in other parts of the world. They use traditional broadcast and print media and social media extensively and can easily be influenced by what they see or read. They demand high quality of care with every interaction with a health care provider. Patients exert heavy influence on government and are increasingly becoming less and less unforgiving when things go wrong in the care of their nearest and dearest.

### METHODOLOGY

Since 2014, extensive consultations have taken place within the medical and dental professions, the Seychelles Medical and Dental Council and the Ministry of Health on various aspects of the new legislation.

In parallel tracks, the public has had several opportunities to voice their opinion on their expectations about the conduct and performance of medical and dental practitioners in all areas of health care and about how they feel these practitioners should be regulated. On several occasions, elected representatives have acted as formidable echo-chambers, further driving home the view of the public on these matters.

Patient support organizations and consumer groups such as Cancer Concern, National Consumers Forum, Civil Society Engagement Platform of Seychelles, Fair Trading Commission and others have called for more or less the same care standards through various consultations and interactions with them on associated matters.

Desk reviews have tried to identify best practices in international medical and dental regulation that would be consistent with the specificities of Seychelles.

The following legislations were considered in the Desk Review:

1. Medical Council of Ireland Act (1987)
2. Medical Council Act of Singapore (2012)
3. Health Practitioners Competence Assurance Act (2003)
4. Kenya Medical Practitioners and Dentists Act

Experts from three other regulatory councils have been consulted and have participated in discussions with the Seychelles Medical and Dental Council and the Ministry of Health:

1. The General Medical Council of United Kingdom
2. The Health Professionals Council of South Africa
3. The Kenya Medical and Dental Council

### ISSUES

The current Seychelles Medical Practitioners and Dentists Act (1994) of Seychelles is fast becoming an irrelevant piece of legislation and its repeal and replacement is long overdue as averred by most of the stakeholders consulted on the matter. Nevertheless, some of the regulations of the Act (such as the regulation relating to the composition of the Council) need not necessary be revoked and can be brought back with the new legislation.

Some of the shortcomings with the current legislation are outlined below.

**Perspective of the public – matters which have manifested during disciplinary inquiry and elsewhere and which the public want regulators to address:**

a. Despite the existence of a statute, the process is still seen as self-regulation and members of the public feel that this so-called “self- regulation” does not work for them especially in an environment where medical and dental practitioners know each other and work with each other all the time. This collegiality is viewed as being mutually protective against the best interests of the patients.

b. The public tends to believe that many doctors put their own interests before those of their patients.

c. Documentation of care is often lacking in quality and quantity.

d. Linguistic impediments prevent many medical practitioners and dentists from communicating adequately with patients to the detriment of care and medical regulators have been unable to do anything significant about this. This challenge is not unique to Seychelles. “The feedback from patients in Scotland is that they do not want doctors from another country,” says Dr. Jean Turner, chief executive officer of the Scottish Patients' Association. (CMA, p158, 2014). “Importing doctors who do not know how our system works, and who, with the best will in the world, may have language difficulties, is fraught with danger.” (CMA, p158, 2014). Similar sentiments reverberate in Seychelles all the time.

e. Patients feel that they do not always have a right to make decisions about their care.

f. Patients feel that doctors are too secretive and complacent about poor practice and do not open themselves enough to scrutiny by regulators who are not actively practising medicine or dentistry.

g. The excessive paternalism (lack of respect for patients) shown by elements of the medical and dental profession is sometimes too evident.

Furthermore, competence, integrity, ethical practice, better communication with patients, better coordination of care, greater display of compassion and empathy from medical and dental professionals, the insistence on the imperative that the professionals should do more, to achieve the right thing for the right patient, at the right place and at the right time have been the public’s clarion call. They surmise that all these would coalesce to produce better health care outcomes and a better patient experience through the health care processes led by medical and dental practitioners.

**Perspectives of the Professionals including regulators:**

The medical and dental professions have recognised various flaws with the current legislation.

1. The functions and powers of the Council are very limited, rendering the Council ineffective to carry out the mandate expected of it.
2. The current legislation does not adequately promote a culture of lifelong learning to address issues of continuous professional development as a vehicle for care quality.
3. The legislation does not:
    a) adequately address registration and regulation of medical and dental practitioners with a specialist degree. There is no specialist register for example.
    b) address medical and dental education and continuing professional development.
    c) address the need for proficiency in at least one of the languages spoken in the country. (Note: there is an argument in the EU that language proficiency should be addressed by recruitment, not registration).
    d) adequately regulate internship and specialization issues.
    e) address professional indemnity insurance.
4. The register is bloated with practitioners who are no longer active in the country.
5. The law is silent about procedures for practitioners to take a break in their professional practice as a result of life circumstances such as illness or long sabbatical leave.
6. The disciplinary procedures are ineffective to say the least.
7. The issue of proper documentation of care (medical and dental records) needs to be addressed in the main law.

### MAIN RECOMMENDATIONS FOR THE NEW LAW

#### POLICY ONE:
The objects and reasons of the new legislation would be to address the concerns of the public and of the profession and seek to achieve the following:

a. Keep the Council in existence as a rather independent agency to regulate the medical and dental professions in Seychelles,

b. Change the fusty image of medical regulation processes,

c. Increase the confidence that the public has in the regulatory system and in the medical and dental professions,

d. Involve the public and the health service users more significantly in regulatory processes,

e. Promote, protect and advance the health and safety of the public.

It is believed that the above can be achieved through regulatory strategies to ensure that practitioners:

a) are adequately qualified after following approved standards of education and training,

b) are competent and display approved standards of professional conduct,

c) only those meeting high standards remain on the register,

d) are given periodic and renewable practicing certificates,

e) those who do not meet standards but have basic training are given opportunities for additional training/examination.

#### POLICY TWO:
With the new legislation, the Council must become a more credible regulatory authority. It shall set standards of medical and dental education and structures and processes to revalidate practitioners using mandatory continuous professional development (CPD).

### FUNCTIONS OF THE COUNCIL UNDER THE NEW LAW

1. Establish and maintain registers of registered medical and dental practitioners and specialists.
2. Set standards of good medical practice and ensure implementation.
3. Give guidance on ethical conduct and behaviour.
4. Set criteria for recognizing specialists and issue practicing certificates.
5. Issue certificates of current professional status.
6. Provide guidance on the keeping of medical and dental records.
7. Assure the quality of undergraduate, post graduate and continuing medical and dental education in the country.
8. Establish and administer systems for registration and licensing.
9. Distinguish specialists from non-specialists in registration and certification.
10. Establish processes for reporting adverse events.
11. Investigate complaints and take necessary action.
12. Deal firmly and fairly with fitness to practice issues.
13. Build and maintain national and international partnerships.
14. Advise the minister for health on medical/dental practitioners and patient safety.
15. Make recommendations for training and education.
16. Develop, implement and evaluate internship programmes and provide verification services for the public.
17. Perform all such acts as are necessary to be carried out under this Act.

## POWERS OF THE COUNCIL UNDER THE NEW LAW

#### POLICY THREE:
The Council must be given powers to do anything requisite or incidental to its functions, including the power to:

1. Acquire, hire or dispose of property, borrow money, and administer trusts/donations.
2. Employ staff, specifically a full-time registrar.
3. Set and collect registration and service fees.
4. Determine required professional qualifications and experience for registration.
5. Modify the register as it sees fit.
6. Approve or reject applications for registration.
7. Define the boundaries of the medical and dental professions.
8. Suspend practicing certificates or impose conditions on scope of practice.
9. Regulate its own procedures.
10. Summon practitioners, inspect places of practice, and copy records during inquiries.
11. Make rules necessary to achieve the objects of the Act.
12. Do all such things as the council deems necessary to achieve the objects of the Act.

### REGISTRATION UNDER THE NEW LAW

#### POLICY FOUR
The purpose of registration and licensing is to ensure that professionals achieve and maintain minimum standards of competence.

*   **Registration:** The act of placing an individual on a list (register) based on acceptable qualifications.
*   **Licensing (Practicing Certificate):** Evidence that the person is currently fit to practice.

These shall be separate processes. Registration and the receipt of a practicing certificate shall be obligatory.

1. Registration shall generally be for a period of two years and renewable.
2. The first contact is registration; subsequent renewals are for the "Practicing Certificate".
3. Renewal process must start at least three months prior to expiry.
4. The council shall communicate when renewal is due.

#### RENEWAL OF REGISTRATION AND PRACTICING CERTIFICATE

Renewal shall be subject to:
1. Evidence of CPD as prescribed.
2. Payment of a fee.
3. Absence of restrictions or circumstances precluding renewal.

#### REGISTRATION AND LICENSING OF SPECIALISTS

#### POLICY FIVE
There shall be special consideration for specialists. Following guidance from the Singapore act, a person may apply to be registered as a specialist if they hold postgraduate degrees or have gained special knowledge and skill in a particular branch of medicine or dentistry.

### INACTIVE STATUS AS MEDICAL OR DENTAL PRACTITIONER OR SPECIALIST

#### POLICY SIX
A practitioner may apply for an inactive practising certificate if they wish to retain a certificate while not actively practicing in Seychelles.

1. Persons with inactive status may not practice and need not comply with CPD.
2. Fees and expiry dates remain the same as active certificates.
3. To restore to active status, the physician must complete required CPD hours.
4. Conversion can occur at renewal or any time within the two-year period.

### REGISTERS

#### POLICY SEVEN
The Registrar shall maintain the following registers, open to the public:

1. **Register of General Practitioners:**
    * Part I: Full registration
    * Part II: Conditional registration
    * Part III: Temporary registration
2. **Register of Specialists:**
    * Part I: Full registration
    * Part II: Conditional registration
    * Part III: Temporary registration
3. **Register of Provisionally Registered Medical Practitioners** (e.g., interns)

### FEES

#### POLICY EIGHT
There shall be no exemption from prescribed fees to ensure financial sustainability and independence.

### FITNESS TO PRACTICE

#### POLICY NINE
Regulation of Fitness to practice shall follow the GMC Recommendations.

#### POLICY TEN
Appeal against any decision of the Council shall be made to the Supreme Court. The Minister for Health shall have no authority to overrule the Council.

### PROSPECTS

The emphasis needs to shift from punitive reaction to pro-action, prevention, and corrective support. Future considerations include the potential transition to an umbrella body model (similar to Australia or South Africa) to achieve economies of scale.

### CONCLUSION

Public confidence is contingent upon a dependable system of regulation. The new law will only be fit for purpose if it adequately addresses all issues contained in this policy document.

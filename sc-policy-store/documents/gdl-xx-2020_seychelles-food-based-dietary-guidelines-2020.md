## 2. Preface
(Signed) **Peggy Vidot (Mrs.), Minister for Health**

The new Food-Based Dietary Guidelines (FBDGs) translate scientific evidence into layman terms to address the evolving health landscape in Seychelles, specifically targeting obesity and non-communicable diseases (NCDs) resulting from the shift toward westernised, processed diets.

## 3. Introduction
Seychelles is a high-income country, but socio-economic transformation has led to increased sedentary behavior and poor dietary patterns. A 2013 survey revealed 57% of men and 72% of women were overweight or obese.

**Overarching Principles:**
* Encourage local, fresh and minimally processed foods.
* Promotion of sustainable healthy diets.
* Establish healthy eating habits by understanding socio-economic and cultural factors.
* Overcome barriers to healthy dietary patterns.
* Create meals using local culinary practices.

## 4. Overarching Principles

### A. Encourage local, fresh and minimally processed foods
Ultra-processed foods (frankfurters, fizzy drinks, etc.) are linked to NCDs. Local options like sweet potato, cassava, and fresh fish are nutritionally superior.

### B. Promotion of sustainable healthy diets
Emphasis on plant-based foods (whole grains, legumes, nuts, fruits, vegetables) which reduces environmental pressure and improves health.

### C. Establish healthy eating habits
Understanding that food choices are influenced by social, cultural, and economic factors. Childhood habits often persist into adulthood.

### D. Overcome barriers
Addressing the misconception that healthy eating is too expensive or difficult. Moving away from "convenient" ultra-processed foods.

### E. Local culinary practices
Promoting traditional Seychellois dishes to maintain cultural identity and nutritional quality.

## 5. Rationale for Revision
Revised to reflect current culinary practices and the rising burden of obesity. New focus includes the "first 1,000 days" (pregnancy to age 2).

## 6. Changes to the Seychelles Food Guide

*   **Grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains:** Renamed from "Rice, bread, cereals, pasta and tubers".
*   **Fish, meat, eggs, and plant-based alternatives:** Explicitly includes eggs and plant-based proteins (pulses, nuts, seeds).
*   **Oils and healthy fats:** Renamed from "foods containing fat and sugar"; sugar has been removed to discourage high intake.

## 7. The Set of Guidelines

### General Population (>5 years)
1. Variety from 5 food groups.
2. Different coloured fruits/veggies.
3. Beans and lentils regularly.
4. Regular fish (especially oily fish).
5. Whole grains and local tubers over rice.
6. Daily milk/milk products.
7. Water instead of sugary drinks.
8. Reduce salt and sugar.
9. Daily physical activity.
10. Avoid or limit alcohol.

### Infants and Young Children (0-5 years)
1. Exclusive breastfeeding for first 6 months.
2. Introduce variety of foods from 6 months; continue breastfeeding to 2 years.
3. Water only (avoid sugary drinks).
4. Encourage exploration of different foods.
5. Fresh, local foods over high fat/sugar options.
6. Animal sources (fish, meat, liver, eggs) for growth/anaemia prevention.
7. Plenty of green/yellow/orange veg/fruits.
8. Daily physical activity.

### Pregnant and Lactating Women
1. Variety from 5 food groups.
2. Coloured fruits, vegetables, and pulses.
3. Whole grains and local tubers.
4. Variety of fish (safe and important).
5. Daily milk/milk products.
6. Water for hydration.
7. Reduce salt and sugar.
8. Daily physical activity.
9. No alcohol, smoking, or illegal drugs.
10. Iron-rich foods to prevent anaemia.
11. Folic acid and iron tablets as prescribed.

## Section 1: General Population (>5 years)

### 1. Variety of foods
No single food provides all nutrients. A balanced diet includes plant-based foods (3/4 of the plate), small amounts of animal proteins, and healthy fats. Water is essential for hydration.

### 2. Coloured fruits and vegetables
Rich in antioxidants and fibre. Different colours indicate different nutrients (e.g., Beta-carotene in orange, Anthocyanins in purple). Recommendation: 400g/day.

### 3. Beans and lentils
High in protein and fibre, low in fat. Excellent meat alternatives. Portion: 1/2 cup cooked pulses.

### 4. Fish and Oily Fish
Rich in Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA) for brain and heart health. Limit red and processed meats (classified as carcinogenic by WHO).

### 5. Whole grains and local tubers
Unrefined grains (bran and germ intact) provide more B-vitamins and fibre. Replace white rice with brown rice, plantains, or tubers.

### 6. Milk and milk products
Crucial for calcium to maintain bones and teeth. For lactose intolerance: use lactose-free milk or fermented products like yoghurt.

### 7. Hydration
Water is essential. Avoid sugar-sweetened beverages. adults need ~2.5L of fluids daily.

### 8. Salt and Sugar
*   **Salt:** Max 5g/day for adults. High salt increases blood pressure risk.
*   **Sugar:** Limit free sugars to <10% (ideally <5%) of total energy. 1 tsp = 4g sugar.

### 9. Physical Activity
Adults: 150 mins moderate or 75 mins vigorous aerobic activity/week + muscle strengthening 2x/week.

### 10. Alcohol
Limit intake to prevent liver damage. Unit = 10ml volume. Max 2 units/day, 14 units/week.

## Section 2: Infants and Young Children (0-5 years)

### 1. Exclusive Breastfeeding
Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months provides ideal nutrition, antibodies, and promotes bonding.

### 2. Complementary Feeding
Start at 6 months. Gradually transition from pureed to mashed to family foods by 12 months. No added salt or sugar.

### 3. Fluids
Water after 6 months. Avoid juices/fizzy drinks. No tea/coffee before 5 years due to iron absorption interference.

### 4. Sensory Exploration
Allow children to touch and taste varied foods to build positive associations and prevent fussy eating.

### 5. Local vs. Ultra-processed
Avoid sugary cereals, savoury snacks (crisps), and processed meats. Prioritize fresh, local food.

### 6. Iron-Rich Foods
Introduce liver, fish, meat, and eggs from 6 months to prevent anaemia and support brain development.

### 7. Vitamin A
Focus on carrots, pumpkins, and dark green leafy vegetables for vision and immunity.

### 8. Activity and Screen Time
Children >12 months need 1 hour of active play daily. Limit screen time (<1 hour for ages 2-5; 0 hours for <2 years).

## Section 3: Pregnant and Lactating Women

### 1. Balanced Diet
Increased energy needs in 2nd/3rd trimesters. Focus on nutrient-dense carbohydrates (sweet potato, oats).

### 2. Fruits, Vegetables, and Pulses
High fibre intake helps prevent pregnancy-related constipation.

### 3. Whole Grains and Tubers
Essential for energy and digestive health.

### 4. Fish and Omega-3
Oily fish (mackerel, tuna) provides DHA for baby's brain and visual development.

### 5. Milk and Calcium
Protects mother's bone density and supports baby's skeletal development. Avoid unpasteurized milk (Listeria risk).

### 6. Hydration
Pregnant women: ~2.5L/day. Lactating women: ~3L/day.

### 7. Salt and Sugar
Reduce salt to prevent hypertension/preeclampsia. Limit sugar to avoid gestational diabetes.

### 8. Physical Activity
150 mins moderate activity/week. Focus on walking and swimming.

### 9. Avoid Harmful Substances
Zero alcohol, smoking, or illegal drugs to prevent Foetal Alcohol Syndrome and other birth defects.

### 10. Iron Intake
Animal sources (haem iron) and plant sources (non-haem iron) are needed to prevent anaemia.

### 11. Supplementation
Folic acid (400µg/day) pre-conception/1st trimester to prevent Neural Tube Defects. Iron supplements as prescribed.

## Appendix 3: Meal Ideas

**Breakfast:** 1 tub plain yoghurt + 2 slices bread OR Veggie Frittata.
**Lunch:** 1 medium fruit + 2 pieces steamed breadfruit + 3tbsp chutney + 3tbsp lentils + grilled fish.
**Dinner:** 2 pieces steamed cassava + Vegetable salad + Fish curry.
**Snacks:** 1 medium fruit / tub of yoghurt / handful of nuts.

3. Choose whole grains and local tubers instead of rice for a healthy heart
4. Eat a variety of fish as they are safe and important for the mother’s health and baby’s development
5. Choose milk and milk products every day for stronger bones and teeth

7. Reduce intake of foods high in salt and sugar to reduce the risk of diet-related diseases.

9. Do not drink alcohol, smoke cigarettes or use illegal drugs when pregnant and breastfeeding to prevent harming yourself and your baby
10. Eat iron-rich foods such as fish, meat, green leafy vegetables, and pulses every day to prevent anaemia
11. Take folic acid and iron tablets everyday as prescribed during pregnancy to prevent birth defects and anaemia

# 2. Preface

The Seychelles Dietary Guidelines of 2007 was the first formal guidelines depicting how we should be eating for good health. These guidelines however are in need of revision to address the evolving health landscape and prevailing public health challenges.

The new Food-Based Dietary Guidelines (FBDGs) for Seychelles translate the most up-to-date scientific evidence into layman/everyday terms for the target population. It takes into account Seychellois culture and specifies the recommended dietary intake across the life course. It seeks to promote greater awareness and knowledge that encourage healthy food choices as part of a healthier lifestyle and as a means of reducing the risk of diet-related diseases.

In crafting the new guidelines for Seychelles, considerations have also been given to the nutritional transition. The shift from predominantly unprocessed traditional foods such as fish, locally produced fruit and vegetables, tubers, roots and breadfruit, to a more westernised dietary intake consisting mainly of refined and processed foods has also contributed to the increase in obesity and its complications such as non-communicable diseases (NCDs).

Reducing the risk or better management of obesity and NCDs can be achieved by simple lifestyle changes. These include eating more healthily, through the inclusion of more local and diversified foods, reducing intake of ultra-processed foods often high in sugar, saturated fat and salt, reducing time spent sitting and increasing physical activity in all age groups.

Tackling the issue of over-nutrition remains one of the top priorities of the Government of Seychelles. This new edition of the Food-Based Dietary Guidelines not only aims to provide practical guidance on how to address some of the root causes of obesity and its comorbidities, but also how to prevent them in the first place.

The guidelines have been developed in conjunction with the existing realities of the country from an economic, social, cultural, environmental and agricultural perspective. They are also in line with relevant and existing strategies, plans and policies that are nutrition specific and nutrition sensitive.

A series of consultations with professionals and the public have contributed to the development of the current guidelines. With the ‘health in all policies’ agenda at the forefront, the Ministry of Health adds these guidelines as a tool to reinforce the need for multi-sectoral collaboration in striving to attain better health for all.

# 3. Introduction

Seychelles is ranked as a high-income country. In 2018, it had a high human development index of 0.801 [1] and a GDP of USD1.59 billion [2]. The increased wealth has resulted in a social and economic transformation in the society, with improved services such as social protection, health and education. Unfortunately the socio-economic transformation has also had a remarkably negative impact on the dietary patterns and lifestyle choices of the population with the main challenges being over-nutrition and its related co-morbidities, notably, non-communicable diseases (NCDs).

Increased wealth has also led to a boom in private car ownership and improvement in public transport facilities thus adding to increasing sedentary behaviours. In children and adolescents increased television, computer and gadget screen time and reduced opportunities for play and exercise further exacerbate the low level of physical activity amongst these population groups [3].

A Health survey conducted in 2013 revealed that 57% of men and 72% of women were either overweight or obese [4]. Annual school survey carried out from 1998, show continued increase in the rates of overweight and obesity among school-aged children. In 2018, 25% of boys and 32% of girls were either overweight or obese [5].

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as diabetes, heart disease, cancer and renal failure have been identified as the main causes of death, illness and disability among adults [4]. In children, overweight and obesity have also been associated with high blood pressure and other detrimental cardio-metabolic conditions [5]. Modifiable risk behaviours such as insufficient physical activity and unhealthy dietary choices are largely responsible for these growing public health issues [6].

Seychelles is highly reliant on food imports with over 80% of food coming from external markets. Local production is unable to compete with the low cost of imported ultra-processed foods due to the high cost of local production, limited land for agricultural activities due to competition with other sectors, limited resources, climate susceptibilities, amongst the other factors [7].

To meet the Global Nutrition target of zero per cent increase in obesity rate by 2025, measures must be taken to ensure that people are guided on how to prevent excessive weight gain, or to lose weight and avoid chronic diseases [7].

Food-based dietary guidelines are an important means of ensuring the individual attains a high nutritional status and hence reduce the risk of nutrition-related diseases [8]. The current food-based dietary guidelines are a series of evidence-based statements aimed at promoting the consumption and enjoyment of healthy, locally produced variety of foods by the Seychellois population. The expected end result is improved health for all groups in the population today and for generations to come. The new guidelines now replace the ones published in 2007 which were designed for all those above the age of two years. The new guidelines are separated into three groups: infants and young children (below five years); pregnant and lactating women; and all those above five years.

While this document is written for professionals and educators, the target group for these guidelines is the general population, hence the messages have been tailor-made to capture everyone. The use of simpler communication materials, including booklets and posters, and also translation into Creole, will ensure accessibility and use by all. These materials will be made available in printed copies and distributed in schools, health centres and the hospitals. They will also be available online via the Ministry of Health website and Facebook page.

The summary guidelines in print will be easily accessible in all Ministry of Health facilities on Mahé, Praslin and La Digue. They will also be made available to educators in other settings such as in schools, work places, sports facilities and wherever health is promoted. Once the guidelines

C. **Establish healthy eating habits** by understanding the social, economic, psychological and ecological factors that help to shape it.

The various external factors that shape an individual’s eating habits are complex and dynamic. Whilst inherently there are biological processes regulating our food intake through the stimulation of hunger and satiety, the influence of the environment cannot be overlooked. Food preferences and eating habits are strongly influenced by the social and cultural environment, starting from early years and continuing throughout life. Childhood experiences will influence habits in later years, so healthy eating at a young age is likely to have life-long health benefits.

The cost of food is one of the main drivers influencing an individual’s food choice. The perceived high cost linked with healthy foods is one of the major reasons why many people choose ultra-processed and convenient foods, as they are usually perceived as being cheaper. However, this may not always be the case. For example in 2020 a cooked pizza (which usually serves one to two people) as a meal option costs more than preparing an actual meal (which can serve up to four people) using local ingredients.

There is also the long-term cost on health from regularly consuming such energy-dense options with a lot of saturated fats, sugar and salt. The social implication around eating is such that it becomes an inter-generational problem. Parents who usually buy the food will feed their children these energy-dense, convenient meals, which is reinforced by peers, hence making these foods the habitual and ultimately the preferred foods.

Types of food available also influences what a person chooses to eat. For instance healthy foods will be the easy and preferred choice in schools or workplaces if it is the only option available, and it is affordable and palatable.

Other influences such as stress, mood, attitudes and beliefs about food varies from day to day and from person to person in different ways. These influences also plays an important role in forming an individual’s food habit. Therefore, it is important that a variety of foods is selected not limited to just their nutritional property but also their flavour, familiarity, availability and social acceptability for each individual.

D. **Overcome barriers that prevents healthy dietary patterns**

There are several hindrances that prevent an individual from eating healthy foods. Often, solutions for these barriers are not pursued. For example, many people wrongly believe that cooking is difficult, and the perceived inability to cook implies that there is greater reliance on foods that require minimal preparation and cooking. Such ‘convenient foods’ includes options like frozen meals, pizzas, burgers, nuggets and sausages.

Moreover the additional appeal associated with such foods such as their low cost, ease of preparation, limited time required to be spent in the kitchen and intense flavour, eventually turns them into daily choices. They are also preferred options in social gatherings when additionally alcohol and sugar-sweetened beverages may also be readily available. The attractive advertising, extensively used to market such products across all media to boost their sale; lures consumers to continue to crave for such foods which are so abundant on supermarket shelves, often at low cost or perceived low cost.

Once a person is used to eating these energy-dense foods it becomes very difficult to make alterations. Internal motivation and behaviour change are the main instigators required for success. Any person can easily learn the skills required to prepare a variety of simple, delicious and healthy dishes. It may be more difficult however for them to appreciate the benefits in the short-term as it requires a bit more time for preparation of meals and there is often a perceived higher cost associated with buying healthy foods. Once the long-term benefits are understood however, it will be easier for the person to sustain a healthy lifestyle.

E. **Creation of meals from local food ingredients portraying local culinary practices**

Many of the local dishes that were once daily occurrences in most households are slowly disappearing. Many people have not learnt how to prepare some of the local delicacies enjoyed during the one-week Creole festival held towards the end of October annually.

It is important to continue to celebrate the traditional culinary ways and dishes whilst embracing other cultural influences. It is inevitable that with urbanisation,
Immigration and trade comes the acceptance of other foods that were not traditionally present at one point in time. It is unfortunate however that other influences such as westernised foods and dishes have surpassed our local and cultural foods.

Local dishes convey many benefits such as use of local ingredients that are usually more nutrient-dense or only available locally; learning cooking techniques that are unique to the Seychellois culture and a distinctive taste linked with the ingredients used and mode of preparation. Some common local dishes have been included in the appendix section within these guidelines for reference and as a means of promotion for the intended users.

Predominantly among young children and the youth, energy-dense, western foods have become the preferred and daily food options with most of them either disliking or not being exposed to local dishes.

## 5. Rationale for revising the Seychelles food-based dietary guidelines

Between 2007 and 2020, Seychelles has undergone a nutrition transition. The guidelines were revised to reflect the current culinary and dietary practices as well as the food environment in Seychelles. The revision also considered and tried to address the rising overweight and obesity and the diet-related non-communicable disease burden in Seychelles. The revised guidelines reflect the current advancements in scientific knowledge, and aligns with new emerging evidence globally into sound food-based guidance to promote health for all Seychellois.

The revised FBDGs are divided into three guidelines and are presented as follows:

1. General population over 5 years
2. Infants and young children (0 – 5 years)
3. Pregnant and lactating women

It is well documented that the first 1,000 days (which is from pregnancy until two years of a child’s life) are critical to lifelong nutrition outcomes of overweight, obesity and stunting. It is for this reason that the revised guidelines have a separate focus on children below five years as well as pregnant and lactating women. This addresses the limitations of the 2007 guidelines which included everyone above two years of age.

With the continued burden of obesity and non-communicable diseases in Seychelles amongst all age groups, it is necessary to address the problem in the very early ages of the human lifespan. Currently the rates of obesity, gestational diabetes and type 2 diabetes continues to increase amongst pregnant women, further predisposing the child to obesity and its complications. Healthy dietary practices during pre-conception and pregnancy are therefore important to improve neonatal outcomes.
# 6. Changes to the Seychelles Food Guide

The food guide is a plate model depicting the food groups and portions for each of the food group which should be incorporated in an individual’s diet. The changes made to the food guide is aligned to emerging evidence as well as the need to ensure that individuals attain a high nutritional status without compromising their nutritional requirements.

| Change | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Renaming of the ‘foods containing fat and foods containing sugar’ food group to ‘oils and healthy fats’** | The intake of small amounts of essential unsaturated fats is important for reducing inflammation and reducing overall cardiovascular risk. Saturated fats on the other hand increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases hence it is important to specify that unsaturated fat is being promoted. Sugar is a nutrient found naturally in many foods but this food group was initially encouraging intakes of small amounts of sugar added to food. Emerging evidence has linked high intake of added sugar to the rise in obesity and associated complications like non-communicable diseases. This is described in more detail in Section 1 under recommendation 8 (Reduce intake of foods high in salt and sugar to reduce the risk of diet-related diseases). Hence the new group has been renamed as ‘oils and healthy fats’ and sugar has been removed. |
| **Fruits and vegetables** | Fruits and vegetables have remain unchanged due to their similarity in terms of nutritional composition. It is felt however that the population should be educated on the importance of increasing their intake of vegetables which are often more nutrient-dense than fruits. This is because many people find it easier to consume fruits instead of vegetables due to palatability. As part of this education, the focus will be on encouraging people to have at least two portions of vegetables and three portions of fruits to meet the targeted ‘5 portions a day’ or 400grams recommendation from the previous guidelines as recommended by the World Health Organisation (WHO). |
| **Milk and milk products** | This group originally composed of milk, yoghurt and cheese and it remains unchanged. |
| **‘Rice, bread, cereals, pasta and tubers’ have been renamed as ‘Grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains’** | Grains encapsulates ‘rice, bread, cereals and pasta’ from the previous guideline. Tubers have been maintained but breadfruit and plantains which are not tubers but local starchy produce have been added as they are frequently consumed by the population. The portion of this group on the plate has been maintained as one third of the plate to reflect the total amount of calories which should come from starchy foods. |
| **‘Fish, meat and alternatives’ has been renamed as ‘Fish, meat, eggs, and plant-based alternatives’** | Many individuals are unaware that eggs feature in this group, hence it was felt that they needed to be more clearly identified by including them as part of the food group name. <br><br>The term plant-based alternatives have been added to incorporate pulses, nuts and seeds. Because of their high protein content, pulses are a very good meat alternative for everyone. Eating pulses regularly is a great way to reduce the amount of meat eaten per week. High consumption of red meat (beef, pork, lamb, and goat) increases the risk of heart disease, some cancers and mortality. <br><br>In addition, since pulses, nuts and seeds are rich in protein, they are excellent healthy alternative for vegetarians and vegans who do not consume animal-based products. Vegetarians and vegans who do not take any animal source foods should combine pulses, nuts and seeds with a variety of whole grains products daily to provide the body with high quality protein to get all the essential amino acids required. |

# Section 1

## Guidelines for the general population above 5 years
# 1. Enjoy a variety of foods from the five food groups every day to maintain good health

No single food contains all the essential nutrients that the body needs to be healthy and function efficiently. The nutritional status of a person depends on the overall balance of foods that is eaten over a period of time, as well as on the needs of the individual. It is after all only through the consumption of a variety of different foods that the nutrient and energy needs of the body will be met. For example, milk contains sufficient amount of calcium and protein but are comparatively deficient in iron and vitamin C, whereas oranges are relatively rich in vitamin C but deficient in calcium [10].

That is why a balanced diet should be one that includes a variety of foods, so that adequate intake of all nutrients are achieved and dietary diversity attained. On the contrary a diet with limited variety and which does not include all food groups is more likely to be deficient in one or multiple nutrients placing the individual at risk of malnutrition. A meal containing a variety of foods is also more appealing and enjoyable.

Over three quarters of the plate include a variety of plant-based foods which should form the basis of what we eat every day. This includes fruits, vegetables, grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains, pulses, nuts and seeds. Small amounts of fish, poultry or eggs should be added to our main meals. Red meat should only be eaten occasionally. Milk and milk products should also be included in moderation daily. Finally, foods that are rich in healthy (unsaturated) fats such as olive oil, sunflower oil, canola oil, and rapeseed oils should also be included in smaller amounts. This is because, although healthy fats are essential for various functions in the body, they do have a high calorie content.

Sugary products have not been included as part of a food group as there is unequivocal evidence about the negative impacts of excessive sugar consumption in relation to obesity and its complications. Therefore, it is important to limit intake of foods that are high in sugar. Although water is not part of a food group, drink adequate amount of water for hydration. Water helps to add balance to one’s diet.

A varied and balanced diet that is encouraged for the Seychellois population comprises of foods from all the food groups as illustrated in the Seychelles Food Guide (SFG) in figure 1. The SFG depicts the food groups in the proportion that they should be eaten as part of a balanced diet.

This guideline highlights the importance of incorporating a variety of foods in meal preparation in line with personal preferences, inclusion of local dishes, family meals and also factor in affordability to ensure that nutrient needs are being met and not compromised.
## 2. Eat different coloured fruits and vegetables every day to keep your digestive system healthy

Eating plenty of fruits and vegetables has been scientifically proven to reduce the risks of many non-communicable diseases (NCDs) including type 2 diabetes, hypertension and cancer. Such benefits arise from the fact that fruits and vegetables are packed with numerous phytonutrients, vitamins (e.g. A, C and E) and minerals (e.g. selenium) which act as antioxidants removing dangerous free radicals in the body.

Fruits and vegetables are very rich in dietary fibre. Different types of fibre including insoluble and soluble fibre are found in varying amounts in different fruits and vegetables. Fibre is essential for a healthy diet and prevention of diseases including cancer, heart diseases and intestinal problems like constipation. Fibre is important for keeping our overall gastrointestinal system healthy. Some fibre fractions are prebiotics and favour the growth of beneficial bacteria or probiotics in the colon. These bacteria ferment fibre, producing short chain fatty acids which is a useful energy source for colon t cells [11].

Phytochemicals in fruits and vegetables are compounds produced by plants that give plants their different colour, odour, and flavour. In addition, each colour indicates an abundance of specific nutrients and antioxidants. The most vibrantly coloured fruits and vegetables have the most nutrition. For example, lycopene thought to have antioxidant properties gives red fruits their colour. Orange fruits and vegetables are high in carotenoids such as beta-carotene. Beta-carotene gives yellow and orange fruits and vegetables their colour and is converted to vitamin A in the body. Dark green leafy vegetables are high in beta-carotene and also a good source of calcium. Blue and purple fruits and vegetables contain anthocyanins, another powerful antioxidant. Thus eating coloured fruits and vegetables ensures that individuals get all the benefits from the different fruits and vegetables.

Most fruits and vegetables are naturally low in fat and contain no cholesterol. Due to their low calorie content and their filling effect, fruits and vegetables are very beneficial for people who want to lose and maintain weight. The World Health Organisation (WHO) and Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) recommends that everyone should consume a minimum amount of 400 grams of fruits and non-starchy vegetables a day (excluding potatoes and other starchy tubers) [12]. This translates into five portions a day (80grams per portion).
# What is a portion?

A portion of fruits or vegetables is equivalent to 80grams and can include the following examples:

* 1 medium fruit e.g. banana, orange, star fruit
* 1 slice of a large fruit e.g. pawpaw, pineapple, melon
* 2 small fruits e.g. passion fruit
* 3 tablespoons/ 1/2 cup of fresh fruit salad
* 3 tablespoons/ 1/2 cup of raw, cooked, frozen or canned vegetables e.g. carrots, bottle gourd or pumpkin
* 1 cup of green leafy vegetables e.g. moringa soup (‘bouyon bred”)

> A child’s portion of fruit and vegetables is smaller than that of an adult, and is equivalent to the amount the child can hold in one hand.

It is well established that local fruits and vegetables supply in Seychelles does not contain excessive pesticide residues and is more beneficial in terms of its superior nutritional content as compared to its imported counterparts. It is important however that all fruits and vegetables are washed well with water to remove any residues that may have remained on the outer surfaces of such foods [10]. It is strongly recommended that people eat more local fruits and vegetables especially when they are in season and are more affordable.
# 3. Eat beans and lentils regularly to stay healthy

Legumes are plants with seedpods that have two halves e.g. beans, peas, soya beans, chick peas, kidney beans, black-eyed beans, green grams and lentils. Pulses are legumes in dry form and come in a range of colours, flavours and textures. Nuts and seeds have similar nutritional properties and health benefits to pulses and is an essential part of a healthy diet. Examples of seeds include pumpkin, sunflower, and sesame seeds while nuts include peanuts, walnuts, almonds, cashew nuts and their butters such as peanut butter.

A diet with regular intake of pulses is strongly recommended given its immense health benefits. There are several components in pulses which researchers have found to prevent certain diseases and conditions such as some cancers, diabetes, heart diseases and obesity. Pulses are a good healthy choice for everyone. Pulses contain high amounts of protein, fibre, have no cholesterol and are also naturally low in fat, with moderate amounts of carbohydrates and therefore provide the perfect base for a nutritious and filling meal. Pulses also contain a variety of vitamins and minerals which include vitamin E, selenium, copper, zinc and manganese, all of which are important antioxidants that can neutralize the effects of damaging agents in our body [13].

Different types of fibre including insoluble, soluble and resistant starch are abundant in pulses, all of which are essential for a healthy intestine. Pulses are also rich in phytochemicals (phytoestrogens, phytosterols and phytic acid), substances which are found naturally in plants and act as antioxidants, removing harmful free radicals in the body thus lowering the risks of cancers and cardiovascular diseases [10]. Nuts and seeds provide zinc, selenium, iron, calcium and Vitamin B6, healthy fats (mono and polyunsaturated fats) as well as some protein.

Due to their high protein content, pulses, nuts and seeds are suitable for vegans and vegetarians, and also an alternative to animal-derived protein for anyone wishing to reduce meat consumption. The nutritional quality of soya beans is superior to that of other pulses as it contains more protein and is an extremely good source of iron and calcium.

## What is a portion?

Aim to eat one portion of pulses or nuts and seeds every day or several times a week to get the health benefits of pulses, nuts and seeds. Increase your beans and lentils intake to 3 portions for those days you choose not to take any meat, fish or poultry in order to meet your protein and other nutrient requirements. Pulses is part of the ‘fish, meat, eggs and plant-based alternatives’ group.

One portion of pulses is equivalent to:
* ½ cup or 3 tablespoons of cooked dry beans, peas and lentils.
* ½ cup of canned cooked pulses
* 3 tablespoons of nuts
* 1 tablespoon nut butters
# Cooking and storage of pulses

Dried pulses need to be soaked and cooked before they are tender enough to use. Most dried pulses need to be soaked for several hours before they are cooked. Soaking time will depend on the type of pulses, and this varies from 4 to 12 hours. It is therefore useful to soak pulses overnight after which the soaking water should be thrown away and the pulses rinsed thoroughly before being cooked in fresh water. Avoid adding salt or acidic ingredients such as tomatoes whilst boiling the pulses as this will toughen the outer layers and prolong cooking time. Additional ingredients can be added after the pulses have been cooked [10].

Consumers should be aware that it is not safe to eat raw or undercooked beans such as kidney and soya beans as they contain toxins which are harmful to health. These dry beans need to be well cooked before eating, as heat deactivates these toxins.

Pulses can be cooked ahead of time although they should be cooled as quickly as possible after which they can be refrigerated or frozen for later use. Cooked pulses will keep for two days in the fridge and much longer in the freezer, although keeping it for too long can affect its taste and texture. Cooked pulses should not be kept out at room temperature for more than an hour or two, as this could allow bacteria to grow and multiply [10].

Tinned pulses are quick and easy to use because they have already been soaked and cooked, so they can be used as is in the preparation of a meal. Canned pulses are a healthy choice but they can also be high in added sugar and salt. Therefore it is important to check the nutrition information on the label to choose those with lower amounts of sugar and salt.
4. Eat fish regularly including oily fish like fresh tuna, bonito and mackerel for a healthy brain

The Seychelles Heart study carried out in 2013 amongst those aged 25 to 64 years revealed that approximately 26% of the study group comprising of 1240 participants ate fish every day and about 50% ate fish 5 to 6 days in a week. Comparatively, about 75% of the population are eating meat and about 50% are eating processed meat once or twice weekly [4]. This highlights the need for continued education to the population on the importance of increasing fish consumption whilst also reducing their intake of meats to improve their overall health outcome.

Recently the consumption of meat over fish has become more popular in the Seychellois diet. This has come about with the relatively lower prices of meat and its ease of access. However, such a trend is already negatively impacting on our traditional diet and likely on our populations’ health. Meat contains a higher proportion of saturated fats as well as much more cholesterol than fish. High intake of saturated fat and cholesterol increases the risk of non-communicable diseases such as cancer and cardiovascular disease [14].

In recent times, based on findings of studies carried out, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared that eating processed meat is linked to certain forms of cancer, putting it in the same category as smoking. The cancer risk is increased especially with the consumption of red meat which includes beef, pork, lamb, mutton and goat [15]. Therefore, adopting a diet containing a reduced amount of red meat and limiting processed meat products is ideal. Fish and pulses such as beans, peas, lentils and others are excellent substitutes, in terms of health benefits, sustainability, versatility and taste, compared to meat and processed meat.

In comparison to meat, fish are rich in high quality proteins, have a high proportion of healthy fats (unsaturated fats) namely omega-3 and contains an abundance of vitamins and minerals. Omega-3 fatty acids are mostly found in oily fish such as fresh tuna, bonito, trevally, swordfish, tuna and mackerel, is linked with a lower risk of heart disease and stroke [16]. It is recommended to eat fish on at least five days a week with oily fish being included at least twice weekly. Fish is best eaten grilled, baked, steamed or made into chutney rather than fried or other methods of cooking that requires adding additional oil.
A total of 1 to 2 portions of fish, poultry, eggs or meat can be eaten daily. One portion is equivalent to:

* 100g of cooked fish, without bones
* 85g cooked poultry (chicken/ poultry), without bones
* 85g cooked red meat ( beef/ pork/ lamb), without bones
* 2 whole cooked eggs

If red meat is eaten, limit to just one portion (85g) of meat and to no more than two portions per week. To meet the two portions daily requirements, choose a portion from other options including fish, poultry, eggs, or plant-based alternatives like lentils or beans.

### Why are processed meats and red meats discouraged?

The World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF) uses the term “processed meat” to refer to meat that has been transformed through salting, curing, fermentation, smoking or other processes to enhance flavour or improve preservation. Depending on preparations, processed meat can include salami, ham, bacon, pastrami, luncheon meat, corned beef and some sausages. Minced meat such as hamburgers or fresh sausages may sometimes (though not always) fall within this definition [17].

Processed meats are usually high in energy, salt, saturated and trans-fat and sometimes have low nutritive value. They often contain preservatives and other chemicals to make them last longer and to improve their texture and flavour. Some preservatives, like nitrites used to preserve processed meats, can react with proteins at high temperatures to form N-nitrosamines, a carcinogen. Other food additives such as artificial food colours, benzoates, saccharin, and sorbates could bring on an array of negative health effects including headaches, allergies, and asthma [18].

WHO has classified processed meat as Group 1 carcinogenic to humans. The classification is based on convincing evidence from epidemiological studies showing the development of colorectal cancer in exposed humans who ate processed meats [19]. Exposure to diets high in processed meat has also been associated with cancer risk, which is defined as average daily consumption of greater than 2 grams (less than a teaspoon) of processed meat preserved by smoking, curing, salting, or addition of chemical preservatives [20].

High consumption of red meat (beef, pork, lamb, and goat) increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), some cancers and mortality. Poultry which includes chicken, turkey and duck are not included as they are considered as white meat. According to the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF), it is probable that red meat consumption increases the risk of colorectal cancers. Consumption of red meat also has a “suggestive” effect on causing other cancers (nasopharynx, lung, and pancreas) [17]. WCRF recommends that, if you do eat red meat, cutting down can help protect against bowel cancer, and if you eat meat, it is best to choose unprocessed meat [17]. WCRF weekly intakes of red meat are between 300 to 500g per week. On the other hand, the Global Burden of Disease Study, exposure to diet high in red meat is defined as average daily consumption of greater than 22.5 grams per day of red meat (beef, pork, lamb, and goat) [20].
# 5. Choose whole grains and local tubers instead of rice for a healthy heart

The best carbohydrate source consists of unrefined or minimally processed foods commonly known as ‘whole grains’. Grains are the seeds of cereal plants (e.g. wheat, rice, corn) that consist of an outer fibre-rich layer (bran), a nutrient-packed inner area (germ) and a central starchy part (endosperm). During the processing or milling of cereal grains, the brans and the germ are often removed to give a more refined grain. Whole grains on the other hand contains all three layers of the grain and therefore have over 75% more nutrients than their refined counterparts.

The outer bran and germ layers of the seed, are rich in fibre, B-vitamins, zinc, phosphorous, selenium, iron and vitamin E [10].

There are a lot of health benefits for consuming whole grains and other starchy foods rich in fibre, vitamins and minerals. There is a lower risk of numerous NCDs including heart diseases and cancer in people who regularly eat whole grains as part of a healthy diet. The benefit is higher in those people who also consume more of the other plant-based foods such as fruits, vegetables, nuts and pulses. Since whole grains are high in fibre, they are beneficial in keeping the digestive tract healthy and help reduce intestinal problems [10].

Therefore, when choosing foods from the grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains group, it is advisable to replace refined cereal-based foods with whole grain varieties. For example, instead of having white bread choose whole grain bread or cassava cake or use whole wheat flour instead of white flour. In Seychelles white rice is the main staple and it is often consumed in large quantities despite its low nutritional value. Replace white rice as often as possible with brown rice, plantains, and starchy roots and tubers such as cassava, sweet potato, yam, and taro which contains more fibre, vitamins and minerals and are therefore nutritionally superior.

## Portion size variation

Energy intake is normally dependent on activity levels, age and gender. Therefore, a person with a higher activity level such as an athlete or a construction worker will need to consume larger portions of starchy foods to obtain the energy required. Someone who leads a sedentary lifestyle must consume a much smaller portion as larger portions can lead to excess energy being stored in the body as fat.

Around 5-6 portions from the grains, tubers, breadfruit and plantains group is recommended daily. Given that grains, roots and tubers have different shapes, sizes and food weights, one portion of food from this group will provide 40g carbohydrate which is equivalent to the following in food weight:

* 2 thick slices (40g) of bread
* 1 medium bowl (80g) of wholegrain breakfast cereal* (*may vary for different cereals)
- 6 tablespoons (80g) raw, rolled oats
- 1 cup (250g) cooked oats
- 1 cup/ 4 tablespoons (120g) of cooked rice
- 1 cup/8 tablespoons (160g) of cooked pasta
- 1 large (80g) boiled plantain (‘bannann senzak’)
- 2 medium sized (130g) boiled potato/ sweet potato/ taro/ yam
- 2 pieces (90g) of boiled cassava
- 2 large pieces (140g) of breadfruit
- 2 cassava flat bread (‘galet’)
6. **Consume milk and milk products every day for stronger bones and teeth**

Milk and milk products such as yoghurt and cheese provide nine essential nutrients including protein, calcium, phosphorous, potassium, and vitamins A, D, B1, B2 and B12. Milk and milk products provide a readily absorbable and convenient source of calcium required for the normal development and maintenance of the skeleton. Calcium is stored in teeth and bones, where it provides structure and strength. Low intakes of calcium have been associated with osteoporosis, which often results in bone fracture [10]. ### Milk intolerance

There are some people who find it hard to digest milk and milk products because they are intolerant to lactose (milk sugar). For these people, milk and milk products do not need to be totally excluded in their diets. Research has identified simple strategies to make milk and milk products easier to digest. These strategies include drinking lactose-reduced or lactose-free milk, eating probiotic yoghurt (with live and active cultures), or consuming hard cheeses such as Cheddar and Swiss that are naturally low in lactose.

Calcium supplements although not substitutes for milk and milk products can still help to meet the body’s calcium requirement. However, a calcium tablet should not take the place of calcium in food as the tablet will be missing out on other nutrients that milk provides, such as vitamin D which helps the body absorb and deposit calcium in the bones. Furthermore, a supplement can never make up for an inadequate diet and therefore a food-based approach to achieving one’s dietary requirements is the best option in attaining good nutritional status.
7. Drink water throughout the day instead of sugar-sweetened drinks like fizzy drinks and juices to stay well hydrated

Although water is not considered a nutrient, it is a fundamental part of our lives and is ranked second only to oxygen as essential for life. We can exist without food for two months or more, but we can only survive for a few days without water. Two thirds of our body weight is water with the average adult body being 55% to 75 % water. Some of the functions of water include digestion and absorption of food, regulation of body temperature and blood circulation, maintenance of normal bowel functions, transport of nutrients and oxygen to cells, and removal of toxins and other wastes from the body. Water contains minute amounts of minerals and has no caloric value, making it the best drink for everyone, and especially those seeking to lose and maintain a healthy weight [10]. Re-hydrating with sugar-sweetened beverages is not a healthy choice since they have added sugars which can promote weight gain if consumed in excess.

### How does our body lose water?
On average, a healthy individual loses approximately 1.5 to 2.5 litres of water every day. Water is lost from the body at a very rapid pace as urine, and to a lesser extent via the skin as perspiration. However, water loss through perspiration is still considerably high as we live in a hot climate. The greater the body weight, activity level or temperature, the more water a person will lose. Small amounts are lost in faeces and from the lungs during perspiration. Water which is lost from the body must be constantly replaced in order to prevent dehydration.

### The thirst signal
Contrary to our drive to eat, our drive to drink is not as keen. Our thirst mechanism kicks in when we are already mildly dehydrated, in other words, thirst is a late signal. The situation gets worse with age and as you get older your thirst mechanism is affected. The body usually needs water long before a person feels thirsty. That is why it is important to get into the routine of drinking water and to drink water before thirst is noticed.

### Dehydration
Most people do not drink enough water and the body responds to this water deficiency (dehydration) in a variety of ways. Symptoms of dehydration include increased thirst, dry mouth, sticky saliva, light headedness, headache,
dizziness, confusion, reduced urine output and dark yellow urine. One of the best ways to recognize dehydration is to pay close attention to the colour of one’s urine. Ideally, light yellow to clear urine indicates adequate water intake. Dehydration is very dangerous for babies, small children and older adults.

## Water recommendation

An average adolescent and adult require a minimum of 2.5 litres of fluids every day, out of which 1.5 litres must be met by the intake of plain drinking water only. The exact water requirement will vary based on gender, age, body weight of an individual and life stage. Pregnant women require additional fluid replacement to ensure that foetal needs are met, as well as providing for expanding extra-cellular space and amniotic fluid. Lactating women have additional water requirements, of 1 litre per day for the first six months of lactation (total of 3 litres/ day). The elderly may not require additional volumes of water, but are at greater risk of dehydration due to decreased thirst sensations. Other susceptible individuals include those who sweat a lot e.g. those working in direct heat and athletes; smokers; people consuming alcohol; travellers on aeroplanes and those with severe vomiting and diarrhoea [10].
# 8. Reduce intake of foods high in salt and sugar to reduce the risk of diet-related diseases

‘Salt’ is a common name for sodium chloride (NaCl) and is important for regulating fluid balance, maintaining normal blood pressure, allowing the muscles to contract and enabling nerve transmission. Foods whether fresh or processed, usually supply the body with more than adequate amounts of salt.

## How much salt should we consume?

World Health Organisation (WHO) recommends that daily intake of salt in adults should be **≤ 5g per day** (i.e. ≤ 2g of sodium per day) to help reduce blood pressure and the risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke and coronary heart attack. In children the recommendation is **≤ 2g per day** (i.e. about 0.8g of sodium per day). Among children the adjustment of recommended sodium intake based on energy requirements is recommended because of the positive association between energy requirements and sodium intake [21].

Infants will get the right amount of salt through breast milk or infant formula, even when they start eating around 6 months, and there is no need to add salt to their foods. If children have too much salt, this could affect their health in future. Although our hot climate, can lead to excessive sweating, there is no need to consume more salt as we only lose a small amount through sweat. Most of what is lost is regained through food eaten. However it is important to drink plenty of water.

## Salt content of foods

Almost 75% of the salt we eat is already added into everyday foods, with most of the salt coming from processed food. Even certain products such as bread and breakfast cereals which might not necessarily taste salty contain added salt. A good way therefore to know how much salt is being consumed from processed food is by reading the food label. If salt is listed amongst the first ingredients, this means the product is high in salt, since ingredients are listed from highest to lowest amounts in a product.

## Ways to reduce salt intake

* Gradually reduce the amount of salt added food
* Replace salt with herbs, spices and other ingredients like thyme, parsley, rosemary, cinnamon, bilimbi, curry leaves, turmeric, lemon or lime juice, ginger, garlic, chili, onions and spring onions to dishes to enhance their flavours
* Make fresh stock and gravy using vegetables, fish and meat instead of using cubes or granules
* Do not add extra salt at the table
* Limit intake of foods high in salt as listed above

## Sugar and its health implications

Sugar is defined as any class of water-soluble crystalline carbohydrates obtained from various plants, especially sugar cane and sugar beet having a characteristically sweet taste and is classified as monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) or disaccharides (sucrose, lactose, maltose). Glucose and fructose are naturally found in fruits and vegetables, lactose is found in milk and milk products while maltose is naturally seen in cereal products.

Sugars can also be added to foods and drinks during processing in various forms such as white, brown and raw sugar, honey, syrups and extracts. There are many different names for sugar. Examples of common names are table sugar, brown sugar, molasses, honey, beet sugar, cane sugar, confectioner’s sugar, powdered sugar and raw sugar.

In March 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) published new guidelines recommending that adults and children alike reduce their consumption of free sugars. ‘Free sugars’ include monosaccharides and disaccharides added to foods and beverages by the manufacturer, the cook or the consumer, and sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices and fruit juice concentrates [22]. Free sugars contribute to the overall energy density of diets, and may promote a positive energy balance leading to weight gain.

Examples of foods/drinks with free sugars include chocolates, biscuits, ice cream, confectionaries and sugar-sweetened beverages. Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB) are drinks with added sugar including: non-diet soft drinks/ fizzy drinks, fruit juices, flavoured juice drinks, sports drinks, sweetened tea, coffee drinks, energy drinks, and electrolyte replacement drinks. For example, a can 375ml soft drinks contains over 9 teaspoons (64g of sugar). Such products are high in free sugars and can contribute to weight gain which can lead to obesity and associated conditions such as type 2 diabetes, heart disease, kidney diseases, non-alcoholic liver disease and dental caries [23, 24, 25].

### Recommendations for sugar

* The World Health Organisation (WHO) recommends a reduced intake of free sugars throughout the life course as excessive intake promotes weight gain and obesity. It also contributes to dental caries especially in young children [22].
* In both adults and children, WHO recommends reducing the intake of free sugars to less than ten per cent of total energy intake (strong recommendation), which is equivalent to 12 teaspoons based on an average intake of 2000 calories per day.
* WHO suggests a further reduction of the intake of free sugars to below five per cent of total energy intake which is equivalent to six teaspoons of sugar per day.
* For a child, intake should be less than the above values for adults although the amounts vary considerably based on gender and age of the child.
* Intake of free sugars is not considered an appropriate strategy for increasing caloric intake in individuals with inadequate energy intake if other options are available.
* A single teaspoon of sugar is around four grams of sugar. Therefore it is important to know how much sugar is in the food you are eating so you can ensure you stay within the recommended limits [22].

* Anything that ends with ‘ose’ is a form of sugar for e.g. maltose, dextrose and sucrose or anything which contains any form of syrups or concentrates contains sugar in them even if they claim to be sugar-free.
* While choosing breakfast cereals opt for whole grain varieties e.g. All Bran cereal, weetabix and oatmeal instead of sugar-coated ones like coco pops and fruit loops.

When consumption of alcohol is more than the body can handle the excess alcohol travels in the blood until it can be broken down. The alcohol circulating in the blood puts a strain on organs in the body including the brain resulting in a slowdown of bodily functions.

Alcohol is a concentrated form of energy with one gram of alcohol providing seven calories. Therefore, drinking alcohol adds more energy to the diet than the body requires, and any surplus energy that the body does not need is converted into fat, leading to weight gain.

It is therefore recommended that those who do not drink alcohol should continue to abstain from it whilst those who choose to drink should do so sensibly and remain within the set limits. Anyone intending to drive should avoid drinking alcohol.

6. Give your child animal food sources such as fish, meat, liver and eggs to promote growth and prevent anaemia

From birth to around six months, the child’s storage of iron in the body will start to decline. Iron from animal sources or haem iron is more readily absorbed by the body and should be included as early as possible when complementary feeding starts.

Non-haem iron comes from plant-based sources like beans, lentils, peas, green leaves like moringa and spinach and are not as readily absorbed by the body. They should be combined with a rich source of vitamin C to improve their absorption. Examples of vitamin C sources include fruits and vegetables like tomato, capsicum, guava, orange, and lime.

It is important to prevent iron deficiency anaemia in children as it can delay or permanently impair mental and motor development. Iron deficiency anaemia is common in children, and is associated with frequent infections, poor weight gain, developmental delay, impaired learning and behavioural disorders. Early diagnosis of iron deficiency anaemia is crucial below 36 months to prevent irreversible neurological damage. Iron deficiency anaemia is usually diagnosed using a blood test [33].

*   The early introduction of cow’s milk as a main drink before 12 months of age
*   Over-dependence on milk where it replaces iron-rich or iron-enhancing foods
*   Lack of iron-rich food sources being introduced as part of complementary feeding

Other common causes of iron deficiency anaemia may include:

*   Rare genetic conditions or diseases e.g. sickle cell disease
*   Blood loss

All children need some ‘down time’ even though they are not naturally inactive for long periods of time. Sitting in strollers, highchairs and car seats (being restrained) for long periods is not good for children’s health and development and goes against their natural tendency to be active and play. The use of baby jumpers and baby walkers is also discouraged. The evidence shows they can restrict the muscle development required for independent walking and may cause injuries [35].
# Section 3

## Guidelines for pregnant and lactating women
# 1. Enjoy a variety of foods from the five food groups every day to maintain good health

A balanced diet is very important when it comes to pregnancy and lactation, as it supports the mother’s and baby’s health. Specific nutrients needs will vary based on the stage of pregnancy, mother’s nutritional status, if there are multiple pregnancies, and if there are any underlying conditions in either mother or baby, amongst many other factors. There is no single food with all nutrients to meet the body needs. It is important therefore to include a variety of foods from all the five food groups to ensure that both the mother and the baby get the right types and amounts of nutrients they need.

During pregnancy there is great variability in the energy requirements between individuals in accordance to their body size and how active they are. Energy needs increase particularly during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. Pregnant women should therefore consume carbohydrate-rich foods that are also rich in other important nutrients such as vitamins and minerals like sweet potato, breadfruit, oats and bulgur wheat. Choosing local tubers for instance like cassava or sweet potatoes instead of white rice will not only provide energy but also fibre, B-vitamins and minerals, like potassium. Women should also eat a variety of foods from other foods groups such as beans and lentils, fruits and vegetables, fish, poultry, meat and milk.

Requirements for other nutrients also vary considerably but are usually adequately met through a well-balanced diet which includes foods from all the different food groups. Exceptions are that routine supplementation with folic acid is recommended from pre-conception through the first three months of pregnancy for optimal foetal development and to prevent neural tube defects. Iron supplementation is also advised throughout second and third trimesters.

Even for mothers with poor food intake, as a consequence of underlying conditions like morning sickness or heartburn, it is encouraged to include a variety of foods as can be tolerated. Having very limited food choices can increase the risk of nutritional deficiencies. A pregnant adolescent also has extra nutritional requirements compared to an adult pregnant woman, because of the additional demands of their own growth and of the baby.

Nutrients derived from foods consumed by the mother during lactation is passed on to the baby through the breast milk. It is therefore equally important to have a well-balanced diet when breastfeeding to ensure that the baby is getting all essential nutrients for his/ her growth and development. The type and amount of nutrients in mother’s milk will fluctuate at each feed based on the food eaten in the previous meal, as well as existing maternal stores for that particular nutrient. By including a variety of foods, the breast milk will become more nutrient-dense.
## 2. Eat different coloured fruits, vegetables and pulses (beans, lentils and peas) every day to keep your digestive system healthy

Consuming a variety of fruits, vegetables and pulses during pregnancy provides the body with a variety of vitamins especially vitamin C which acts as an antioxidant, protecting the body’s cells from damage and is also vital for the formation of collagen which is a structural protein. This is important in supporting increased tissue growth during pregnancy. They also provide minerals like potassium which plays an important role in acid-base regulation, fluid balance, muscle contraction and nerve conduction.

Including fruits and vegetables of different colours also means that mother and baby are exposed to a greater variety of phytochemicals which has been found to have anti-carcinogenic properties alongside a range of other benefits that each one conveys to the body. Different types of phytochemicals produced by plants give fruits and vegetables their different colour, odour, and flavour. Thus, each colour indicates an abundance of specific nutrients and antioxidants.

Many phytochemicals have antioxidant activities, helping the body fight free radicals and oxidative stress. During pregnancy, the woman’s immune system is weakened hence there is increased susceptibility to pathogens. Including adequate amounts and a diversity of fruits, vegetables and pulses daily can help to strengthen the immune system and boost the body’s ability to fight infections.

Including pulses regularly also provides mother and baby with protein, vitamins such as vitamin B1, which is important for energy production, minerals like iron which is crucial for blood oxygenation, and both soluble and insoluble fibre. Fruits and vegetables, like pulses, also provide fibre which is important for good digestive health.

Constipation is a common complication especially in the later stages of pregnancy as a consequence of altered gastrointestinal functions, decreased physical activity and dietary changes. Consuming plant food sources such as fruits, vegetables and pulses that are rich in fibre together with adequate hydration can help to prevent and alleviate constipation.

During lactation it is also important to include a variety of fruits, vegetables and pulses as the baby’s main source of nutrient is breast milk and the nutrient content of the milk depends on the type of food consumed by the mother.

The recommended portion size of fruits and vegetables for pregnant and lactating women is the same as for the general population with a minimum of two portions of fruits and three portions of vegetables, bringing to a total of five portions minimum recommended daily.
### 3. Choose whole grains and local tubers instead of rice for a healthy heart

Choosing wholegrain varieties and local tubers or staples will provide mother and baby with more nutrients in comparison to choosing more refined products like white rice or pasta. Examples of whole grains include oatmeal, corn, and bulgur wheat whilst local tubers or staples include options like cassava, sweet potato, breadfruit and plantain. This group mainly provides carbohydrate which is the main source of energy for the body and is needed by the pregnant women for her bodily functions and also to sustain her pregnancy and growing foetus.

Whole grains, local tubers or staples are generally higher in vitamins, minerals and dietary fibre. Dietary fibre is important for the body’s digestive health and regular bowel movement. During pregnancy there is a higher probability of constipation because of an increase in the hormone progesterone, which relaxes smooth muscles throughout the body, including the digestive tract but also as the pregnancy advances there is more stress on the intestines because of increased foetal size. This means that food passes through the intestines more slowly. It is thus beneficial for pregnant women to include more high-fibre foods instead of refined foods.

The portion size needed by pregnant women is variable. It will depend on the stage of their pregnancy, whether or not it is a multiple pregnancy, if there are underlying conditions, and also the person’s level of physical activity. It is recommended however that all pregnant women include a variety of foods from this group, preferably whole grains and local tubers. Ideally at least one portion of whole grains should be included in all meals to ensure that energy needs of both the mother and the baby are being met.

During lactation it is also important to include foods from this group as breastfeeding is an energy-consuming activity. Additionally the provision of fibre from wholegrain sources helps to keep the digestive system healthy and helps prevent constipation among mothers.
### 4. Eat a variety of fish as they are safe and important for the mother’s health and baby’s development

Fish is a rich source of many nutrients like protein and minerals such as iron, magnesium, potassium and zinc, and vitamins like vitamin D and B12 which are all important for the health of the mother and the baby. All types of fish should be included as they each have a unique nutritional profile with varying types and amounts of nutrients. In particular oily fish like mackerel, trevally, bonito, and fresh tuna should be eaten at least twice a week as they contain higher amounts of certain nutrients.

Oily fish have higher concentrations of vitamin B12, which is a vitamin only found in animal products. Vitamin B12 has an important role in pregnancy as it is involved in amino acid metabolism and DNA synthesis. This means that it supports increased tissue growth and attrition and also supports the baby’s growth. Oily fish also have higher concentrations of vitamin D, a fat-soluble vitamin, important for calcium homeostasis in the body. The active form of vitamin D aids in calcium absorption in the small intestine and regulates calcium excretion in the kidneys. It therefore has an important role for bone mineralisation in the baby.

Vitamin A (retinol) is also abundant in oily fish. Vitamin A is needed for normal development and differentiation of body issues hence it helps support the increased tissue growth in mothers during pregnancy but also essential for embryogenic development and foetal growth. Vitamin A is also needed for the synthesis of a pigment called rhodopsin in the retina which is needed for vision in dim light.

Inclusion of oily fish also provides omega-3 fatty acids, a family of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids. The two most beneficial omega-3 fatty acids are EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid) both found in highest concentration in oily fish, with anti-inflammatory and anti-thrombotic effects. EPA supports the heart and immune system whilst DHA supports the brain, eyes, and central nervous system, which is why they are important for pregnant and lactating women. EPA and DHA also contribute to the neurological and visual development of the baby. Deficiency of EPA and DHA in pregnancy can have negative effects on the baby’s development. An exclusively plant-based diet does not provide sufficient omega-3 fatty acids for the mother’s and baby’s needs. Although some seeds are high in the precursors of omega-3 fatty acids, the human body is not efficient in converting these into the active essential nutrients.

Overall, consuming a variety of fish on a regular basis during pregnancy is recommended and is safe. Concerns have been raised about the possible harmful effects of mercury in fish. Most fish do naturally contain some mercury, but at very lower levels, and no cases of mercury poisoning has ever occurred as a result of eating normal ocean fish. Moreover, the Seychelles Child Development Study, conducted over the past 30 years, has demonstrated clear benefits of fish consumption on children’s development and no convincing evidence of any harm to warrant any concern [36]. During lactation it is also important to continue to consume fish since nutrients such as omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin B12 and Vitamin D can be passed on to the infant through breast milk to continue to support the infant’s cognitive and visual development, growth and bone formation, respectively. The low levels of mercury in the fish consumed by the mother has no impact on the safety of breastfeeding.
## 5. Choose milk and milk products every day for stronger bones and teeth

Milk and milk products include milk, yoghurt and cheese, and provide important nutrients like protein, minerals like calcium, phosphorus and potassium as well as vitamins like vitamins A, D, B1, B2 and B12.

During pregnancy it is important to consume milk and milk products since nutrients like calcium, vitamin D, and phosphorus contribute to the mineralisation of baby’s bones and teeth (which are actually formed before birth). They also contain other nutrients like protein which aids in baby’s growth. Since the body becomes more efficient at absorbing calcium, calcium supplementation is not usually required so long as there is a dietary source.

As the requirement of calcium increases slightly during pregnancy, if the mother is not taking enough calcium-rich foods, the body will remove calcium from the mother’s stores, which is mainly her bones and teeth, for the baby’s development. To avoid or minimise this from happening, calcium supplements are generally advised if the mother is not consuming or has very low intake of milk and milk products and other rich sources of calcium, has low vitamin D status, is vegetarian or a teenager [11].

Plant-based alternatives like almond milk, soy milk and tofu that have ideally been fortified or natural sources like moringa leaves can be used as an alternative option by those not consuming any milk and milk products. Plant-based sources however are lower in protein and many of the vitamins and minerals found naturally in milk and milk products. For instance plant-based sources naturally lack vitamin B12 so it is preferable to choose those that are fortified. Additionally the bioavailability of certain nutrients, such as calcium, is much higher in milk, milk products from animal sources as compared to plant-based sources.

Whilst breastfeeding, the mother should also consume milk and milk products regularly as they are nutrient-dense. For those who are unable to consume milk and milk products, it is advisable to include plant-based sources like moringa leaves, dark green leafy vegetables, sardine eaten with bones, almonds with the skin on and tofu, together with a calcium supplement.

It is also essential that pregnant and lactating women avoids unpasteurized fresh milk as it contains bacteria that can be harmful during pregnancy or lactation and can cause food poisoning. Unpasteurized milk and milk products may contain *Listeria monocytogenes* which causes Listeriosis. Listeriosis can affect the fetus and lead to abortion, stillbirth or delivery of a sick infant.
6. Drink water throughout the day instead of sugar-sweetened drinks like fizzy drinks and juices to stay well hydrated

The water requirement of a woman increases during pregnancy and lactation. Overall, fluid needs increase during pregnancy in order to support the production of amniotic fluid, build new tissues and a higher blood volume. In addition, both pregnant and lactating women require water for digestion of food, maintaining normal fluid balance and excreting wastes and toxins.

During the lactation period, hydration becomes particularly important, since breast milk production significantly increases the mother’s water loss, as the water from the mother’s body is additionally lost through breast milk. It is recommended that a mother drinks a glass of water with all meals and during or immediately after breastfeeding. Dehydration is common among those who do not drink enough water while breastfeeding and may cause low milk supply. The symptoms of dehydration include dry mouth, headache, dizziness, decreased urine output and dark coloured urine.

During pregnancy the minimum intake should be at least 2½ (10 glasses) litres whereas during lactation the minimum intake should be at least 3 litres of water per day. Additional fluids can also be included but the requirements for water should be met. Therefore if a pregnant woman chooses to drink juices for example, then this is additional to her 2½ litres water recommendation.

Increased consumption of sugar-sweetened drinks e.g. fizzy drinks, cordials and juice leads to rapid increase in maternal and infant weight gain which can predispose the mother to gestational diabetes in pregnancy. Increased intake of caffeinated beverages like coffee, cola and tea, can raise the blood pressure as caffeine acts as a stimulant and can also lead to heart burn by causing increased production of stomach acid. On the other hand, water helps prevent urinary tract infection (UTI), constipation and dehydration during pregnancy and lactation, so water should be the preferred beverage of choice in order to remain well hydrated and prevent dehydration.
## 7. Reduce intake of foods high in salt and sugar to reduce the risk of diet-related diseases

Salt plays an important role in helping to regulate and maintain your body fluid but taking too much salt affects the kidneys, causing the body to retain water. This extra fluid results in greater blood volume, which causes blood pressure to rise.

As such it is important to reduce intake of salt and salty foods during pregnancy and lactation. Excessive salt intake can lead to high blood pressure which increases the risks of stroke and heart problems, as well as other complications. Salty foods include canned foods, crisps, sauces, processed meat e.g. sausages, luncheon meat, ham and bacon; sauces like tomato sauce, oyster sauce, soya sauce as well as stock cubes, yeast extracts like marmite and pickles; and salted fish/ meat. It is important to limit the use of salt when cooking and flavour the foods naturally with herbs, spices, lime and chili.

Additional to the high salt content, most of the processed meats are not thoroughly cooked during processing as they only undergo treatments like curing or smoking. Such products can lead to Listeriosis which can infect the foetus during pregnancy and cause food poisoning in the mother during both pregnancy and lactation. In addition, WHO classified processed meat as Group 1 carcinogenic to humans. For this reason it is better to avoid such products during pregnancy and lactation and choose more natural and whole foods.

During pregnancy, as a consequence of hormonal changes, many women have a craving for sweeter foods. Regular consumption of foods high in sugar like confectionary, sweet cakes and biscuits as well as sugar-sweetened beverages like fizzy drinks and juice can lead to excessive weight gain and its complications. Such foods may also displace other nutrient-rich foods in the diet of the mother during pregnancy and lactation when eaten in large amounts. Such displacement may cause nutritional deficiencies. This may be relevant in particular for women who exclude whole food groups such as not eating any fruits or vegetables. It is therefore best to choose fresh products as often as possible and limit the intake of ultra-processed foods.
# 8. Engage in physical activity every day for a healthy body weight

Physical activity is an important part of any healthy pregnancy. It can improve aerobic fitness, improve mental health and optimise gestational weight gain. Regular physical activity generally reduces the risk of many complications, such as depression, gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, and lower back pain by strengthening abdominal and back muscles. Moderate-intensity aerobic activity, such as brisk walking is safe for uncomplicated pregnancies for both the mother and the baby. Physical activity does not increase the risk of low-birth weight, early delivery, or early pregnancy loss, if done properly.

Some examples of safe moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity that can be undertaken during pregnancy includes: walking and swimming. Others such as jogging, stationary cycling, strength training, and low-impact aerobics can also be considered following consultation and approval by a medical practitioner before initiation. Any strenuous physical activity such as contact sports, heavy lifting, kickboxing and bike riding should be avoided as they can lead to complications.

Reduced physical activity or sedentary behaviour during pregnancy increases the risk of complications such as excessive weight gain, constipation and back pain. Once the baby is born it is good to continue being physically active. Physical activity will help the mother to lose the weight gained during pregnancy alongside a healthy and balanced diet. This is usually easier if the mother was physically active before and throughout pregnancy.

Similarly to the general population, pregnant or lactating women should do at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity per week (equivalent to 30 minutes a day, five days a week), during and after their pregnancy. It is best to spread this activity throughout the week to allow your body to have enough time to rest and recover. The activity can also be spread out into three bouts of ten-minute intervals throughout the day to make up the 30 minutes’ recommendation.

Pregnant and lactating women are encouraged to engage in activities that are feasible, enjoyable and easy to sustain. Alongside physical activity, adequate hydration and a varied diet with all food groups is also encouraged for improved overall health outcome of both the mother and the baby. Physical activity helps to gain the right amount of weight during pregnancy and avoids excessive weight gain.

9. **Do not drink alcohol, smoke cigarettes or use illegal drugs when pregnant and breast feeding** to prevent harming yourself and your baby

| Pregnant women should not drink alcohol at all. Alcohol can pass from the mother’s blood into the baby’s blood where it can damage and affect the growth of the baby’s cells. Brain and spinal cord cells are likely to have the most damage. Prenatal exposure of the foetus to alcohol can lead to a condition known as ‘foetal alcohol syndrome’ which is characterized by a broad range of anomalies listed below:

* pre- and postnatal growth retardation,
* the child having distinctive facial features including a small head, flat face, and narrow eye openings,
* pre-term delivery,
* central or craniofacial dysmorphia,
* neurological and behavioural disorders
* cognitive function disorders, which can persist into adulthood. |

Pregnant women are therefore advised not to consume any kinds of alcohol during pregnancy.

During lactation it is also not recommended to consume alcohol as the alcohol passes through the breast milk to the baby and can make the baby irritable and unsettled. When consumed in large amounts, alcohol can cause drowsiness, deep sleep, weakness, and abnormal weight gain in the infant, and the possibility of decreased milk-ejection reflex in the mother. Breastfeeding mothers should therefore be cautious about having alcohol as it can have an adverse effect on the baby’s health.

Smoking during pregnancy affects the baby’s health. The nicotine, carbon monoxide, and numerous other poisons that is being inhaled from a cigarette are carried through the bloodstream of the mother and goes directly to the foetus. Smoking while pregnancy can have the following effects:
* Lowers the amount of oxygen available to the mother and foetus
* Increases the baby’s heart rate
* Increases the chances of miscarriage and stillbirth
* Increases the risk of pre-term delivery and/or low birth weight baby
* Increases the risk of developing respiratory problems in foetus
* Increases the risk of birth defects
* Increases the risk of Sudden Infant Death Syndrome

Smoking during lactation decreases milk production in mothers and alters milk composition. In addition to risk of exposing the new-born to tobacco smoke, smoking during lactation may also cause nausea, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea among breast fed infants so it is advised not to smoke during the lactating period.

Substance abuse in pregnancy is associated with significant maternal and foetal morbidity. Drug abuse may also result in miscarriage, premature birth, low birth weight, and a variety of behavioural and cognitive problems in the child. A baby can also be born dependent on the drug, a condition called neonatal abstinence syndrome [37]. Substance abuse during lactation is also not advised as they pass through the breast milk and can have adverse effects on the baby.
10. Eat iron-rich foods such as fish, meat, green, leafy vegetables, and pulses every day to prevent anaemia

Iron is needed for the formation of haemoglobin in red blood cells of both mother and baby. The demands for iron continues to increase as pregnancy progresses due to the accumulation of iron by the foetus especially during the last trimester. During pregnancy however the body adapts to increased iron needs by mobilizing iron stores, increased absorption of iron from the diet, and reduced iron losses with the cessation of menstruation [11].

Including a variety of iron-rich food sources is recommended throughout pregnancy. Animal sources such as fish, poultry and meat contain a more bioavailable form of iron but plant sources can also provide adequate amounts of iron when combined with a Vitamin C source to enhance its absorption. Rich plant sources of iron include green leafy vegetables and pulses. Preparing and cooking these with other fruits or vegetables like tomatoes and capsicum that are rich in Vitamin C will allow more of the iron to be absorbed.

Women presenting with overt iron deficiency or iron deficiency anaemia at a pre-natal clinic visit should be given iron supplementation but also encouraged to include more iron-rich food sources in their diet.

During lactation, especially early on, women are encouraged to ensure that they prevent anaemia by including a variety of food sources that contain iron. This will ensure that their baby gets adequate amounts of iron in breastmilk especially those below six months.
## 11. Take folic acid and iron tablets everyday as prescribed during pregnancy to prevent birth defects and anaemia

Folic acid supplementation should begin prior to conception in planned pregnancies and continue in the first trimester of pregnancy. Supplementation is crucial because the amount of folic acid from food is insufficient to meet the demands of the foetus in that period. Folic acid deficiency can lead to ‘neural tube defect’ (NTD) in which the baby’s spinal cord and brain fail to develop properly.

The recommended amount of folic acid that should be taken for the first three months of pregnancy from supplements is 400µg per day. Women with certain conditions like diabetes or a history of NTD in a previous pregnancy are advised to take 5mg or 5000µg per day to further reduce the risk of NTD in their baby. Alongside the supplements, it is always good to include foods that are rich in folic acid such as beans, tomatoes, lentils, oranges moringa, and papaya throughout the pregnancy.

Iron demands increase in pregnancy and the mother is put at risk of anaemia due to the demands of the foetus. Haemoglobin levels measured at a prenatal clinic visit and throughout pregnancy will provide a good indication of whether or not supplements should be given. Iron supplementation is indicated for mothers with a haemoglobin value less than 11g/dL. One of the side-effects of iron supplementation is constipation especially when in the form of tablets. Some women tolerate the liquid form of iron better and this may be used alternatively provided it has an adequate amount of iron. Additionally pregnant women should also drink a lot of water, eat food high in fibre such as vegetables, fruits, pulses, and whole grains, to help relieve the symptoms of constipation.

The recommended daily intake of iron is equivalent to 60 mg of elemental iron, which is 300 mg ferrous sulphate heptahydrate, 180 mg ferrous fumarate or 500 mg of ferrous gluconate [38].

# Appendix 4: Additional information for breastfeeding

## Breast milk composition

**Colostrum** is the special milk that is secreted by the breast in the first 2–3 days after delivery which is a thick, sticky fluid high in protein, antibodies, white cells, growth factors and vitamin A. It is important that infants receive colostrum, and no other feeds, at this time because it helps protect babies against viruses and bacteria, and helps protect the digestive tract. It also has all the food and water the baby needs. Colostrum comes in very small amounts. This suits the baby’s very small stomach and the immature kidneys that cannot handle large volumes of fluid. 

Milk starts to be produced in larger amounts between 2 and 4 days after delivery, making the breasts feel full. The milk is then said to have “come in”. From day 7 to 14, the milk is called transitional, and after 2 weeks it is called mature milk.

**Preterm breast milk** - The milk of a mother giving birth before 37 weeks gestation, preterm breast milk, has more protein, higher levels of some minerals including iron, and more immune properties than mature milk, making it more suited for the needs of a premature baby. A mother’s milk can even be used before the baby is able to breastfeed. The mother can express her milk, and it can be fed to the baby with a cup, spoon or tube.

**Mature breast milk** - Mature breast milk contains all of the major nutrients – protein, carbohydrates, fat, vitamins, minerals and water in the amounts the baby needs. It changes in relation to the time of day, the length of breastfeeding, the needs of the baby, and diseases with which the mother has had contact. The components of breast milk provide nutrients as well as substances that help in digestion, growth, development and provide protection from infections. Breast milk continues to provide these nutrients, protection, and other benefits as the child grows, these components do not disappear at a certain age.

## Positioning and attachment

A mother needs help in the first few days to make sure that she is able to position and attach her baby correctly to the breast, to ensure that her baby is suckling effectively. For a mother to produce the milk that her baby needs, her baby must suckle often and suckle in the right way. A baby cannot get the milk by sucking only on the nipple. Good positioning helps to ensure that the baby suckles well and helps the mother to produce a good supply of breast milk.

It takes about 3 to 6 weeks for breastfeeding to become fully established and hence it is important that the baby is fed on demand during this period. The breasts produce milk in response to the baby’s suckling and therefore the more the baby is put to the breast, the more milk will be produced.

### How to help a mother to position her baby

1. **Baby’s head and body in line** - The baby’s body should be straight, not bent or twisted, but with the head slightly back.
2. **Baby held close to mother’s body** - The baby’s whole body should almost face his mother’s body. He should be turned away just enough to be able to look at her face.
3. **Baby supported** - the baby’s whole body should be supported with the mother’s arm along the baby’s back.
4. **Baby approaches breast, nose to nipple**

**Signs of good attachment are:**

* More of the areola is visible above the baby’s top lip than below the lower lip
* The baby’s mouth is wide open
* The baby’s lower lip is curled outwards
* The baby’s chin is touching or almost touching the breast.
# Signs of effective suckling

* The baby takes slow deep suckles, sometimes pausing.
* You may be able to see or hear your baby swallowing after one or two suckles.
* Suckling is comfortable and pain free for you.
* Your baby finishes the feed, releases the breast and looks contented and relaxed.
* The breast is softer after the feed.

## Storing Expressed Breast milk General guidelines

* Wash hands before handling breast milk
* Choose suitable container:
    * Glass/plastic with cover
    * Clean/sterile (wash with hot soapy water and rinse with hot water)
* Store only the amount required in one container that the baby takes at one feed
* Labelled with name and date (Use oldest milk first)
* Feeding of fresh milk is encouraged

### Storage

| Storage method | Time |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Fresh milk** | |
| Room Temperature (25° – 37°C) | 4 hrs. |
| Air condition Room 15° – 25°C | 8 hrs |
| < 15°C | 24 hrs |
| Refrigerator (2° – 4°C) | < 4 days |
| **Frozen Milk** | |
| Freezer compartment inside refrigerator (1 door fridge) | 2 weeks |
| Freezer part of a refrigerator freezer (2 doors fridge) | 3 months |
| Separate deep freeze | 6 months |
| Thawed in a refrigerator | 24 hrs. |

### Thawing

* Frozen breast milk may be thawed slowly in a refrigerator and used within 24 hours
* It can be defrosted by standing in a jug of warm water and used within one hour
* Do not boil milk or heat on stove, over a direct fire or in microwave oven (destroys some of its properties and can scald baby’s mouth)
* Warm only the amount of milk that will be used at one feeding because milk cannot be saved once it has been warmed
* The fat in the milk may separate out in small globules therefore gently shake it to recombine the fat with the rest of the liquid before giving to baby

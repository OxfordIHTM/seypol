## Scope of this guidance
This guidance is intended for use by clinical commissioning groups to support them in commissioning rehabilitation services for their local population. It may also be of use to others with an interest in rehabilitation.

**The document outlines:**
- What rehabilitation is, i.e. scope, breadth and depth.
- The components of good quality rehabilitation.
- How to know whether the services that are being commissioned are of good quality.
- How to compare rehabilitation services locally, regionally and nationally.

The guidance also provides access to a great many resources within its reference list, hyperlinks and comprehensive appendices.

*The document will be reviewed in 2017/18.*

## Key Messages

- **Rehabilitation Intervention Underpins All Conditions**: It is a philosophy of care that helps people fulfil their maximum potential and optimise their contribution to society.
- **Rehabilitation is Everyone’s Business**: It is an active and enabling process that focuses on the impact of a condition on a person's life rather than just the diagnosis.
- **Runs Through the Life Course**: Rehabilitation is a continuous journey from childhood through adulthood.

### WHO Objectives for Rehabilitation
- Preventing the loss of function
- Slowing the rate of loss of function
- Improving or restoring function
- Compensating for lost function
- Maintaining current function

## What is Rehabilitation?

### Definition and Scope
Rehabilitation is a philosophy of care that ensures people are included in their communities, employment, and education. It focuses on the impact of health conditions or disabilities on a person’s life.

**Spectrum of Services:**
- **Habilitation**: Developing skills for the first time (e.g., children with developmental difficulties).
- **Recovery**: Recovering from unexpected illness (e.g., stroke, surgery, cardiac events).
- **Management**: Managing long-term conditions or exacerbations.
- **Self-management**: Enabling individuals to manage their own health and reduce secondary problems.

### Rehabilitation as Prevention
Rehabilitation can be a preventative measure (prehabilitation), such as prioritizing exercise post-stroke to reduce the risk of further vascular events or pre-surgery intervention to improve functional outcomes.

### A Person-Centred Approach
Fundamental to rehabilitation is a person-centred approach, ensuring support is built around individual circumstances. This includes:
- **Self-management**: Empowering people to take control of their condition.
- **Personal Health Budgets**: Providing choice and control over healthcare.
- **Co-production of Care Plans**: Designing services with the people who use them.
- **Capacity and Activation**: Assessing the individual's ability and motivation to change.

### Rehabilitation Specialists
Specialist knowledge is provided by various professionals, including:
- Art/Music/Dramatherapists
- Audiologists, Dietitians, Orthoptists, Podiatrists
- Clinical Psychologists and Psychiatrists
- Doctors of Rehabilitation Medicine
- Occupational Therapists and Physiotherapists
- Speech and Language Therapists
- Specialist Social Workers and Nurses

## Rehabilitation Model

Rehabilitation follows a **Biopsychosocial Model** (based on WHO ICF). The pathway is divided into phases based on complexity and independence:

| Phase | Description | Example Services | Funding Source (Typical) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Phase 1** | Acute complex specialised rehabilitation | High physical dependency, renal, cardiac, oncology | National (NHS England) |
| **Phase 2** | Specialist rehabilitation | Supra-district services, spinal injury units | National or Local (CCG/LA) |
| **Phase 3** | Specialist rehabilitation | MSK therapy, vocational, stroke rehab | Local |
| **Phase 4** | Non-specialist rehab (trained staff) | Health centres, gyms, community centres | Local or Private |
| **Phase 5** | Structured peer support | Walking groups, dance clubs, stroke clubs | Private, Voluntary, Charity |
| **Phase 6** | Community assets | Parks, cycle paths, outdoor gyms, apps | Private, Voluntary, Charity |

## Principles and Expectations

### Expectations of Good Services ("I" Statements)
1. I have knowledge of, and access to, joined-up rehabilitation services.
2. My rehabilitation focuses on all my needs, including return to work.
3. My care is a partnership between me and my providers.
4. I am supported to self-care and self-manage.
5. My goals are clear, meaningful, and measured.
6. I am supported to reach my potential.
7. I can refer myself to services easily.
8. I have a single point of contact.
9. People important to me are recognized and supported.
10. I am provided with information on my progress.

### Principles of Good Services
- Optimise physical, mental, and social wellbeing.
- Recognise carers as critical team members.
- Instil hope and balance risk.
- Use an individualised, goal-based approach.
- Ensure early and ongoing assessment.
- Support self-management through education.
- Use a wide variety of evidence-based interventions.
- Deliver via integrated multi-agency pathways.
- Maintain strong leadership and accountability.
- Share good practice and contribute to evidence.

## Why Commission Rehabilitation?

### Strategic Ambitions
- Prevention and reduction in demand for health services.
- Support for employment and self-management.
- Reduction in unplanned admissions and length of stay.
- Integration of care (breaking silos between mental and physical health).

### Economic Benefits
- **Work**: Vocational rehab gets people back to work sooner.
- **Falls**: Physiotherapy reduces fall-related costs (which cost the NHS >£2.3bn/year).
- **Hospital Stay**: Integrated services can reduce length of stay (e.g., amputee rehab from 40 to 22 days).
- **Long-term care**: Neuro-rehabilitation reduces the need for continuing care.

## Checklist and Ten Top Tips

**Checklist for Commissioning:**
- [ ] People-centred services
- [ ] Consideration of existing guidance and standards
- [ ] Integrated children and young people’s services
- [ ] Commissioning for the whole pathway

**Ten Top Tips:**
1. Recognise the rehabilitation you already commission.
2. Have ambition for your services and the people they serve.
3. Make services "join up" with common principles.
4. Treat rehabilitation as integral, not an "add-on".
5. Identify common outcome measures for benchmarking.
6. Consider the range of delivery settings (community, 3rd sector).
7. Take a strategic view to "invest to save".
8. Cross-check local services against the rehabilitation model.
9. Ask for advice and support via professional forums.
10. Engage providers and service users for continuous improvement.

## Benchmarking Tool

Commissioners should monitor services using a tool covering:
- **Service Definition**: Team name, specification, and pathways.
- **Provision**: Site, frequency, and staff mix.
- **Volume**: Annual referrals, wait times, and discharge criteria.
- **Quality**: Outcome measures, KPIs, and patient involvement.

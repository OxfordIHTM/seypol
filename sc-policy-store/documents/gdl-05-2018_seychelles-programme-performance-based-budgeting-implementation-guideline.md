## Purpose of the Guideline

This Guideline set out the PPBB System in the Seychelles. It is intended to guide the Ministry of Finance, Trade and Economic Planning (MoFTEP) and the MDAs on the steps required to first implement a PPBB medium-term budget in an MDA, as well as on the routine processes and institutions of the system once implemented.

The Guideline is a living document, reflecting the learning of the Government of Seychelles in implementing PPBB medium-term budgeting. This is the seventh version, issued in April 2018, and takes into account the learning from implementing PPBB since 2012.

The Guideline therefore replaces all previous versions.

#### Section 1: PPBB in Seychelles: Implementation and main concepts
This section explains the purpose, underlying concepts and benefits of PPBB, as well as the approach to implementing PPBB in the Seychelles.

**A. The purpose of PPBB**

Programme performance-based budgeting (PPBB) is a budgeting and reporting system which aims to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of public expenditure. The benefits of PPBB are:

*   **Oversight:** there is better information to see whether plans and budgets are linked to government’s priorities.
*   **Managerial:** less micro-management by higher levels of the public-sector and more commitment from operational levels to achieve policy objectives.
*   **Political:** allows government to be clearer on the link between policies, expenditure, and outcomes.

## C. Implementing PPBB in the Seychelles
Between 2013 and 2019, the PPBB System has been implemented as follows:

* **2013:** PPBB started in the Seychelles with two pilot ministries. The Ministry of Fisheries and Agriculture (MOFA) and Ministry of Education (MOE) [and their associated agencies] began to implement PPBB for the 2015 fiscal year;
* **2014:** MOFA and MOE prepared a budget in PPBB format;
* **2015:** MOFA and MOE implemented their PPBB Budget; and three more pilot ministries started preparing PPBB for the 2016 fiscal year i.e. Finance Trade and the Blue Economy (now MOFTEP), Home Affairs, and Land Use and Housing.
* **2016:** All MDAs started budgeting in PPBB format without performance information (Presentational PPBB)
* **2017 onwards:** All MDAs moved onto the full PPBB systems (with Performance Information) in two sets;
* **2018:** All MDAs are planning, budgeting and implementing using PPBB for the 2019 budget. This includes a full strategic medium-term budget and setting performance targets against which they report to the National Assembly.

The following improvements will be made to the system in future:

* **PPBB in budget execution:** MoFTEP will continue to restrict movement between the main categories of economic expenditure (personnel remuneration, goods and services, and capital [PSIP] allocations) during budget execution. Major virement between programmes requires MoFTEP permission.
* **PPBB in budget reporting:** All MDAs that submitted budgets with performance information for 2018, will provide Section 31 Reports in 2019. The remainder will provide their first Section 31 Reports in 2020, on the 2019 budget.
* **PPBB in auditing:** The Auditor General will be phasing in the auditing of performance information as part of annual audits, from 2020 on the 2019 budget.
* **Improving the PPBB system over time:** The GoS acknowledges that improving the quality of budgeting in a PPBB system is a long-term undertaking. Therefore, the GoS will continue to roll-out and imbed the PPBB system by pursuing the following:
    * Strengthening the quality of planning;
    * Improving the coverage and quality of programme performance information and the systems used to manage the information, including linkages between the PPBB reform and the development of a monitoring and evaluation system (M&E system);
    * Allowing more expenditure discretion to MDAs;
    * Continuously improving PPBB documentation;
    * Introducing in depth programme reviews; and
    * Providing on-going PPBB training.

## Important shifts from the previous budgeting system to PPBB

* Improved linkage of strategic planning to policy;
* Management and evaluation linked to PPBB are in place;
* A strategic budgeting phase is part of budget preparation, giving MDAs more discretion at the finalisation stage of budget preparation;
* The National Assembly are given better information to link the budget to performance during budget approval and budget oversight;
* In-year internal and public reporting are not only on financial information, but on progress towards the achievement of the targeted results.; and
* Audit practices will change to include an audit of performance information.

PPBB is a three-phased budget preparation process which includes top down and bottom up processes

* The PPBB medium-term budget process comprises top-down and bottom-up mechanisms;
* Top down includes the President, Cabinet, National Assembly and Senior Management, both verbally and through policy and communiques;
* Bottom up includes government officials, office workers, unit heads, sub-programme managers, programme managers and management committees. (see box overleaf for a graphic explanation of the top-down-bottom up budget process)

## B. New Structures, Roles and Responsibilities in PPBB

### Portfolios
The PPBB reform introduces working at the portfolio level.

* A portfolio equals a ministry plus its departments and agencies within a functional sector of government for which a minister is politically accountable.
* MoFTEP in coordination with Cabinet, will determine portfolios.
* A portfolio will fall within a functional sector of government, but may be a sub-section, if the sector is split across two ministers. It is possible for a minister to be responsible for more than one portfolio, if the MDAs under his/her oversight do not belong to one functional sector.

### PPBB Portfolio Working Group (PWG)
Portfolio Working Groups are now being introduced in PPBB. The role of the Portfolio Working Group is to:

* Allocate resources within a single ceiling between MDAs in the Portfolio in the MTES phase
* Review MDA budget proposals and performance across a portfolio

Portfolio Working Groups should meet quarterly and would typically be constituted as follows:

* Principal Secretaries
* Director Generals
* Agency Chief Executive Officers
* Heads of Account
* Human Resource Directors
* Special Advisors (if applicable).

### MDA Accounting Officers
The MDA Accounting Officer (PS, CEO; Director-General) is accountable to the Minster for the performance of their MDA. He/she, therefore is accountable for planning and budgeting and needs to lead and manage the entire PPBB process.

## MDA PPBB Working Groups

A well-functioning PPBB WG is the cornerstone of the success of PPBB implementation. Each MDA should have a PPBB Working Group (PPBB WG).

The PPBB WG should meet monthly throughout the year, and weekly during budget submission periods. Instead of attempting to set up an entirely new structure, the PPBB WG can be accommodated in the regular MDA management meetings.

### The role of MDA PPBB WG in Budget Preparation
* Ensure sector and portfolio strategic plans are incorporated in budget planning at programme level;
* Decide on allocations across programmes, based on MDA strategic priorities;
* Coordinate new spending proposals across all programmes;
* Work with the Head of Accounts to discuss the implications of trade-offs between programme’s spending priorities;
* Coordinate budget (MTES and PPBB) submissions; and
* Ensure budget (MTES and PPBB) submissions are submitted before deadline.

### The role of the MDA PPBB WG in Budget Implementation
* Coordinate MDA procurement plans;
* Coordinate MDA cash flow projections;
* Ensure consistency and relevance of performance information;
* Decide on measures required to monitor programme and project implementation;
* Set rules on expenditure signoff;
* Design a budget and performance reporting and monitoring cycle; and
* Prepare for monthly budget review meetings; quarterly budget review meetings; and the mid-year review process.

### Composition of the PPBB WG
The PPBB WG should comprise the following people:
* The Permanent Secretary or CEO;
* Head of Accounts;
* Special Advisor (if applicable);
* Human Resource Director; and
* PPBB Programme Managers and Sub-programme Managers.

## MDA PPBB Technical Teams

The PPBB Technical Team carries out the technical application of the work of the PPBB WG, including:
* Delegating responsibility for the MDAs Microsoft Excel Budget Tools (normally to the Head of Accounts/Head of Accounts);
* Ensuring preparation of the narratives to accompany budget tables in the MTES budget submissions and PPBB statements; and
* Ensuring correct pay roll coding is implemented in liaison with DPA.

*(The PS/CEO may not attend all technical team meetings, but should be appraised of issues on a regular basis.)*

### Composition of the PPBB Technical Team
The PPBB Technical Team should comprise the following people:
* Head of Accounts or officer fulfilling head of accounts duties
* Human Resource Director
* Designated PPBB Programme Managers

## PPBB Programme Managers
A Programme Manager is the senior official who is responsible for a budget programme (or sub-programme) within the PPBB system of budget management. This would usually be the division or unit manager, or director of the administrative unit funded by the programme.

### Programme Manager’s role in Budget Preparation
* Attend PPBB WG meetings;
* Ensure sector and portfolio strategic plans are incorporated in budget planning at programme and sub-programme level;
* Work with the Head of Accounts to discuss trade-offs between their sub-programme’s spending priorities and those of other programmes within the MDA;
* Set performance measures and targets for their programmes and sub-programmes;
* Prepare budget (MTES and PPBB) submissions;
* Identify, cost and motivate new spending proposals;
* Check the accuracy of the coding of the MDA payroll (NB new staff and positions and staff changed);
* Ensure the sub-programmes falling into their programme submit budget proposal and that these are incorporated in the MDA budget;
* Decide on any new use of cost-centres and apportion programme resources to administrative cost-centres;
* Finalise operational plans given programme resources; and
* Adjust services, outputs and targets of their programme and sub-programmes according to the final budget allocation

### Programme Manager’s role in Budget implementation
* Attend PPBG WG meetings
* Manage all aspects of delivery for their programme and sub-programme;
* Attend budget management and reporting cycle meetings, including monthly budget review meetings by the PPBB WG; quarterly budget review meetings; and the mid-year review process;
* Ensure that programme resources are allocated and used to achieve target performance are in line with the approved programme budget and any further restrictions imposed by the MoFTEP on virement of resources;
* Prepare programme and sub-programme procurement plans;
* Prepare programme and sub-programme cash flow projections (by administrative unit/ blue level of economic classification);
* Design a budget and performance recording, reporting and monitoring cycle;
* Break down annual performance measure targets into quarterly targets;
* Decide on measures required to monitor programme and project implementation;
* Implement rules on expenditure signoff;
* Monitor the achievement of programme and sub-programme performance targets, to take corrective action when material deviations occur;
* Report against performance targets to the MDA management and the PPBB WG;
* Monitor programme expenditure, and take corrective action when resource utilisation is materially above or below projected use (ex post control);
* Monitor policy performance in the programme, and progress against performance measure targets, including both the PPBB measures and additional internal measures;
* Prepare reports on programme implementation, both financial and non-financial, to MDA management as determined by the MDA protocol, and formal inputs into external reporting; and
* Comply with MDA, MOFTEP and other reporting requirements.

### Section 1. What is PPBB?

1. Programme performance-based budgeting (**PPBB**) comprises budgeting for and reporting on public expenditure by programmes, while taking into account the performance of the programmes and associated public sector institutions, given allocations. In this way, PPBB improves effectiveness and efficiency of public expenditure.

2. From an oversight perspective, the aim of PPBB is better information on the link between government’s policy priorities and plans, and the use of its resources. From a managerial perspective, the aim is for less micro-management by higher levels of the public sector hierarchy, in return for commitments in terms of policy results by lower levels. From a political perspective, PPBB allows governments to be clearer on the link between policies, expenditure, and outcomes. This means changed roles for different actors compared to a traditional line-item system (Box 1).

#### Box 2. Roles of budget actors in a PPBB system

In 2016 the remainder of Government MDAs designed their programme structures, migrated and costed their 2017 budgets on a programme format and submitted their budget proposals to the MoFTEP in this format. The allocations to MDAs were then also in the new programme format. This was termed ‘presentational PPBB’ and differs from full implementation insofar as the 2016 new MDAs did not develop performance information or set performance targets.

7. In 2017 and 2018, for the 2018 and 2019 budgets respectively, the remainder of Seychelles MDAs moved onto the full PPBB systems in two sets. This means that from the 2019 budget all MDAs are planning, budgeting and implementing on a programme-basis, undertake full strategic medium-term budget preparation, and set performance targets against which they report to the National Assembly.

## Further development of the PPBB system

### PPBB in budget execution
8. As PPBB affects not only budget preparation, but also budget execution and reporting, programme-based virement controls are being used from the 2017 budget for all MDAs. In PPBB the MoFTEP will continue to restrict movement between the main categories of economic expenditure (personnel remuneration, goods and services, and capital (PSIP) allocations) during budget execution. For the time being, current restrictions on the movement of funds between the main economic items of expenditure for example within goods and services, also require MoFTEP approval. As experience with PPBB grows, these may be relaxed. In PPBB however, from the 2017 budget for all MDAs, virement between programmes requires MoFTEP permission.

9. The introduction of Public Financial Management Act Section 31 performance reports is linked to the roll-out of PPBB. ## Improving the PPBB system over time

13. The GoS acknowledges that improving the quality of budgeting in a PPBB system is a long-term undertaking. Therefore, the Government of Seychelles will continue to roll-out and imbed the PPBB system by pursuing the following:

* **Strengthening the quality of planning.** The quality of PPBB is closely linked to the quality of planning. If ministries do not undertake quality medium-term planning, with a strong analytical and costing base, it is unlikely that their future programme budgets will provide the intended strong link between national and sector priorities. The introduction of strategic medium-term budgeting and expenditure planning in the rollout phase set out above, is only a first step in building the necessary systems and capacity. Improving the quality of planning will take on-going effort, including strengthening strategic planning, improving the use of sector diagnostics and data, and the costing of on-going services and new programmes as a basis for informed allocations in line with priorities.
* **Improving the coverage and quality of programme performance information** and the systems used to manage the information, including linkages between the PPBB reform and the development of a monitoring and evaluation system (M&E system) in government. It is accepted that MDAs will initially develop performance information frameworks using fewer rather than more measures and focusing on the quality of the data. Over time, however, MDAs will be required to systematically improve the coverage and quality of their performance measures. At the same time, MDAs will be required to develop effective systems to manage performance information, including its collection, verification, storage, and use, which will serve both the PPBB and M&E systems of government.
* **Allowing more expenditure discretion to MDAs.** Over time the MoFTEP will allow more expenditure discretion to MDAs that are performing in terms of financial and policy results, including by increasing the thresholds where MoFTEP permission to shift funds between programmes and line items is required.
* **Continuously improving PPBB documentation.** It is likely that the GoS will adjust the PPBB documentation to facilitate quality NA oversight.
* **Introducing in depth programme reviews.** A performance budgeting system comprises two components: on-going performance monitoring through programme performance indicators and targets, and occasional more in-depth programme reviews. These programme expenditure reviews (PERs) will be done at the level of a sector (or a portfolio as defined below), at the level of an MDA, or at the level of a specific service within an MDA. They would be aimed at an in-depth consideration of the effectiveness and efficiency of expenditure and service delivery, and generate recommendations that could lead to in-depth changes to MDAs service delivery choices. They would normally be undertaken jointly by the MoFTEP and the sector/portfolio/MDA concerned, and may involve other central agencies like the Vice President’s Office and the Department of Public Administration. Over a medium-term period of ten to fifteen years, the MoFTEP would aim to review most key services in government. Introducing PERs however is not part of the current wave of reform, and will occur only after 2019.

14. **Providing on-going PPBB training.** Since 2015 the roll-out of PPBB has been supported by the Guy Morel Institute (GMI) of the University of Seychelles in partnership with the MoFTEP. This was through a PPBB module for the Diploma on Public Financial Management, and through short courses. Over time these courses will be adjusted to reflect modifications to the reform, and repeated for new entrants into PPBB-relevant posts.

> **Box 3. What is an MTES and its role in the central budget process?**
> 
> A medium-term expenditure strategy (MTES) is a tool that allows MDAs to examine how they are using their existing budgets and reprioritise existing funding from obsolete programmes/ projects and lower priority spending to new policy priorities and higher priority spending.
> 
> * **Projecting a baseline:** The tool requires the MDA PPBB Teams to project the forward cost of delivering services without any change in delivery modes based on their most recent expenditure outturn, but taking into account all existing decisions with expenditure implications that may not yet be reflected in these outturns. This projection is called a baseline projection. The projections are done as a first step, in March.
> * **Assessing spending space:** A next step – starting once the MTES circular and ceilings have been issued -- is to consider, given the indicative MTES ceilings issued, what the difference between the indicative MTES ceiling and the baseline projection is, as an indication of the funding the MDA has available to introduce new initiatives or services, or increase spending on existing initiatives/services.
> * **Assessing how to address new priorities and costing the proposals:** The third step is for the MDA to assess how much it will cost to deliver its new priorities, or increase delivery on existing services, and to prioritise which of these priorities would be the most important to fund.
> * **Increasing spending space:** As MDAs are not allowed to submit expenditure proposals above the indicative ceiling, it is up to the MDA to reprioritise spending from its baseline to fund its new priorities. This can be done by trying to deliver services more cheaply, or reducing the level of some lower priority existing services to fund new priorities or higher priority existing services.
> 
> These key strategic decisions -- how much can be shifted in the baseline from which existing activities and prioritising proposals for expenditure increases – are at the heart of an MTES. They are captured – supported by analysis, costing and associated performance projections -- in an MTES document, which is submitted to the MoFTEP as a first strategic budget proposal.
> 
> MDAs submit MTESs within indicative ceilings, which the MoFTEP to make decisions on which MDA proposals to approve in order to issue finalised budget ceilings at the start of the detailed process.

## Portfolios

31. The PPBB reform introduces working at the portfolio level in the budget system to coordinate policies and
expenditure across associated MDAs. A portfolio equals a ministry, its departments and associated agencies
within a functional sector of government. The guiding principle for forming portfolios is that the portfolio must

politically accountable. A portfolio therefore will fall within a functional sector of government, but may be a sub-
section of it, if the sector is split across two ministers. This also implies that it is possible for a minister to be
responsible for more than one portfolio, if the MDAs under his/her oversight do not belong to one functional
sector. The MoFTEP in coordination with Cabinet, will determine portfolios.

## Ministries, departments and agencies

32. Unique budget structures are associated with ministries, departments and agencies. When a ministry
comprises one coherent functional sector of government, there will be one budget structure (and PPBB statement)

departments responsible for different functional sectors of government, each department will have its own budget
structure (and PPBB statement), such as the Ministry of Habitat, Infrastructure and Land Transport. All agencies
will have a single budget structure.

## PPBB Programme Managers

40. A Programme Manager (or sub-programme manager) is the senior official who is responsible for a budget programme (or sub-programme) within the PPBB system of budget management. This would usually be the division or unit manager, or director of the administrative unit funded by the programme.

* During budget implementation, the Programme Manager has responsibility

An issue arises however, when the relationship between administrative divisions or units and programmes is not one-on-one. For example, when a programme funds more than one unit of which the managers have equal status in the organogram of the organisation, which manager will take responsibility for the full programme?

44. When MDAs appoint Programme Managers in circumstances where the relationship is not one-on-one with administrative divisions and units (or cost centres), appointment of Programme managers will follow the accountability for resources principle. This would apply as follows:

*   When more than one unit (or cost centre) is funded by the same programme and the heads of all units are at an equal level in the MDA organogram, but programme resources are used predominantly by one unit, the Programme Manager will be the head of that unit, but with the heads of other units being responsible for the resources allocated to their units in the apportionment of programme resources to the administrative classification. The Programme Manager will be responsible for approving resources above a certain threshold, and collecting information from other managers in the programme for other responsibilities.
*   When units (or cost centres) are funded roughly equally, the Programme Manager responsibility will move one level up in the organisation’s organogram, with the managers of the units (or cost centres) being funded bearing the primary responsibility for resources allocated to their units in the apportionment exercise. #### SECTION 1: Defining budget programmes
Budget programmes are coherent groupings of activities that are designed to fulfil common objective(s). The programme budget structure of a public body must represent all the spending of body arranged into coherent programmes and sub-programmes, reflecting the objectives associated with the public body.

The programme budget structure of an MDA is an additional dimension in the budget classification and chart of accounts, as opposed to the administrative and economic classification.

This section provides answers to FAQs on PPBB Programmes.

**What are key features of a budget programme structure?**

A PPBB budget programme structure:
* Allows MDAs to budget by objective;
* Is set for the medium/long term;
* Has coherent, functional groupings of services;
* Does not change the mandate of the MDA; and
* Does not need to reflect administrative structures.

**What is the relationship between PPBB budget programmes and MDA administrative structures?**

PPBB budget programmes do not need to reflect the GoS administrative structures. Programme structures allow the GoS to budget by objective, while administrative structures allow the GoS to control expenditure by unit.

**How many budget programmes should there be per MDA?**

Too many budget programmes would bury central decision-makers under too many small allocation choices and will lead to a highly fragmented programme budget which is difficult to implement. Too few budget programmes makes prioritising difficult for decision makers as they can’t see clear links between services and objectives. Programmes should:
* Be formulated at the level MOFTEP will make decisions about expenditure priorities;
* Be approximately 20- 33% and not less than 10% of an MDA budget with 3-5 Programmes per MDA (these are rough benchmarks).

In addition:
* Complex mandates and large budgets may have more programmes;
* Small agencies and boards would probably require only 1 Programme;
* The final number of programmes must be approved by MOFTEP.

**When should sub-programmes be created?**
* When there are distinct sub-functions/objectives within large programmes;
* When it allows for better budget choices and is necessary for transparency;
* When functions fall below the level of the MDAs programme hierarchy and are sufficiently distinct;
* When allocations to function/sub-function are large enough in terms of volume of funding (having a sub-programme budget of a few million rupees, makes MDA prioritising cumbersome);
* When salaries and goods and services of a sub-programme are distinct from other functions; and
* If their creation, does not lead to a fragmented budget, which is difficult to implement.

Yes (even if they are small and have 1 programme).

### (h) What happens to the development budget?

71. In Seychelles the unitary budget comprises two components which are differentiated in terms of the purpose of expenditure (operational versus development or investment) and which cuts across Government Finance Statistics economic categories of expenditure. Personnel expenditure, goods and services and minor capital outlays (such as expenditure on computers and furniture) are considered operational or recurrent cost, differing from expenditure on projects under government’s Public Sector Investment Programme. The two components are treated differently in the budget process.

72. The PPBB budget – which plans holistically for all expenditures required to achieve objectives – is the key mechanism to integrate these two components. Programmes will therefore include both recurrent and PSIP expenditure. It is important to plan for and reflect the cost of development, investment, and capital projects together with operational expenditure by programme so that:

* (i) the appropriate mix and timing of expenditures can be determined to achieve objectives cost-effectively;
* (ii) programmes can be compared against each other and the inputs, outputs, and outcomes of programmes can be evaluated against their full cost.

The consolidated programme budget of an MDA will show both the recurrent and the PSIP budget by sub-programme and by economic classification of expenditure. The PSIP tables will remain in the consolidated budget document, reflecting development expenditure across government.

73. At the MDA level, therefore, the PPBB process plan for operational and PSIP expenditure in an integrated manner. Furthermore, in Y0 for the Y+1 budget year, the MDAs will need to motivate for funding for projects based on programme-based medium-term expenditure planning. In other words, the contribution of a project to the achievement of outcome and output targets of the programme will be the basis on which projects are assessed. Projects that receive allocations under the Public Sector Investment Programme (PSIP) will also be reflected in the MDAs PPBB statements aligned to the programme under which they fit.

74. At the centre however, there are some differences in how recurrent and PSIP expenditure are treated. For example,

* PSIP allocations are not incremental but are the sum total of all approved projects. When a project stops, the associated allocation also stops.
* There are additional requirements to motivate PSIP expenditure requests coming from the public investment process rules.
* PSIP projects above a threshold require approval by the Public Investment Committee, reflecting the importance of investing in the right things across government and the need to assess the feasibility of investment spending.

### (i) What happens when during implementation, an agency or function shifts in or out of a ministry?

76. A PPBB budget is structured according to functions. When an agency or function shifts in or out of a Ministry during implementing the PPBB, the budget structure will adjust depending on where the function fits into the structure. For example, if it is a function, it may come in as a new sub-programme, bringing the resources attached to the function along. Or, if there is already a sub-programme in the Ministry that undertakes that function, the expenditure for the function will be merged into the existing sub-programme. An agency would have its own programme budget structure. If agencies shift during the roll-out of the reform, into a Ministry implementing PPBB, there would need to be intensive work with the agency to develop programme structures so that it can plan and report as the same way as the rest of the portfolio.

### (j) What happens when a programme cuts across ministries?

77. There are different uses of the word programme. In the PPBB system budget programmes function within the appropriation to government entities. A “programme” that cuts across government is not the same as a budget programme, it is a policy or strategic planning programme or initiative that is being implemented within or through the budget programmes of the ministries involved. An example of a policy programme is a cross-government climate change programme. However, it is still a programme that requires resources to be committed, tracked and reported against. The PPBB system will include the mechanisms to allow that to happen. Firstly, once the joint structures overseeing the policy programme has identified activities and associated costs, these will be taken into account as an existing commitment of the MDA in its budget process. Secondly, the PPBB programme segment in the chart of accounts, allows for expenditures against such programmes to be identified, which then allows reporting on the programmes once the information is extracted from all the MDAs involved.

78. In the Seychelles the framework adopted for the development of the Medium Term National Development Strategy, also uses the word programme to refer to such policy programmes. It is important to note that a strategic policy programme in the National Development Strategy is not necessarily equal to a budget programme in the Seychelles budget structure. The Seychelles National Development Strategy policy programmes may:

* (i) span two or more MDAs;
* (ii) fit within an MDA but span two or more programmes;
* (iii) fit within an MDA and within one programme.

This is because the Medium Term Development Strategy structure is determined by the medium-term priorities of the Seychelles from a sector perspective, whereas budget programmes are determined by the long-term functions of specific MDAs, mapped to their administrative structures. The coding of the programme structure however, allows for non-budget policy programmes to be tracked across the budget structure.

## Section 2: Revising programme structures

### A. Devising a new programme structure

84. Revising programme structures comprises three main steps, namely (i) checking whether the programme structure should and may be revised; (ii) reviewing the mandates / long-term objectives of the MDA, and (iii) developing a revised programme structure against these mandates, taking into account how the MDA operates.

#### Step 1: Checking on the need to revise the programme structure

85. MDAs will revise programme structures in two sets of circumstances: firstly when their mandate has changed. This can occur at any time during the budget cycle, although ideally mandate changes should occur prior to the start of the budget preparation in March each year. Secondly, MDAs may request to change their programme structures when after first using their existing structure, they find that their current structure is not conducive to strategic budget choices or coherent budget execution. These changes may only occur between January and March each year, before the budget process commences. In rare cases, when MDAs administrative structure changes, they may also consider revising their programme structures, but only because a revised programme-structure will represent a better fit while still fulfilling the objectives of budgeting by objectives/service delivery areas. Programme structures should not be revised to align with administrative structures without this condition being met.

86. MDAs may not decide on changing their programme structure without consulting the MoFTEP. The MoFTEP will review the reasons for a revision of the structure, and work with the MDA to develop a new structure in cases where the reason is valid.

#### Step 2: Review the mandates/long-term objectives of the MDA

87. The process to revise programme structures is similar to the process MDAs first used when they designed their current structure. A first step is to review the mandates of the MDA.

88. The mechanism to link MDAs’ programme structures to their objectives/strategic plan is through their long-term mandates/functions. These mandates – which should also drive strategic plans -- are outcome focussed and as such, are timeless. They will not normally be within the direct control of the MDA, but will reflect what it is that government seeks to influence in terms of the desired direction of socio-economic development in the country in the long-term through the services of the MDA. While the measures and targets associated with MDAs’ mandates will change over successive National Development Strategies, the mandates themselves will not change unless they are explicitly adjusted by the Government. Using these high-level long-term mandates to formulate budget programmes therefore ensures useful and stable units to which the budget can be allocated at the highest level.

## HEADLINE SUMMARY

### DEVELOPING AND MANAGING PPBB PERFORMANCE INFORMATION

#### Section 1: Defining Performance Information for PPBB
Performance information (PI) is the key informational building block of the PPBB, Results-Based Management and monitoring and evaluation systems. The two main objectives for monitoring PI are:

* To improve service delivery; and
* To increase public sector effectiveness and efficiency.

The box below provides key principles for performance information in PPBB.

> **Quality of Performance Information in PPBB**
> * **Comprehensive**: covers all expenditure and key policy initiatives for all units;
> * **Cascaded**: all levels of service delivery are measured;
> * **Limited**: only measures directly linked to the PPBB are tracked;
> * **Streamlined**: existing measures are used, and multiple measures are not collected for the same purpose;
> * **Appropriate**: the right things are measured; and
> * **Reliable**: verified data sources are used.

#### A. Role of Performance Information for PPBB purposes
PPBB performance information needs to:

* Measure progress towards achieving the MDA’s medium-term policy objectives using medium-term budget allocations;
* Inform Cabinet and the National Assembly for decision-making and scrutiny of the budget; and
* Inform the public of the current state of public services and its progression over time.

MDAs may collect additional performance information for results-based monitoring and evaluation purposes, or for internal management purposes.

**Difference between performance indicators in PPBB and in PME and internal management**

Performance information and indicators are also used as part of RBM monitoring and evaluation. The key differences between RBM performance monitoring and evaluation (PME) and PPBB, are that PME:

* May not cover all GoS public services or all MDAs; it focuses on MDAs and areas relevant to high-level goals;
* Often sets indicators that are cross-cutting and have wider policy purposes; and
* Sets indicators that may not be linked specifically to the MDA’s PPBB parameters for the medium term.

#### B. Defining outcomes, outputs and inputs and key relationships between them

* **Costs**: amounts required to purchase inputs;
* **Inputs**: resources that contribute to the production and delivery of outputs/services;
* **Activities**: Processes or actions that use a range of inputs to produce the desired outputs/services;
* **Outputs**: The final products, or goods and services produced for delivery;
* **Outcomes**: the medium-term results for specific beneficiaries that are the consequence of achieving specific outputs; and
* **Impacts**: the long-term results of achieving specific outcomes.

#### C. Types of Performance Indicators included in MTES and PPBB Statements
Each programme and sub-programme*, must set indicator(s) for their MTES and PPBB budget submissions to:

* Indicate whether the strategic priorities of the MDA are achieved (budget programme outcome indicators);
* Indicate the delivery of key outputs, associated with medium-term strategic priorities; and
* Indicate whether the MDA is efficient in achieving the strategic priority.

Programme 1 is not required to have indicators. However, if there is an important strategic priority funded in Programme 1, indicators may be set.

## Section 2: Steps in developing PPBB Performance Indicators

Performance indicators are developed at the MTES stage. Step 1 below is the first step of an MTES, namely determining the MDAs current service delivery challenges, and what that would mean for its strategic priorities or service delivery improvements over the budget period. If any of the MDAs programmes do not fund a MDA-wide strategic priority, the indicators for that programme should be set by following the process for the programme itself.

### Step 1: Discuss collectively the strategic performance improvements required by the MDA

* **Step 1a:** List the current service delivery challenges facing the MDA. This refers to the gaps or weaknesses in service delivery by the MDA not the internal challenges the MDA believes prevents it from delivering services well.
* **Step 1b:** Convert the current challenges into the associated improvements in service delivery the MDA would prioritise.
* **Step 1c:** Consider the chain of results needed so that the MDA can best reach the desired service delivery improvement. Work backward from the strategic priority. What does it need to change in its systems, its staff’s, clients’ / beneficiaries’ or stakeholders’ behaviour to get the improvement? What does that mean in terms of MDA activity and output changes? What inputs are needed to deliver that? (See Chapter 4 Summary and Guidelines for more information on Step 1a to c).

### Step 2: Select/Design performance measures that will measure whether the strategic priority is achieved, and whether the strategy to achieve it, is being implemented and implemented efficiently.

* **Step 2a:** Link each strategic priority to a programme and make it an outcome of the programme. Select/Set measures that will show whether the strategic priority is achieved.
* **Step 2b:** Design/select measures that will demonstrate whether the critical outputs for achieving the service delivery improvement (the strategic priority) are being delivered.
* **Step 2c:** Design/select measures to indicate whether the MDA is efficient in implementing the results chain.

Before designing new measures, always check whether there are any existing indicators that can be used.

### Step 3: Assess the list of draft indicators against good-quality criteria for performance indicators

Each proposed indicator should be checked against the following criteria:

### Section 1: Defining Performance Information for PPBB

#### A. Introduction

107. Performance information (PI) is the key informational building blocks of the Government of Seychelles’ PPBB, results-based management and monitoring and evaluation systems. PI is the means by which the Government will monitor improvements in the delivery of effective and efficient public services to the Seychellois. As such, MDAs are required to develop and manage a comprehensive set of appropriate, timely and reliable performance information for PPBB.

108. The government has two main objectives for wanting to monitor the performance of services provided by the public sector using public resources:

* Improve service delivery to the people of the Seychelles; and
* Increase public sector effectiveness and efficiency.

109. The box below provides the definition and the key principles for performance information in PPBB.

> **Box 8. Overview of Performance Information in PPBB**
>
> Performance information refers to performance measures that are relevant for budget management, monitoring or evaluation purposes, as required.
> 
> Performance measures are the indicators selected by the MDA or external institutions to monitor budget, policy and personal performance.
> 
> It is important that the set of measures collected and managed by an organisation are:
> * **Comprehensive**, in other words cover all expenditure, policy initiatives, administrative units, type of performance (e.g. effectiveness, efficiency, productivity etc) and organisational values; as well as measures required by other institutions;
> * **Cascaded**, in other words that at any level of monitoring (whether at the MoFTEP, Ministry/Agency, Programme or project level) only a limited number of measures are tracked to enable focus;
> * **Streamlined**, for example that organisations are not required to collect data for additional measures when the organisation itself already uses an existing measure that will fulfil the same purpose; or do not collect multiple measures for the same purpose;
> * **Appropriate**, in other words measures the right things and is the best measure possible (against common indicator values such as being specific, measurable and time-bound) given data availability;
> * **Reliable**, in other words are underpinned by reliable, verified and eventually audited data sources; and
> * **Used**, in other words play a material part in government decision-making processes.

#### B. Role of Performance Information for PPBB purposes

110. Performance information may be used for many different purposes. The Government’s objective for using performance information in PPBB is to show what it is achieving with its budget funds and to demonstrate to the National Assembly and thus the Seychellois people how effective is policy performance.

**Specific role of Performance Information in PI**

111. The specific role of performance information in PPBB includes:

## Section 2: Preparing an MTES

### A. Key features of an MTES
An MTES is used for the MDA to make strategic trade-offs within its ceiling. An MTES explains how a portfolio will use its available resources to achieve portfolio objectives over the medium term taking into account the ongoing cost of past policy decisions.

* A good MTES has a thread from challenges and strategic priorities to performance information targets;
* An MTES is NOT equal to costing through the PPBB tool;
* An MTES is about expenditure priorities

Priorities need to be stated at the correct level. Key is:
* Priorities should be specific (not read as a mandate, an input, an internal challenge, or an activity)
* Priorities should clearly relate to the budget structure
* There should be a limited number of priorities
* Priorities anchor a coherent MTES

#### From priorities to strategic budgeting decisions

* Priorities flow from a statement of the gaps / weaknesses in service delivery the MDA is facing (the Current Challenges of the MTES document and PPBB Statement). An internal challenge or lack of inputs (lack of resources, lack of qualified staff, lack of space, lack of transport) cannot be a MTES priority.
* A MTES priority is the positive statement of what would change in the MDAs service delivery, should the MDA address its most important current challenges. The priorities are determined by listing challenges in order of importance, and identifying the exact service delivery improvements associated with the top three or five. The MTES is a strategy precisely because it involves deciding the service delivery challenge to address first, and findings way to finance the associated interventions. Within limited resources MDAs cannot fix all gaps and weaknesses simultaneously.
* Current challenge and priorities must be specific, not just a restatement of the MDAs mandate. MDAs must be able to show progress on priorities over the budget period (three years).
* MDAs are advised to develop a result chain, or a theory of change, on how they will bring about the targeted improvement in service delivery (their strategic priority). A generic result chain is provided below.
* Setting out a result chain is to identify the interventions (the NSPs) the MDA must finance over the medium term to deliver on the targeted priorities. It will also determine how to measure whether the improvement is resulting from its interventions (the outcome indicators) in an efficient manner (the efficiency indicator), and whether the interventions are being implemented (the output indicators).
* It pays to be realistic about the number of priorities, and the interventions targeted to achieve the priorities. MDAs should use the medium term to spread the cost of an intervention, and seek savings in other activities and programmes to finance delivery. This involves assessing the spending space, prioritising between NSPs and findings savings in a reiterative process. Over the MTES and beyond, programme managers will need to need to look for increasingly cheaper ways of still delivering on the priorities.

### Section 1. Baseline projections: estimating the future cost of current services

147. A baseline is:

> The calculation of the forward cost of an MDA’s services/activities, if no changes are made, but taking into account (i) changes in the external environment the MDA does not control, such as price increases on the goods it buys and (ii) policy commitments already made but not included in the latest available outturns.

A baseline does not include any future policy changes, or changes in service volume, in activities or in inputs that the MDA controls, that have not yet been approved or confirmed.

148. MDAs’ baseline projections represent a central early step in the budget process. This is because a key constraint on the allocation of future budgets is the current commitments of the ministry, department or agency (MDA). The preparation of a baseline is necessary as a basis for budgeting for two reasons:

* **Top down:** baseline projections provide the MoFTEP with early information on the likely distribution of expenditure across government over the medium-term, crucial for determining the overall and MDA indicative MTES ceilings in the macro-fiscal framework.
* **Bottom-up:** baseline projections provide an MDA with the basis for a robust assessment of its spending space, and expenditure pressures.

## A. How to undertake baseline projections

149. MDAs project baselines using the PPBB Tool. A set of detailed instructions accompany this tool and is in the Compendium of Annexes to this Guideline, Annex 6. This section provides high level guidance and explanation of approaches and methods.

### Basis for projections
150. Projections are based on the best available outturn information, usually estimated expenditure for the previous year. The projections adjust this estimate in projecting the cost of the MDA if it operates as it did in the previous year, taking into account:

*   **i.** price increases for inputs;
*   **ii.** the cost of additional inputs needed, when demand change, e.g. when there is likely to be more claimants for social grants because tourism jobs are disappearing, as important tourism markets face an economic downturn;
*   **iii.** the cost of existing new commitments, e.g. if a new policy has been financed in the budget of the current year and the cost was not yet covered in the spending of the previous year.

### Methods to project
151. The baseline projections follow different methods for personnel, goods and services, and PSIP and MCO costs.

*   Because of the size of the personnel budget as a proportion of expenditure, projecting a baseline of staff costs for wages and salaries in cash is a detailed costing exercise, namely the manpower budget exercise (MBE). This starts off from the latest payroll and by programme, sub-programme, post category or even post, projects what additional recurring costs over this basis will be incurred in the current or next year, on account of existing commitments and decisions taken.
    *   **Very important:** Requests for new posts or new schemes of service are done through the new spending proposal (NSP) component of the MTES exercise.
*   For goods and services and wages and salaries in kind, MDAs use the latest full-year outturn information to project their baseline cost. This means that MDAs will use the latest available outturns for the 2015 budget year to project a baseline in 2016 for the 2017 to 2019 medium-term period. While use of outturn data provides a reliable base of how available resources were actually used to deliver services, it may not provide a full understanding of the cost of policy or operational decisions taken during the spending year (for example appointments made during the year for which a full year’s cost is not included in the outturn numbers), or any reflection of policy decisions already taken with unavoidable costs in the projection period. In costing out any one line, MDAs therefore have to take into account:
    *   The future cost of items for which a full year’s cost is included in the line;
    *   The cost of items that are only partially included, adjusted to reflect a full year’s cost;
    *   The cost of new policy/operational decisions that are already confirmed that are unavoidable and impact on the projection period. The tool uses a detailed programme budget, with the migrated outturn amounts against each programme and sub-programme from the migration process already entered.
*   For minor capital outlays: the projection comprises the expected actual forward cost of existing approved and funded projects and expenditure, as determined by an assessment of project progress and remaining forward financing needs. For some types of minor capital outlays, the allocation will behave more like a recurrent provision for purchasing of assets of the kind, e.g. computers, when these are purchased every year in larger agencies and ministries.

## Projecting different kinds of costs

153. In order to project each economic item, particularly in goods and services, MDAs will need to decide what drives cost for that item. In order to undertake this analysis, basic costing concepts are used. These are costing units and unit costs; fixed and variable costs, and once-off and recurrent costs. We explain each below.

### Costing units and unit costs
Costs are driven by costing units. In practice the projection of a programme baseline requires making judgements on:

* **The cost units that drive expenditure** in the economic classification items for that programme. For example, personnel cost is driven by the number of personnel; maintenance and repairs may be driven by the square meter of buildings to be maintained; and materials and supplies by the number of units of learner supply materials required; and
* **What the likely unit cost will be.** In most cases costs can be adjusted for changes in price by applying a standard inflation rate. However, in some cases a specific change in price that may be larger or smaller than inflation adjustment may need to be applied. For example, the personnel costs associated with providing planning and budget services would need to be adjusted by the specific increases agreed by the Government of Seychelles.

### Fixed, variable and step costs
Whether the baseline expenditure associated with an economic classification item will grow or not over the projection period, depends on whether the number of underlying costing units will increase or decrease despite no change in policy or service commitments, and changes in unit cost associated with price. To identify when the number of costing units are likely to change for baseline purposes, the following cost concepts are useful:

* **Variable costs:** Some costs change in relation to cost drivers. For example, in education expenditure on text books will rise when the number of learners in school rises if text book policies are not changed, and fall when the number of learners decreases. Variable costs are thus normally associated with a cost driver: in the example above the number of learners is the cost driver in the cost of providing text books.
* **Fixed costs:** Some costs will not grow in proportion to key programme variables, but remain fixed. For example, the cost of planning and budgeting for the education sector will remain the same no matter the number of learners.
* **Step costs:** Step costs are costs that do not change in a one on one relationship to the cost driver, but only change once a certain threshold is reached. Learner transport may be such an example: to transport thirty learners to a school is the same as the cost of transporting sixty when using school buses with a capacity of sixty learners. However, if the number of children in the school rises to eighty, the school bus would need to undertake two trips – thereby doubling some of the costs.

### Recurring costs and once-off costs
Different activities have different cost implications. The first distinction to make when calculating an indicative cost for any one activity is to determine whether an activity (i) carries a once-off cost for the sector, (ii) carries repeat costs for the sector, or (iii) carries a once-off cost of one kind or magnitude, as well as repeat costs of different kinds or magnitudes. ## Projection methods and the PPBB Tool

155. **PERSONNEL EXPENDITURE:** As noted above, all MDAs will be undertaking a manpower exercise to determine their projected baseline personnel expenditure. Instructions for the manpower exercise will be issued in the MTES circular, and the PPBB tool includes a sheet that allows for recording and describing factors by programme and sub-programme, calculating the expected cost of each factor. A separate sheet to project Gratuities are provided and requires MDAs to indicate by post the gratuities that are already contracted / approved.

156. **GOODS AND SERVICES:** Depending on the cost analysis, MDAs must select the projection method by which to project what the future cost by economic line item will be. Three projection methods are used for recurrent expenditure lines under goods and services:

* **Expected price increase only:** Increasing spending by expected price increases (or inflation). This method is suitable for economic classification items that represent mostly fixed costs within the programme. Inflation can be used, or a factor that is higher or lower than inflation. The MoFTEP will determine the factor to be used and communicate it in the MTES circular. For each MDA it will already be included in their PPBB tool. MDAs can elect to adjust this factor. As an alternative, for example, the economic item can be increased at the same rate as experienced in the past.
* **Variable cost:** Increasing spending by calculating the change in the number of cost units, and the expected price increases (or unit costs). This is suitable for expenditure that represents variable costs.
* **Lump sum costing:** In this method existing or once off commitments are added to the baseline, after they have been costed, or based on their previous costing. This method would be used for policy / operational commitments that are not yet (fully or partially) in the base actual outturn expenditure, or that are once-off costs that would not reflect across all years.

## Section 2. Preparing an MTES

162. AN MTES asks for strategic trade-offs within an MDA’s ceiling, thereby helping it to use its available resources more effectively and efficiently and improving its outcomes as a result. MDAs submit their MTESs to the MoFTEP for consideration in the MTES Review: it is the most important opportunity for MDAs to present their plans and budgets for consideration for funding.

### A. What is a Medium-term Expenditure Strategy (MTES)

163. A medium-term expenditure strategy sets out how a portfolio will use its available resources to achieve MDA objectives over the medium term, taking into account the ongoing cost of past policy decisions.

164. MTESs comprise the strategic level thinking within an overall MTES ceiling to

    i. identify service delivery / performance issues and determine how to finance addressing them;
    ii. identify expenditure issues (such as the lack of resources to carry out routine operational activities like inspections because funding is tied up in salaries and fixed overhead costs) and
    iii. seek ways of optimally using resources for service delivery / policy priorities within MDA.

165. It is about strategically moving resources from lesser priorities to higher priorities and reducing wastage within programmes, and within the MDA overall. The MTES is a key instrument for aligning MDA policies and expenditure to achieve objectives. An MTES requires a serious review of how MDAs do business.

166. The MTES is therefore also an important (and for the upcoming budget year the last) vehicle for proposing new PSIP projects, within the context of overall MDA priorities. From submission of the MTESs new proposed PSIP projects go for appraisal and approval by the MoFTEP and the Public Investment Committee respectively, in line with the Public Investment Programme Guidelines[^1]. 168. It is important not to equate an MTES with the costing done through the PPBB tool. The PPBB tool merely
(i) projects a baseline; (ii) does the basic calculation on spending space to guide the MDA in decision-making; and (iii) provides a space to record the financial implications of MDA decisions on the costs and activities they propose to fund in the coming year within ceiling; (iv) and generates standard budgeting tables within ceiling.

## B. Important concepts in the preparation of an MTES

172. The bullets above already introduced the key concepts of economy, efficiency and cost-effectiveness. The following further concepts are key to achieving these objectives through the preparation of an MTES.

### MDA available resources and indicative resource envelopes

173. A spending agency’s available resources are equal to its MDAN MTES ceiling together with other funds at its disposal, such as donor resources (in cash or in-kind commitments). What is included in the MTES should only be certain resources, not likely resources. In the case of donor resources this means resources for which contracts have already been signed.[^7]

174. An indicative MDA envelope/ceiling [^8] will be provided early in the MTES process to MDAs. It will be a global MTES ceiling, in other words a combined recurrent and capital ceiling. This is the only point in the annual budget process at which MDAs can consider their expenditure strategies in a global ceiling, proposing to the MoFTEP how funding might need to shift between capital and recurrent.

175. The envelope is indicative, in other words it will most likely change for the detailed budget process, and again in the final allocations. Eventually, the MOFTEP will issue ceilings that include possible growth for MDA spending. When this is instituted – once the central processes are able to produce these ceilings – the indicative ceiling will be likely to be the maximum likely forward resources for the MDA, in other words, any subsequent changes in allocations are likely to be downwards.

## Savings, efficiency gains and reprioritisation

178. Any baseline always contains expenditure that can be saved by cutting activities, reducing the level of activities or outputs, or delivering them more efficiently. These savings / efficiency gains are important, as they allow a baseline to be reprioritised over time towards more important expenditures. Savings or efficiency gains that are identified by the MDA can be used by the MDA to finance new expenditure proposals (reprioritisation of expenditure).

> **Box 16. Underspending is not savings**
> It is important to note that there is a conceptual difference between ‘savings’ as defined here, and ‘underspending’. This is an important distinction for MDAs to protect their budgets. Underspending is expenditures that were not made despite them being budgeted and potentially important to achieve policy objectives. Such ‘unused’ allocations are often re-allocated by the MoFTEP in the mid-year review to other MDAs that have developed pressing expenditure needs during the fiscal year, often with commensurate impact on the willingness of the MoFTEP in the subsequent year to allocate additional funding to an MDA that did not fully spend their budget. As the MoFTEP is responsible for optimal allocation of funding between MDAs, such choices by the MoFTEP can be justified: it is the responsibility of MDAs to optimally use their resources.

179. If no savings or efficiency gains are made, a ministry or agency’s budget will just grow over time, leaving only very marginal additional resources to pay for new priority expenditures.

180. As set out in the introduction to MTES, savings and efficiency gains can be realised as economies (lower cost of inputs); efficiency (more outputs per Rupee); or cost-effectiveness (better value for money through better choices on how MDAs operate).

## New spending proposals

181. New Spending Proposals (NSPs) are changes over the baseline of the MDA due to the introduction of new activities or changes in the level of activities. New Spending Proposals are generated by MDAs against their PPBB programmes and include:

* **New policy initiatives, activities and projects:** these may be investment projects, or new policy initiatives and recurrent activities, for example the introduction of an additional subject at secondary school level. A new spending initiative is likely to have both capital and recurrent costs. Investment projects have recurring cost implications; new recurring policy initiatives will most likely have capital cost implications.
* **Increases in current service levels and up-scaling of ongoing investment projects:** changes in units of output due to demand factors or past policy decisions, or increases in project cost due to price are captured in the baseline projection. Planned policy decisions that will result in increased services or increases in project cost are captured in the strategic plan as NSPs.

Increases on account of price or contract price increases or changes in demand would be part of the baseline, as they are the unavoidable consequence of the original policy decision when the spending was introduced.

## Allocation criteria
185. Allocation criteria are the decision-making rules by which the MDA will make decisions between competing claims on available resources. These may include relevance to MDA objectives, cost-effectiveness, capacity to implement new programmes on time, past spending performance of the associated units, etc.

>

### Box 18. Developing MDA-wide agreed strategic priorities, and strategies to achieve them

MTES and budget strategic priorities refer to the service delivery improvements that the MDA is selecting for achievement over the medium term. MTES / budget strategic priorities will not necessarily cover all of the service delivery areas of the MDA, as it is unlikely that the MDA will have the resources to address all shortfalls at the same time. Rather, MTES / budget strategic priorities are ‘strategic’ precisely because the MDA has determined that they are the most important areas – amongst all the service delivery problems it is facing -- to target for the next three years, given its available resources.

MTES strategic priorities are targeted on a rolling basis. In other words, from one MTES to a next the same priorities may be targeted if the MDA determines that these have not yet been fulfilled and it is unlikely to have budget space for adding priorities. Or, if one or more priorities will have been achieved by the start of the budget year, the MDA may introduce new priorities. It may also find that an emerging issue is so important to address, that it needs to put a priority of the previous year on hold, and address the emerging issue first.

Addressing the input / internal challenges the MDA faces, may not be presented as MTES / budget strategic priorities. In other words, for MTES / budget purposes “Appointing and retaining qualified and skilled staff” is not considered as an appropriate priority statement, for expenditure prioritisation. This is because many MDAs in government would list that as a priority. It is very difficult for the MOFTEP, Cabinet or the National Assembly to make decisions on which MDAs should be funded to address this internal challenge. They can only make that decision if they are clear on the service delivery gaps / shortfalls that it causes. For example, they are more able to prioritise between having quality new roads built (previously hampered by a shortage of engineers), and stopping tax avoidance (previously hampered by a lack of forensic investigators).

### Section 1: The central budget process in brief

216. In a medium-term PPBB system, the centre (MoFTEP and Cabinet) has responsibility for setting the overall expenditure ceiling (through a medium-term fiscal framework) and making trade-offs within available resources between MDAs (through a medium-term budget framework). 217. Each of these steps comprises an assessment of available resources (through a medium-term fiscal framework); as well as an assessment of expenditure priorities, needs and inefficiencies, to arrive at the distribution of the available resources between MDAs.

## STEP 5: SUMMARISING ASSESSMENT (for MTES/PPBB Submissions that pass Step 1)

**Overall assessment of strategy:** is it feasible? Is it the right strategy, given own and national priorities?

### Summarising questions
* **Summary of Step 2:** Are baseline projections credible? Is NSP and saving costing credible?
* **Summary of Step 3:** Is the MDA’s strategy believable? In other words, will it be able the implement the expenditure plans set out?
    * Will it deliver budget credibility? Are there areas it is deliberately underfunding that will require additional funding during the year?
    * Does it have the capacity implement the funding as requested? Are implementation timelines for savings/NSPs feasible?
* **Summary of Step 4:** Is it the most appropriate expenditure strategy given its own and national priorities?
    * Is the proposed spending economical? Does it acquire inputs at the least cost?
    * Do the right programmes/sub-programmes and items grow, given stated priorities?
    * Do they grow enough to deliver the targeted performance? Is the targeted performance adequate given growth and demand?
    * Is delivery modes and the associated expenditure choices effective and efficient?
* **Should additional funding be provided?** Should the Scenario B requests be considered? This assessment is likely to be only for MTES submissions, as the Budget Planning phase submissions (the draft PPBB Statements) are not expected to allow Scenario B proposals.

Analysts can use the following principles as a basis for making recommendations on the MDA’s Scenario B proposals:

#### Top-down considerations:
1. **The budget constraint:** the analyst’s recommendation needs to be guided by the overall constraint on aggregate expenditure. Updated macro-fiscal projections should be available by this time. How the updated projections are likely to impact on the overall expenditure ceilings and their distribution, must be communicated to analysts so that their recommendations on additional funding are realistic. Making sure that recommendations are constrained by the latest top-down fiscal and budget framework projections at this point would be a minimum necessary component of a top-down budget framework process.
2. **Alignment with national policy priorities:** A second important component of a top-down budget process is clear guidance on national expenditure priorities and distributional limitations on the budget. One kind of top-down distributional limitation on the budget is fiscal policy priorities/limitations, such as limiting personnel expenditure to an overall ceiling. However, and importantly, it should also include guidance on which sectors / policy areas are priority. This could be guided by the National Development Plan, or other strategic priorities such as Blue Economy investments. The analytical tasks that analysts undertake on MDAs submissions are much facilitated by such guidance, as it means that they are not working in a vacuum, but have a framework of national expenditure policy priorities against which they can benchmark their recommendations.

***

> There is as yet no explicit top-down process to set national medium term expenditure priorities prior to final budget allocations: the budget process would be much enhanced and facilitated if such explicit priorities are introduced much earlier in the process. It would form a key component of a top-down budget framework process. Medium term expenditure priorities are the sectors, subsectors and policy areas within sub-sectors that are singled out by Cabinet/the President for funding. They would be drawn from national strategic documents, and would represent the immediate priority interventions or issues for funding within a budget framework.

## Bottom-up considerations:

3) **Necessity of the proposal:** A first consideration is against the urgency and importance of the proposal. Can the intervention / expenditure be postponed; what would be the consequences of postponing it / not providing the funding?

4) **Relevance to MDA priorities:** The analyst would also be guided by the policy priorities that the MDA has set out in its MTES. She/he should examine the MDA’s own justification for shifting funding within ceiling, and prioritization of new spending proposals. If in agreement that the shifts are necessary and the proposals are indeed ranked in order of importance/urgency given the stated priorities, the recommendation should be that they are to be funded in the same order, provided that they are quality proposals. The remainder of the principles here are about determining the quality of proposals.

5) **Relevance to national priorities:** The analyst should be guided by the national priorities identified by Cabinet, and the NDP. A discussion with the PSIP office on public investment priorities is also advisable.

6) **Cost-effectiveness:** The efficiency/cost-effectiveness of the proposal must be considered. If the analyst agrees that the objective of the proposed intervention is urgent, the next question is whether the intervention represents the most cost-effective way to achieve that objective, and if so, whether the proposed use of funding is efficient and economical.

7) **Manageable risks:** The analyst needs to assess whether the implementation risks are manageable (particularly, implementation capacity, and relative dependence on other agencies besides the MDA).

8) **Sustainability:** The analyst needs to assess to what degree the shifts in economic distribution of expenditure, and proposed NSP activity/service are sustainable in budget terms, but also in institutional and environmental terms.

9) **Credibility of planning:** The analyst needs to assess to what degree the budget, including NSP plans, is credible and the costing realistic.

263. The discussion above in Chapter 4 and earlier parts of this Chapter makes reference at several points to the PSIP process, and how PSIP proposals are treated differently in the budget process. 264. The PSIP process is part of PPBB. Only PSIP projects that are approved for funding in the PPBB process will go ahead. Only PSIP projects that are approved by the Public Investment Committee can be approved for funding.

265. Note that not all capital expenditure is PSIP-related. The Seychelles budget process also includes a budget category of expenditure called **minor capital outlays (MCOs)**. This category comprises expenditure on ongoing capital items that are a normal part of the operational cost of running government offices and programmes, such as computers, printers, furniture, air-condition equipment, cars, etc. In the COA MCOs are still classified as capital expenditure (mostly expenditure on machinery and equipment), but the process followed to budget for these outlays is similar to the process for goods and services.

266. PSIP expenditure refers to major capital expenditure projects that are seen as public investment projects, and which require to be assessed and approved through the PSIP process before they can be approved for funding within the budget process.

267. The Public Investment Policy makes a distinction between two categories of PSIP proposals:

## HEADLINE SUMMARY

### EXPENDITURE ANALYSIS FOR PPBB
Budget analysis helps Government to understand how expenditure meets policy objectives. There are a number of questions that can be addressed using budget analysis, such as:

1.  **Prioritisation:** How does the budget compare to resources spent in other areas?
    **Adequacy:** How much is being allocated to an issue or a type of input? Is this sufficient?
2.  **Improving allocations over time:** What progress are being made to finance an issue or resolve an expenditure issue?
    **Effectiveness, efficiency and economy:** Are outcomes being achieved in a cost-effective way? How efficiently are outputs being delivered?
3.  **Equity:** Are resources being allocated fairly across different population groups, genders, areas etc?

The following techniques can be used:

#### Financial analysis techniques
*   Comparing planned and realised expenditure reviews how well the budget system is working.
*   Calculating budget shares checks priorities through budget analysis.
*   Checking for priority and progress over time identifies trends in budget allocations.
*   Checking for real value of expenditure converts allocations from nominal to real amounts.

#### Using unit costs: investigating the quality of spending, adequacy and equity
Working out how unit costs change over time is a highly useful exercise when assessing budgets.
*   **Economy, efficiency and cost-effectiveness:** Unit costs are key calculations to determine economy and efficiency. This is best done through comparative analysis or against unit costs for the same service or unit of government over time.
*   **Adequacy of budgets:** Unit costs can be used to assess the adequacy of budgets. While there are very few standards worldwide on what unit costs should be, usually analysts can identify a band of expenditure that can be considered adequate.
*   **Fairness of expenditure (equity):** Unit costs for beneficiaries or regions could also be used to assess the fairness of expenditure, or expenditure equity.

#### Economic analysis
Public economics answers two types of questions:
1. How does government expenditure (and revenue and debt) affect the economy?
2. How can government expenditure be allocated, and revenue be raised to maximise welfare?

The following types of economic analysis can be done (for example):
*   **Cost analysis** tells you whether you can afford an expenditure choice.
*   **Cost-benefit analysis** tells you whether you should undertake an intervention, and what the relative net benefit of different interventions are.
*   **Least cost or cost-effectiveness analysis** tells you how to achieve an outcome in the cheapest way / allows us to assess whether the delivery choices of government are cost-effective.
*   **Benefit-incidence analysis** tells you whether government expenditure choices are equitable, or correct, if compared with government policy priorities on who should benefit from expenditure programmes.
*   **Analysis of risk and feasibility** will assess financial, service delivery, policy and political risk. It is important that the unintended consequences are anticipated and included in the calculus for a course of action.

#### A. Why undertake budget analysis

282. Budget analysis helps Government to understand how expenditure meets policy objectives. There are a number of questions that can be addressed using budget analysis. These are:

*   **iv. Questions about what is prioritised:** How does the budget for this purpose compare to resources spent in other areas? Are the ‘real priorities’ that are expressed through what is prioritised in terms of spending in line with government/Portfolio/MDA stated policy priorities?
*   **v. Questions about whether the budget is adequate:** How much is being allocated to an issue or a type of input? Is this sufficient to ensure that objectives are reached? *   **vi. Questions about progress towards improved allocations over time:** What progress are being made to finance an issue, resolve an expenditure issue? What progress has been made in financing a priority?

For example, if a ministry’s personnel expenditure share is too high in a sector that should have high complementary inputs, the distribution of expenditure would mean that people are employed, but sit in their offices as there is no money to implement activities.

283. The budget can be analysed in terms of growth or distribution of the budget, or a combination of these two. **Comparing planned and realised expenditure**

284. This basic budget analysis technique is well known and widely used in the Seychelles. It provides information on how well the budget system is working. ## Calculating budget shares: a check on priorities through budget analysis

285. A budget allocates resources to different categories of expenditure (sectors, programmes, administrative units, economic input types), and different beneficiaries (between regions, between different genders, between different income groups). Because of inflation, the real or purchasing power value of a given amount of money declines. The terms nominal and real expenditure are used to distinguish between mere money value and purchasing power value of budget allocations.

This can be straightforward (ie average pay per teacher in region one compared to average pay per teacher in region two), or more complex, looking for example at factors that drive different costs per units, such as educator learner ratios in education.

## C. Economic analysis

297. It is not possible to cover economic analysis in depth in this guideline, but the basic concepts are introduced. The intent is to stimulate thinking for future years of MTES preparation, when more in-depth analysis of specific programmes will be required from MDAs and Portfolios.

298. Economics is often described as the science of scarcity, the condition in which wants and needs inevitably exceeds resources. It is the study of how individuals and societies make decisions about ways to use scarce resources to fulfil wants and needs.

299. For individuals this means choosing between different items within their budgets (remind the class of the household budget exercise); for business it is about choosing between hiring people or expanding their equipment investment as both would be unaffordable; for governments it is about choosing how much to spend on education compared to all other sectors, given limited resources.

300. Public economics is about the choices governments make, to deliver public goods and services given that resources are always limited. Economic analysis is therefore crucial to making budgets work better for the growth and development of a country.

301. Economic analysis of public expenditure is a sub-branch of public economics overall. Public economics focuses on answering two types of questions:

* **3)** How do government policies affect the economy? In budget terms the question is how do government expenditure (and revenue and debt) affect the economy?
* **4)** How should government policies be designed to maximise welfare? In budget terms the question is how can government expenditure be allocated and revenue be raised to maximise welfare?

### Types of economic analysis

#### Cost analysis
302. Cost analysis will tell you whether you can afford an expenditure choice. It is the first step of a full economic analysis. It estimates the total programme costs and determines who incurs those costs. A full cost analysis could include both financial and economic costs, where financial costs are the costs that will be incurred by the budget, and economic costs, which include the opportunity cost (in other words the benefits precluded by taking a course of action).

303. In the MTES, particularly the early phases, the focus is however on financial costs. An extended type of cost analysis is **programme cost analysis**, which not only looks at the costs incurred by the intervention budget itself, but also at the costs incurred for example by participants or intended beneficiaries in the intervention.

## Cost-benefit analysis

304. Cost-benefit analyses will tell you whether you should undertake an intervention, and what the relative net benefit of different interventions are. It compares the costs and benefits of public sector activities to evaluate whether a project should be implemented, or to compare the costs and benefits of two or more policy options. It requires

* That all costs and benefits should be expressed in the same terms, namely a monetary value to provide a common unit of value by which to compare costs and benefits.
* That all benefits for all users of a service should be considered and quantified
* That all costs should be considered, including the cost of negative consequences of the intervention
* It measures the costs and benefits over a specific period of time, discounting them to a present value

305. If the benefit of the intervention outweighs the cost, it is in principle a feasible intervention to undertake. If the cost benefit ratio of one option is higher than the other, it would be the better option to pursue. For example, a cost-benefit analysis in education might ask whether investment in primary education would be more favourable than investment in secondary education.

## Least cost or cost-effectiveness analysis

306. Least-cost or cost-effectiveness analysis tells us how to achieve an outcome in the cheapest way / allows us to assess whether the delivery choices of government is cost-effective. It looks at different ways of delivering the same outcomes, and compares their costs. Once a Portfolio/MDA has successfully identified, quantified and valued the social cost and benefits associated with different alternatives, the least cost alternative for delivering on the project or the most cost-effective way for achieving project objectives can be determined. When undertaking least cost analysis it is important to ensure that the alternative ways will result in the same output at the same quality. If there are differences, the analyst should adjust the assessment of benefits between the options to ensure that the cost benefit analyses are comparable.

307. A least cost project is identified by comparing the capital and operating costs of the different ways of delivering on project objectives and (i) assessing which alternative presents the lowest present value of costs or (ii) calculating an equalising discount rate for the difference in costs. A third option to assess which alternative is the cheapest is to estimate the average incremental economic cost for each additional output for each alternative[^1]. This will allow the identification of the lowest per unit cost.

308. Least cost analysis is important for identifying efficiency gains within existing MDA budgets.

## Benefit-incidence analysis

309. Benefit-incidence analysis will tell us whether government expenditure choices are equitable, or correct, if compared with government policy priorities on who should benefit from expenditure programmes. These analyses are carried out to examine existing spending programmes, comparing the distribution of benefits from public spending to the distribution of income to determine whether the overall impact is progressive. These studies combine household data on consumption of public goods with information on public expenditures. A unit subsidy per person is calculated, and household usage of the service is then aggregated across key social groups to estimate the pattern and distribution of service provision. They are particularly useful to determine the fairness of expenditure.

310. In summary, economic analysis allows policy and budget allocation decisions to be made based on evidence.

## D. Analysis of risk and feasibility

311. Budget analysis and economic analysis provide evidence-based answers to or guidance on important public expenditure questions, such as whether to make a proposed expenditure choice and what expenditure choice to make. But, it does not tell us whether government will be able to make that choice / undertake that intervention.

312. A final set of analysis that need to be taken into account is the analysis of risk to the budget, and the analysis of the feasibility of the selected options.

313. In terms of risk, public decision-makers need to consider the severity of the risks identified, as well as the likelihood of risks being realised. An analysis of risk will include an assessment of financial, service delivery, policy and political risk. It is important that the unintended consequences are anticipated and included in the calculus for a course of action.

314. For feasibility several dimensions can be looked at, including whether an intended course of action is technically feasible: is the capacity to implement it available to the agency, or can it obtain the capacity; what cooperation from other state institutions or private institutions is required and what progress has been made in agreeing this cooperation; can the spending institution absorb the allocations proposed. It is also important to consider the political feasibility of the course of action: do relevant stakeholders view the policy as acceptable.

The restrictions on virements within programmes between main categories of operational expenditure, e.g. from goods and services to wages and salaries, as well as between sub-categories of expenditure, e.g. from office expenses to materials and supplies may be relaxed on a case by case basis, if the MoFTEP is assured that an MDA’s budget can be managed ex post, by performance.

A final limitation is that specific allocations to new expenditure projects will not be allowed to be vired from these allocations to other components of their expenditure programmes, without first getting permission from the MoFTEP.

## Supplementary budgets

336. Given that Seychelles is a small island state with an open economy and exposed to unpredictable weather patterns, it is unlikely that the approved Budget would have allowed for all uncertainties. The use of virements is intended to allow limited flexibility to the executive and MDAs to implement their budgets and absorb the effects of unforeseen events within their budget limits. When budget increases or changes are required that goes beyond virement, this requires a supplementary budget.

337. As is the practice currently, the supplementary Budget will be submitted to the NA after the mid-year review, capturing all foreseen changes by the Executive at that point. This will in principle finally set the budget limits within which the executive would need to operate.

338. In a PPBB system it can be expected that the share of overall resources subject to change in a supplementary budget should reduce, given the high premium it places on reliable planning. Similarly, the MoFTEP will be far stricter in applying the concepts of ‘unforeseen and unavoidable’ before proposing additional funding or changes on behalf of MDAs to the NA, given the opportunity given to MDAs in the budget preparation process to plan their own resources in line with their objectives and ongoing expenditure obligations.

339. Nonetheless, in the PPBB System, the Supplementary Budget will remain key to allocate resources or make intended resource use transparent in the following cases:

* Where circumstances have forced the GoS to use the Contingency Fund (see box below on the treatment of the Contingency Fund in accounting for its use in the PPBB system);
* When additional donor contracts are concluded during the fiscal year, and the first cash tranche is disbursed and used in the fiscal year;
* Where more revenue is likely to be collected than expected, to allocate the additional resources (to MDAs or to communicate the intent to reduce the deficit), or when less revenue will be collected, to indicate where the cuts will be; and
* Where shifts in budget allocations are required for unforeseen and unavoidable expenditure.

> **Box 3. Treatment of the Contingency Fund**
> 
> The Contingency Fund is set up in the Constitution as a real budget allocation: in other words, resources need to be set aside every year to meet unforeseen needs as specified by the Constitution. To date, the fund has been allocated in the Appropriation Act, and expenditure against it raised using the MoFTEP expenditure codes. This however means that the expenditure does not reflect against the programmes of the actual MDA utilising the resources, which is not in line with PPBB principle of reflecting the full cost of programmes.

353. The programme segment occupies digits 15 to 21 of the Chart of Accounts. It has been set up to be unique, in other words, there will be no duplication of numbers within the segment across the chart of accounts. This means that it can be used as the primary key in the accounting system.

## Section 2: PPBB Reporting

361. In a PPBB system expenditure control shifts from ex ante internal control to ex post external control. This is to allow managers more discretion to manage. In turn this is dependent on a regular and comprehensive reporting system with MDAs reporting the financial and non-financial results of their management decisions so that they are accountable for the results.

362. All MDAs will have their unique programme segment and programme-based financial reports loaded onto their TIS, so that programme expenditure in line with programme budgets can be monitored by Programme Managers (or sub-programme managers) and the MDA PPBB WG. This means that programmes will be enforced by ‘ex post’ rather than ‘ex ante’ controls (see box below for an explanation of ex post and ex ante).

> **Box 6. Ex ante and ex post controls**
> 
> Control in public financial management systems can be by two main means, system-based controls that operate before a commitment is made or a payment voucher raised, or report-based controls that operate as an accountability mechanism after a commitment has been made or payment voucher raised. Convention refers to the first set of internal controls as ‘ex ante’ controls, and second set ‘ex post’ controls. Note that ‘ex post’ controls are also external to the executive, while ‘ex ante’ controls will almost always be internal.
> 
> A common feature of PPBB systems is that they move from ex ante to ex post controls. In Seychelles this shift will only happen gradually.

363. This external reporting is a continuation of internal reporting, as MDAs are required to cascade the tracking of the implementation of budgets and achievement of performance targets in the organisation, to ensure timely, regular and effective use of resources.

### F. Key distinctions in the reporting cycle:

364. Reporting should be internal and external. Internal reporting occurs at three levels, namely within the MDA, from the MDA to the finance ministry, and from managers (or the civil service) to political principals. External reporting is at two levels, from the executive to the legislature, and from government to citizens.

365. Reporting occurs both in-year and after the end of the financial year. Internal and external in-year reporting is critical while the budget is being implemented, so that corrections can be made in time. Reporting after the end of the financial year is also crucial, to ensure accountability after the fact.

366. When the Auditor General has audited the reported financial and non-financial information and can provide assurances to the recipients of reports that the information provided in them is reliable, these are then known as audited outturns.

367. Reporting therefore has two functions: firstly to enable the ex post report-based controls, and secondly, to enable transparency. These two are mutually reinforcing in ensuring the public servants and politicians can be called to account for their decisions in implementing their plans and budgets. ## I. Year-end Financial Statements and the PFMA Section 31 Report

385. At the end of the year government releases its Financial Statements. These are done according to Seychelles accounting standards, reporting on the consolidated financial transactions and balances of government and all its entities. The consolidated financial statements report on all government MDAs. In addition, Category 2 agencies – those agencies that undertake a regulatory function – provide financial statements directly to the National Assembly.

386. The PFMA however, requires all public entities to use international public-sector accounting standards (IPSAS), which means that government will shift to these accounting standards.

387. Appropriation accounts differ from financial statements insofar as they provide financial information not against standardised accounting categories, but against budget classifications. In IPSAS appropriation accounts are required as part of the financial statements. In the PPBB system audited appropriation accounts would be provided in the consolidated financial statement report by programme and economic sub-items.

388. Appropriation accounts will also be provided by the PFMA Section 31 Annual Performance Reports. These reports provide information on financial outturns for the previous year, as well as audited outturns for the year before, against the budget classifications. They also provide information on the achievement of performance targets, and the objectives of MDAs.

## Section 3: Audit and oversight

393. **Internal audit:** Reviewing PPBB processes and performance information will form part of the scope of internal audit from the 2020 financial years onwards.

394. **External Audit:** The Auditor General of the Seychelles shall start auditing performance information (AoPI) for the period ending on December 31, 2019. The audits will be conducted on a phased approach over a 5-year period and will involve audits of Section 31 Reports programme performance measures information (Table 23 above). The Auditor General will conduct audits of a number of MDAs per year selected based on risk basis, using criteria such as the size of the MDAs budget, and the strength of PI systems as assessed in the regulatory audit.

395. The phased approach will enable

* the Auditor General to develop appropriate audit protocols for the additional mandate, and design approach (determine the standards to use, audit criteria and tools), capacity and systems to conduct such audits;
* the MoFTEP and DPA to develop a framework for managing programme performance information and assist the MDAs to develop and implement policies, systems and procedures to prepare, collect, monitor and report on performance information (simply to apply the framework).

396. The audit of performance information shall involve

i. assessing the internal policies, controls and systems to collect, monitor and report performance information;
ii. reviewing the existence, consistency, format and quality of PI;
iii. assessing the quality of performance information and its accuracy; and
iv. a management letter that records areas that require improvement.

397. After the initial 5 years, the Auditor General shall conduct AoPI of the MoFTEP consolidated performance measures/target report and issue an audit opinion. The consolidated performance measures/targets report shall form part of the annual financial statements.

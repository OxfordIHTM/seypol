# Seychelles National Plan for People With Dementia

## The Current and Foreseeable Future Impact of Dementia in Seychelles

Dementia is a general term used to describe a group of conditions characterized by the loss of memory, language, and ability, among other things. The symptoms of dementia can vary in depth, include changes in language, problem-solving, or the ability to recognize or identify familiar people, place, or objects.

Alzheimer's disease is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60-80% of cases, and vascular dementia is the next most common at around 17% of cases.

The Alzheimer's Association identifies ten early signs and symptoms of dementia:
1. Memory that disrupts daily life;
2. Challenges in planning or solving problems;
3. Difficulty completing familiar tasks at home, at work, or at leisure;
4. Confusion with time or place;
5. Trouble understanding visual images and spatial relationships;
6. New problems with words in speaking or writing;
7. Misplacing things and losing the ability to retrace steps;
8. Decreased or poor judgment;
9. Withdrawal from social activities and activities;
10. Changes in mood and personality.

### Risk Factors for Dementia

The impact of Alzheimer's disease is already being felt across Seychelles - on individuals and families, as well as the broader emotional and financial responsibility, on businesses through lost productivity from family caregivers and state-funded resources, and on local communities, including the healthcare sector, which incurs significant costs for care and services. And, the greatest challenges are yet to come.

While Alzheimer's disease is not a normal part of aging, the age is the greatest risk factor for the development of the disease. The prevalence of Alzheimer's disease is expected to increase exponentially after age 65. With our aging population, the prevalence of Alzheimer's disease is predicted to increase in coming years.

First-degree relatives (sons and daughters) and lack of (physical activity) and other co-existing medical conditions (diabetes, hypertension, depression and obesity) are other risk factors which increases chances of developing Alzheimer's disease. A family history of Alzheimer's and genetic influences associated with this also increase risk.

## Impact on Individuals

Studies suggest that people guess 45 or over, live on average four to eight years after a diagnosis
of Alzheimer's disease, but some survive as long as 20 years. The progressive nature of
dementia, its impact on cognitive function, and the associated behavioral changes create
specific challenges for individuals.

In the early stages, declining memory may lead to challenges in managing finances, work,
and other daily activities, as well as relationships. As the disease progresses, people with
dementia may also experience difficulty in initiating activities, decision making and
behavioral changes, which may lead to social isolation.

Over time, they depend more and more on family or others, to help them with daily living.
More than 60% of people with dementia may wander away from home at some point in the
disease. And whereas the needs of people with dementia vary, some of the most common
reasons for hospitalization of people with Alzheimer's. Eventually, people with
dementia need total help with feeding, dressing, bathing and toileting.

#### Impact on Families and Caregivers

Dementia affects the whole family. Family members provide the vast majority of care for
those living with dementia. Family caregivers caring for a person with dementia in America,
provide an average of 21.5 hours per week of care.

While most family members provide such caring willingly and experience benefits through this
role, for many the experience is stressful and subject to negative outcomes – stress, health risks,
depression, and emotional strain. The greatest impacts on dementia caregivers are increased
stress, depression, anxiety, fatigue, insomnia, and care-related burnout, and poor health outcomes.
Findings also indicate that caregivers of people with dementia are twice as likely, as compared
to non-caregivers, to report poor health. They are also more likely to have a chronic health
condition such as depression and/or anxiety. They are also more likely than non-caregiving
peers to report their health as fair or poor.

These emotional and physical impacts lead to increased health care costs for dementia
caregivers nationwide.

## Impact on Health Care Systems

As the number of people with Alzheimer's and other dementias increases, so will the
pressure in health care systems, which will lead to an increase in potentially avoidable
emergency admissions or inappropriately hospitalised patients.

People with Alzheimer's and other dementias are more likely to have other (chronic)
conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, leading to more frequent visits to clinics
and hospitals.

## Impact on Government, Other Parties and Society

Dementia, including Alzheimer's disease is one of the costliest diseases to society. It affects
a financially dependent group of people (under 65s and over 80s). The largest component of costs
attributable to dementia is the cost for institutional and home-based long-term supports and
services.

Dementia also puts employment impacts and expenses, as more family caregivers need to miss
work days, reduce hours work or leave their jobs completely.

# Ministry of Health (MOH)

The Ministry of Health has a key role to play in developing a National policy on healthy
aging, which promotes equitable access to quality care, including for dementias, in
partnership with relevant legal authorities.

Additional training and education of the elderly needs to be integrated into Continuous Professional
Development (CPD) opportunities for the various categories of health professionals.

Relevant modules in training curricula of courses offered in the National Institute of Health
and Social Services (NIHSS) needs to be reviewed by experts in the field and updated to
reflect current knowledge and best practices in dementia care. This will enable health
professionals with services offered locally and mechanisms for queries and referrals.

A national campaign to educate the general public, as well as leaders and politicians, will need
to be developed to raise awareness of dementia and related problems affecting the elderly
and carers, and to promote services to meet their needs.

## Health Care Agency (HCA)

The Health Care Agency, with its wide array of specialized staff, should take the
responsibility of a national focal point for care of patients suffering from dementias. The
document outlines the need for a strong link between social services, health services,
home-care support and long-term inpatient care options available to patients, referrals and
mechanism for support.

Whilst this is being prepared, the relevant departments within the wider health system will
need to be reinforced in terms of funding, staffing and equipment, to build the additional
capacity to care for patients with dementia. While this will require a significant investment,
this imposes additional costs at the early stages, it will likely benefit the patient and the
caregivers by providing a better quality of life and additional support, and in the long run the
health system, by avoiding unnecessary admissions and reducing the number of emergency
visits by elderly.

directives from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Global Action Plan on Dementia. The

involved in analyses to improve dementia-care. The proposed steps for developing an

## The Importance of Integrating Alzheimer's/ Dementia Care into the National
Health System

Proposed state level health care reform should be focused on changes in the health system
that include a strengthened patient-centered holistic home medical model concentrating on the
social and health care management.

As chronic health care management evolves in Seychelles, it is critical that emerging data
and best practices around the identification, treatment and management of dementia
discovered in the world are integrated into the health system. This requires transformations
effects at the state and local levels, improvements must include the capacity to:

* Detect early signs and symptoms of cognitive impairment;
* Make and disclose a timely diagnosis;
* Provide care planning and support for advance decision-making and end-of-life care;
* Coordinate care for wellness, medical and support services needs;
* Provide education and training to healthcare providers and caregivers;
* Promote independent and active engagement of persons with dementia in the
community.

### Dementia Care Companion

Ensuring that state and local service systems are dementia-capable and friendly is critical.
Many who seek assistance from these systems have dementia or may have cognitive
dysfunction. Family caregivers who assist these individuals in navigating these service systems
regularly contact these systems seeking assistance in coping with their loved ones' special needs.
These systems need to be more responsive on operations and services for help with daily activities
short-term respite, and long-term care. The state and local service systems should be designed to meet
the types of supports with people that have dementia and their caregivers use assistance with
dialogue, orientation, and coordination of services. The system should be inclusive of support
service options, and home and community service providers who understand dementia.

The key aspects of building a dementia-capable care system are:

1. Educate the public about the brain. This would include information about the risk
factors influencing dementia, the possibility of prevention, and the importance of the
management of symptoms if individuals have dementia, support programs, and
opportunities for family caregivers.
2. Identify people with possible dementia and recommend that they see a physician for a
family history, evaluation and tests to rule out reversible causes of dementia or
conditions that resemble it.

4. Ensure that program eligibility and resource allocation take into account the impact
of cognitive disabilities.
5. Enable providers to communicate effectively with people with dementia and their
caregivers and provide services that: (a) Are person- and family-centered; (b) Offer
self-direction of services; and (c) Are culturally appropriate.
6. Educate health care providers to recognize dementia and recognize the symptoms of
dementia and appropriate services.
7. Implement quality assurance mechanisms that measure how effectively providers serve
people with dementia and their caregivers.
8. Encourage the development of dementia friendly communities, which include key parts
of dementia-capability.

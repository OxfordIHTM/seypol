## Foreword

The quality of housing has major implications for people’s health. Housing in cities is of particular concern, with the world’s urban population predicted to double by 2050 and, with it, the demand for housing. In both developed and developing countries, improving housing conditions and reducing health risks in the home is thus critically important.

Improved housing conditions can save lives, reduce disease, increase quality of life, reduce poverty, help mitigate climate change and contribute to the achievement of a number of Sustainable Development Goals, including those addressing health (**SDG 3**) and sustainable cities (**SDG 11**).

**Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus**
Director-General
World Health Organization

## Executive Summary

Improved housing conditions can save lives, prevent disease, and increase quality of life. The **WHO Housing and health guidelines (HHGL)** provide practical recommendations to reduce the health burden due to unsafe and substandard housing conditions.

### Table 1: Recommendations of the WHO Housing and health guidelines

| Topic | Recommendation | Strength of recommendation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Crowding** | Strategies should be developed and implemented to prevent and reduce household crowding. | Strong |
| **Indoor cold and insulation** | Indoor housing temperatures should be high enough to protect residents from the harmful health effects of cold. For countries with temperate or colder climates, 18 °C has been proposed as a safe indoor temperature. | Strong |
| **Indoor cold and insulation** | In climate zones with a cold season, efficient and safe thermal insulation should be installed in new housing and retrofitted in existing housing. | Conditional |
| **Indoor heat** | In populations exposed to high ambient temperatures, strategies to protect populations from excess indoor heat should be developed and implemented. | Conditional |
| **Home safety and injuries** | Housing should be equipped with safety devices (such as smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, stair gates and window guards) and measures should be taken to reduce hazards. | Strong |
| **Accessibility** | Based on current and projected national prevalence of populations with functional impairments, an adequate proportion of the housing stock should be accessible. | Strong |

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Housing and health

#### 1.1.1 WHO approach to healthy housing
Healthy housing is shelter that supports a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being. It provides a feeling of home, including a sense of belonging, security and privacy.

#### 1.1.2 Key health risks related to housing
- **Structural deficiencies:** Increase likelihood of slips or falls.
- **Poor accessibility:** Puts disabled and elderly people at risk of injury and isolation.
- **Thermal issues:** Difficulty heating homes contributes to poor respiratory/cardiovascular outcomes; high temperatures cause heat-related illnesses.
- **Air quality:** Indoor pollution harms respiratory health (e.g., asthma).
- **Crowding:** Increases risk of infectious diseases.

## 3. Household Crowding

### 3.1 Guideline Recommendation
**Recommendation:** Strategies should be developed and implemented to prevent and reduce household crowding. (**Strength: Strong**)

### Table 3.1: Measures of Crowding

| Standard | Definition of Overcrowding |
| :--- | :--- |
| **UN-Habitat** | More than three people per habitable room. |
| **American Crowding Index** | More than one person per room; severe if > 1.5 persons per room. |
| **Canadian National Occupancy Standard** | Extra bedrooms required to ensure specific pairings (couples, lone parents, etc.) have their own room. |
| **Eurostat** | Insufficient number of rooms relative to household composition (couples, singles over 18, etc.). |

---

## 4. Low Indoor Temperatures and Insulation

### 4.1 Guideline Recommendations
1. **Indoor housing temperatures** should be high enough to protect residents from cold. In temperate/colder climates, **18 °C** is proposed as a safe balance. (**Strong**)
2. **Thermal insulation** should be installed in new housing and retrofitted in old housing in climate zones with a cold season. (**Conditional**)

---

## 5. High Indoor Temperatures

### 5.1 Guideline Recommendation
**Recommendation:** In populations exposed to high ambient temperatures, strategies to protect populations from excess indoor heat should be developed and implemented. (**Strength: Conditional**)

### Table 5.1: Estimated Temperature Thresholds

| City/Country | Indoor minimal risk temperature | Indoor maximum acceptable temperature |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Boston (USA) | 21–22 °C | 25 °C |
| New York (USA) | 22–24 °C | 27–28 °C |
| London/Manchester (UK) | 22–23 °C | ~25 °C |
| Thailand | ~30 °C | ~32 °C |

## 6. Injury Hazards

### 6.1 Guideline Recommendation
**Recommendation:** Housing should be equipped with safety devices (such as smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, stair gates and window guards) and measures should be taken to reduce hazards that lead to unintentional injuries. (**Strength: Strong**)

---

## 7. Housing Accessibility

### 7.1 Guideline Recommendation
**Recommendation:** Based on current and projected national prevalence of populations with functional impairments and taking into account trends of ageing, an adequate proportion of the housing stock should be accessible to people with functional impairments. (**Strength: Strong**)

## 9. Implementation

Implementation requires coordination between national, regional, and local governments. The **Health in All Policies** approach is emphasized to ensure that health objectives are integrated into housing and urban planning policies.
